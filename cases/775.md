# The Nudge Lab: Six Briefs Before the Committee

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-775 · **Companion simulation:** Express 775 — *Choice Architect: Nudge, Bias and the Irrational Consumer* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, January 12, 2026, at 08:40, Rosalind Mehta opened the Nudge Lab's work plan for the first half of the year and counted six briefs. She had been the Lab's lead choice architect for eleven days. The first brief, from a regional parcel carrier that wanted more of its staff saving into a pension, was due back with a design by Friday. The last was not a design at all: a Parliamentary select committee had asked the Lab to appear in June and answer one question, whether nudging was manipulation.

Each brief asked her for two things in writing: which bias she judged was driving the behaviour, and which intervention the Lab would deploy. Every design would be measured once it had run, logged, and carried forward to the committee as evidence, for the Lab or against it.

## The Protagonist

Mehta was thirty-six. She had trained as an economist, spent six years building household-finance models for a government department, and joined the Lab because she had grown tired of models in which people always read the small print. She had run trials before. She had never signed off a design that would reach millions of households, and she had never been questioned in public about one.

## The Decision

Six times between January and June, Mehta had to name the bias and choose the design. The briefs arrived in a fixed order, each with a target set by the body that had commissioned it (see Exhibit 2). Nothing already deployed could be withdrawn. A design, once live, stayed in the Lab's record and in the record the committee would read.

## The Company and its Setting

The Nudge Lab was a unit of fourteen staff that advised UK government departments and public bodies on the design of choices: forms, defaults, letters, bills, menus and registration processes. It did not set taxes, ban products or pay subsidies. Its remit was narrower and, to its critics, more troubling: to change how a choice was presented while leaving every option open.

The Lab's charter required every intervention to preserve the option of saying no. To hold itself to that, its ethics panel, chaired by Dr Siân Lloyd-Hughes, kept a freedom gauge, an index from 0 to 100 that recorded how much choice the Lab's portfolio of interventions had left intact. The gauge began each programme at 100. Most designs drew something from it, some far more than others, and nothing the Lab did later gave any back. Lloyd-Hughes had told the committee's clerk that she would bring the gauge to the hearing.

The Lab also had a house ritual. Before designing anything, an architect sat through the choice as the public met it (the job offer, the menu, the bill, the form) and made it on the spot, without warning. Only then did the evaluators show what the public, in aggregate, had done. Several of the Lab's staff called it the most instructive ten minutes of each brief. Mehta had been warned that it was also the most humbling.

## The Situation

At 08:40 on January 12 the Lab's gauges stood where every programme began (see Exhibit 1): total measured effect at 0%, the freedom gauge at 100, no diagnoses logged and no targets met.

The first brief came from Calderbrook Parcels, a carrier with 2,400 staff. Most were already enrolled in its workplace pension under the statutory duty. The brief concerned the 900 who were not: drivers' assistants under 22 and part-timers below the earnings trigger, who could join only by ticking a box on their offer letter. The box came unticked. Forty per cent of the 900 had ticked it. The company wanted participation above 85% and had asked the Lab how to get there.

Mehta's own ten minutes had already happened. That morning the evaluators had handed her a mock offer letter with the same unticked box. She had read it, signed it and left the box empty.

The other five briefs were already on the plan. A hospital trust wanted uptake of healthy dishes in its staff canteen up 20% without removing anything from the menu; on the menu the Lab had been shown, the first item was a £65 steak. An energy regulator wanted the next smart-meter display to cut household use by 4% with tariffs unchanged. A health body wanted at least 75% of adults to have registered an organ-donation decision, with the freedom gauge still above 50 once the design was in. A financial-inclusion programme wanted the savings rate of low-income households up 15%; in her own session Mehta would be offered £80 today or £100 in a month. And the committee wanted the Lab's position.

If Mehta changed nothing, her own file suggested what would follow. Most of Calderbrook's 900 would leave the box unticked, the canteen would keep selling what it sold, and the gauge would sit at 100 on a record with nothing in it to defend. The Lab's director, Anthony Ferreira, had made the other half of the argument in her first week. A portfolio that took nothing from the gauge and met no target, he said, would be the easiest thing in the world to defend and the hardest to justify funding.

## What Mehta Controlled

For each brief she decided two things.

The first was the **diagnosis**: the one bias she judged to be driving the behaviour in front of her. The Lab's diagnostic sheet listed six: default or status-quo bias, anchoring, framing, present bias (the pull of today over later), loss aversion and herding. Each brief's sheet offered four of them, and only one answer could be logged. The Lab's evaluators held that a design aimed at the wrong bias tended to underdeliver, however sound it looked.

The second was the **intervention**, chosen from the designs the commissioning body had agreed to consider (see Exhibit 2). For Calderbrook: keep the opt-in box as it was; require every new hire to make an active yes-or-no choice at hiring; enrol staff automatically with the right to opt out; or enrol them automatically with contributions that stepped up at future pay rises. For the canteen: take the expensive anchor dish off the menu, add a premium healthy dish at the top, put the healthy dishes first and at eye level, or feature a rotating chef's healthy pick. For the smart meter: show kilowatt-hours only, show the cost in pounds only, show a comparison with neighbours, show a smiling or frowning face for efficiency, or show the carbon footprint in kilograms of CO2. For donor registration: a law of presumed consent with the right to opt out, a mandatory active choice for every adult, a prompted choice at driving-licence renewal, or a purely emotional campaign to opt in. For savings: a marginally higher interest rate, a deposit that locked the money away, an app that rounded purchases up into savings, a prize draw for savers, or a scheme in which households committed future pay rises to savings in advance. For the committee: hard paternalism, on the ground that government knew best; nudging without shoving; rejecting nudging as manipulation; or nudges disclosed openly, default included.

Four figures would follow her through the six months. The **total effect** added up the measured effect of every design deployed, in per cent. The **freedom gauge** showed what was left of the 100. The **diagnoses confirmed** showed how many of her readings the evaluators had upheld. The **targets met** showed on how many briefs the measured effect had reached the commissioning body's target. The Lab's log recorded all of it brief by brief (bias, whether it held, design, effect, gauge, target), and after the sixth brief Ferreira would grade the portfolio out of 100 from that log.

## Closing

At 09:05 Mehta pulled the Calderbrook file onto one screen and the blank design sheet onto the other. The mock offer letter lay beside the keyboard, box still empty, her signature underneath. The sheet had two lines, one for the bias and one for the design. On Friday they would become the first entry in the record Lloyd-Hughes would carry into the committee room.
