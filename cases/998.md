# Rutavela: Nine People, Six Time Zones

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-998 · **Companion simulation:** Express 998 — *RemoteFirstLeadership — Rituals That Keep a Distributed Team Real* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, April 7, 2026, at 08:10 Madrid time, Elena Moraleda opened the quarterly pulse survey for the team she ran at Rutavela Software. She read the line headed *team connection* first. It stood at 5.0 out of 10. In March 2025 it had stood at 8.0. In the free-text box below it, one answer read: *I don't know who half of these people are.*

Moraleda, engineering manager of Rutavela's Integrations team, knew the survey was not the worst news of the month. In the previous fortnight two of her engineers had told her, separately, that they were considering offers elsewhere. Her director, Begoña Larrauri, wanted her plan on Monday, April 13: where the team's effort would go in the quarter that had just begun, how the changes would be introduced, and whether, as Rutavela's chief operating officer had started to suggest, the answer was to bring people back to an office.

## The Protagonist

Moraleda was forty-one. She had joined Rutavela in 2019 as a senior engineer in its Madrid office and had run the Integrations team since 2022. When the company gave up most of its office space and went remote-first in 2023, her team was five people in three time zones, and the move had looked like a change of address: the same stand-up, the same planning meeting, the same one-to-ones, held on video. By April 2026 the team was nine people, Moraleda included, in six time zones (see **Exhibit 1**). Six of them had been hired after the office closed. The last time the team had met in person was a two-day offsite in Madrid in August 2025, which five people attended. Four of the nine had never been in a room with the rest.

## The Decision

Moraleda had to decide, quarter by quarter for the next four quarters, how to spend the team's capacity for change across five working practices, and how to put them in place. The first quarter's plan was due on Monday.

## The Company and its Setting

Rutavela Software, S.L., founded in 2014 and registered in Madrid, sold route-planning and dispatch software to delivery fleets in Spain, Portugal, Mexico, Brazil, India and Australia. It employed about 210 people. The Integrations team built and maintained the connectors between Rutavela's platform and its customers' warehouse, telematics and billing systems, and most of its engineers lived close to the customers whose systems they knew. Rutavela kept one office, a forty-desk hub in Madrid. Seven of Moraleda's eight reports had been hired on contracts that named their home city as their place of work.

## The Situation

The team was not failing at its work. Releases shipped, and the team chat was busy from Sydney's morning to Mexico City's evening. But one-to-ones had been getting shorter, the weekly meeting had become a round of status updates in which nobody laughed, and decisions took three to four days of back-and-forth in threads that reopened whenever someone in another time zone woke up. In the Madrid office the same kind of decision had usually been settled in a day. Larrauri's group tracked that pace as decision speed against the office baseline; the Integrations team stood at 0.3 times it. No hour of the day fell inside working hours in all six cities.

Nadia Ferreira, Rutavela's people partner for engineering, rated the team's retention risk as high. If nothing changed, she expected the two engineers who had spoken to Moraleda to resign within ninety days and a third to follow. The last time Rutavela had replaced an integration engineer, the search had taken five months, and nobody else had understood the connectors the leaver had built.

Several people had already offered Moraleda an answer. Íñigo Salvatierra, the chief operating officer, had told the March leadership meeting that teams which sat together decided things in a day, and that Rutavela should consider bringing teams back to hubs. Diego Arreola, the team's most senior engineer, in Mexico City, had told Moraleda that nobody needed another meeting; the team needed things written down once and kept decided. Marta Quintela, the product designer in Lisbon, had argued at the last retrospective that the problem was not process at all, but that half the team had never shared a meal.

Nothing had yet been spent on any of this in 2026. Larrauri had not set a ceiling, but every euro spent on team practices and travel would appear in the quarterly cost report. Finance had already asked whether an in-person week, quoted at €25,000 to €40,000 for nine people over five days in one city, would be the first thing cut if the quarter came in tight (see **Exhibit 2**).

## What Moraleda Controlled

Each quarter Moraleda counted what she could push through as a hundred points of effort: her own hours, and the team's attention for anything that was not customer work. She could not spend more than a hundred, and Larrauri had said that a quarter with fewer than forty committed would not count as a plan. She could divide the points across five practices in any proportion.

The first was **async-by-default**: writing as the normal way of working, with a rule that every non-urgent thread received a reply within twenty-four hours. The second was a **decision log**: a single record of every decision, with its owner, its date, the options considered and the reason, to be the reference whenever a question came back. The third was a pair of **anchor meetings**: a weekly forty-five-minute team sync and a forty-five-minute one-to-one with each person every two weeks. The fourth was **explicit human connection**: random coffee pairings between people who did not work together, a short personal video update at the start of the weekly sync, and birthdays and work anniversaries in the calendar. The fifth was the **annual in-person week**: booking five days for all nine in one city, and protecting the money for it.

She also had to choose, each quarter, **how** the practices would be put in place. She could write them herself and announce them as the team's new rules. She could draft them, take the team's input and launch them together, over a draft week, an input week, a launch week and a review. Or she could set the redesign aside and back Salvatierra's proposal, asking her team to return to an office.

Ferreira's tracker would show how far each of the five practices had taken hold, starting from zero, and what the team's practices and travel had cost that quarter. At the end of each quarter Larrauri would read three numbers: the team connection score from the pulse survey, decision speed against the office baseline, and Ferreira's rating of retention risk. At the end of the fourth quarter she would judge the redesign on the same three.

## Closing

On Friday, April 10, at 18:40 in Madrid, Moraleda opened a blank document and titled it *Q2: how we work*. In Mexico City it was 10:40 on Friday morning; in Sydney, 02:40 on Saturday. Before going to bed, the engineer in Sydney had left a message in the team channel: *Is this going to be something we get a say in?* Under it, the engineer in São Paulo had added: *Or can we just have the rules by Monday?* Larrauri's call was on Monday at 09:00.
