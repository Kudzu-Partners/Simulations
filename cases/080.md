## Abstract

Tienda Azul was, in the case universe, a small retail shop founded by María Castillo in the commercial corridor of a mid-sized capital city, opening for business on Monday, February 2, 2026 with an owner equity contribution of USD 25,000. The first four-month operating period from February through May generated eighteen discrete business events — ranging from the founder's initial investment, a rent payment, equipment purchase, inventory acquisition on credit, cash sales, credit sales, wage payments, receivables collection, accounts-payable settlement, depreciation, and interest accrual, to an end-of-period inventory count — that the business's part-time Chief Accountant would process through four progressive phases. Pablo Sandoval, a qualified accountant retained on a part-time retainer to handle the Tienda Azul books, faced a single integrated engagement: convert the eighteen events into journal entries, post to ledgers, build the Income Statement and Balance Sheet, and interpret five financial ratios with five specific action items for the founder. The case opens at 8:18 a.m. on Monday, June 1, 2026, at the Tienda Azul back office, as Sandoval opens the Phase-1 journal-entry workbook for the first-period close.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Record eighteen business transactions as journal entries applying the debit-credit discipline, chart-of-accounts, and recognition rules correctly.
2. Post the journal entries to T-account ledgers and verify that the trial balance balances, diagnosing any imbalance as an error source.
3. Construct the Income Statement and the Balance Sheet from the ledger balances, and explain how the two statements articulate through net income and retained earnings.
4. Compute and interpret five financial ratios (current ratio, gross margin, return on equity, debt-to-equity, inventory turnover) as signals for specific managerial actions.
5. Recommend a complete Phase-4 action-item set for the founder based on the ratio signals, and identify the single transaction most correlated with the period's ratio outcomes.

## The Protagonist

Pablo Sandoval, 44, was a qualified accountant with a degree in accounting from the Universidad de la Capital and a sixteen-year career spanning three mid-market accountancy firms before establishing a part-time independent practice in 2021 servicing founder-run micro-and-small businesses in the commercial corridor of the capital. He had, across the 2021–2025 window, serviced thirty-one micro-business first-period closes, with an average client retention above twelve months. His engagement with Tienda Azul had been negotiated on December 8, 2025 at a monthly retainer that covered the Phase-1 and Phase-2 mechanics and included a Phase-3 financial-statements build and a Phase-4 ratio interpretation at the end of each four-month operating period. His December onboarding note to María had read: "I will keep the eighteen-event discipline. You will keep the receipts. The rest is the craft."

## The Decision

Sandoval had to commit, by 9:00 a.m. on Friday, June 5, the four-phase closing package that María would treat as the first-period reference record:

1. **Phase 1 — Journal Entries** — whether to apply the accrual-basis recognition rule across the eighteen events and whether to flag any events as requiring reclassification after the April-period review.
2. **Phase 2 — Ledger Posting** — whether to post to a seven-account T-account structure (Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, Accounts Payable, Common Stock, Revenue, Expenses) or to expand to a more granular twelve-account structure that tracked equipment, accumulated depreciation, interest payable, and retained earnings separately.
3. **Phase 3 — Financial Statements and Phase 4 — Ratio Interpretation** — whether to deliver the Income Statement and Balance Sheet with a single end-of-period snapshot, and whether to interpret five financial ratios (current ratio, gross margin, ROE, debt-to-equity, inventory turnover) with three, four, or five specific action items for María.

The three sub-decisions would interact and would compound across the four phases. A closing package that applied accrual-basis recognition across the eighteen events, posted to a twelve-account structure that separated equipment and accumulated depreciation, delivered the Income Statement and Balance Sheet with a single end-of-period snapshot, and interpreted five financial ratios with five specific action items produced, on Sandoval's working papers, a balanced trial balance to the dollar, a net income of USD 3,430, an ending equity of USD 21,670, and five action items María could execute across the June-through-September period.

## Company Background

Tienda Azul had been founded by María Castillo, a 38-year-old former retail-store manager at a national chain, on January 28, 2026, with a single-owner-operator retail concept focused on a mid-market household-goods mix across three product categories — kitchen and tabletop, home-textile, and small-decorative-object — sourced from a cluster of eight national and two imported suppliers. The store's single location occupied 72 square meters of commercial-district frontage on Calle del Comercio, at a monthly rent of USD 2,000. The founder had contributed USD 25,000 of personal savings on February 2, 2026 — the equity investment recorded as the first of the eighteen business events.

The first-period transaction log carried a chronology that told, if the accounting rules were applied correctly, a legible business story. Events 1 through 7 — the owner's USD 25,000 investment, the first month's rent of USD 2,000, the store-equipment purchase of USD 5,000, the inventory acquisition of USD 12,000 on thirty-day credit from the principal supplier, the first cash sales of USD 3,500, wages of USD 800, and the collection of USD 2,400 from a credit sale — sat inside the February operating month. Events 8 through 13 spanned March and April: additional inventory acquisitions, a supplier A/P settlement of USD 4,800, a monthly rent, monthly wages, additional cash and credit sales, and a small equipment-maintenance expense. Events 14 through 18 covered May: the final month's rent and wages, a depreciation accrual on the equipment, an interest accrual on a USD 3,000 short-term supplier advance carried at 9% annualized, and the May-end inventory count that completed the period.

The competitive context for Tienda Azul's first period comprised four adjacent small-retail operators in the Calle del Comercio corridor — a household-goods peer at forty meters to the east, a decorative-object specialist at eighty meters to the west, a kitchen-supply operator at a block north, and a mid-market home-textile chain at three hundred meters south — none of which, in the first period, had launched a competitive pricing event.

## Industry and Market Context

The small-retail-business accounting landscape in 2026 was characterized by three structural features the four-phase closing package sat inside. First, the post-2023 LATAM small-business reporting environment had, under a regional harmonization effort, standardized around a twelve-month financial-reporting periodicity for tax purposes but had retained founder-flexibility on the internal management-reporting cadence — the four-month internal-close rhythm Tienda Azul had chosen reflected an owner-operator preference for mid-year visibility into ratios and action items, rather than a regulatory requirement. Second, the LATAM small-retail inventory-turnover benchmark had, across a 2023–2025 sample of 218 household-goods-focused small retailers, clustered at a four-month-period turnover of 1.6–2.0 times for the category — a benchmark that placed Tienda Azul's first-period turnover of 1.1 below the peer-sample range. Third, the owner-operator financial-literacy literature had, across a 2020–2024 sample of ninety-four LATAM small-business-survival studies, documented a 0.52 correlation between a founder's demonstrated ability to interpret three basic ratios (current, gross margin, inventory turnover) and the business's month-twenty-four survival — a finding that elevated the Phase-4 interpretation from a bookkeeping formality to a survival instrument.

Empirically, the post-2018 financial-accounting pedagogy had two robust findings the four-phase closing package had to respect. First, teaching sequences that moved progressively from transactions to journal entries to ledgers to statements to ratios — the exact sequence this case framed — produced student comprehension outcomes 2.2 times higher than sequences that attempted to front-load the Financial Statements without the journal-entry-and-ledger discipline. Second, the single most common first-period-close error in small-business bookkeeping was the mis-recognition of inventory acquisitions — specifically, the recording of inventory as an expense at the time of purchase rather than at the time of sale through COGS — an error that produced trial-balance imbalances in 43% of first-year practitioner closes.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Sandoval had, across the February-through-May period, received weekly batches of receipts and transaction records from María in a small three-ring binder. He had, at the end of each month, performed a light Phase-1 running-record to catch any recognition errors early, and he had flagged two events for reclassification at the period close — the equipment-maintenance expense in March (initially recorded as a direct expense; Sandoval had confirmed capitalization was not required at the USD 340 threshold under the small-business materiality rule) and the USD 3,000 supplier advance in April (initially recorded as a payable; Sandoval had determined it carried explicit interest terms and required a separate interest-accrual at period end).

### What Sandoval knew

He had built three alternative closing packages on his working papers. A Minimalist Package (seven-account T-account structure, single-snapshot statements, three-ratio interpretation with three action items) would deliver the legally required reporting with the smallest scope — but would miss the equipment-and-depreciation articulation and the inventory-turnover signal. A Standard Package (twelve-account structure, single-snapshot statements, five-ratio interpretation with five action items) would deliver the full founder-facing report with the ratio-based action items the case's pedagogical ambition implied. An Expanded Package (fifteen-account structure with detailed expense categorization, three-month-average ratio interpretation, eight action items) would deliver a sophisticated report but would exceed the retainer envelope and the founder's bandwidth to execute.

### What he did not know

Whether María would be able to act on the five ratio-based action items inside the June-through-September window (his 2025 client retention had been 91% on five-item packages but below 64% on eight-item packages); whether the supplier advance interest-accrual reclassification would generate a follow-up question from the supplier's accounting contact on the June statement delivery; whether María's decision to start a second product line — hinted at in their May 15 check-in conversation — would reshape the September-period close; and whether the regulatory harmonization on small-business reporting would extend into additional reporting requirements for the 2027 cycle.

### What others were saying

**The founder, María Castillo**: *"Give me five things I can do by September. Three is too few to know. Eight is too many to act on."*

**The supplier's accounts-receivable clerk**: *"Book the thirty-day trade credit cleanly. If the payment lands on the thirty-first day, the supplier's auto-dunning picks it up."*

**The small-business-advisor network Sandoval belonged to**: *"The inventory turnover ratio is the first-period conversation. Slow turnover is the prompt. Fast turnover on the first period is often a re-buy signal. Interpret, do not dictate."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Sandoval had narrowed the space to three closing packages.

- **Option A — Minimalist:** Seven-account T-account structure, single-snapshot statements, three-ratio interpretation with three action items. Delivers the legally required reporting. Misses the equipment-and-depreciation and inventory-turnover signals.

- **Option B — Standard:** Twelve-account structure, single-snapshot statements, five-ratio interpretation (current ratio 4.2, gross margin 54%, ROE 15.8%, debt-to-equity 0.22, inventory turnover 1.1) with five action items. Matches the founder's five-things commitment. Aligns with the retainer envelope.

- **Option C — Expanded:** Fifteen-account structure, three-month-average ratio interpretation, eight action items. Sophisticated. Exceeds retainer envelope and founder execution bandwidth.

Sandoval's private view, written on the first page of his working papers, was that Option B was the right closing package and that the single subsequent move most correlated with María's September-period-close outcome was the inventory-turnover action item — specifically, the recommendation to reduce the June-through-September inventory re-buy by 22% relative to the February-through-May period, the implementation of a payment-plan review for the three slowest-moving SKUs, and the reinvestment of USD 1,400 of net income into a small second product-line pilot that María had referenced.
