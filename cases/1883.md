# Alimentos Don Aldo: The Signature

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1883 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1883 — *Scaling the Family Firm* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On a Tuesday morning in March 2026, Aldo Bonetti sat at his desk in Rosario and read, for the third time, the letter of intent Hiper Litoral — the country's second-largest supermarket chain — had sent him. It offered a national listing for his company's frozen ready-meals: an estimated ARS 4,200 million a year in incremental revenue, against the ARS 9,600 million the company earned in 2025. He had forty-five days to sign it. In the same folder sat a note from the family's lawyer about a shareholders' agreement that still did not exist, and a date in June for the heart surgery he had delayed for a year.

## The Protagonist

Aldo Bonetti had founded the company in 1991 as a one-room workshop making dry pasta and bottled sauces for shops within walking distance. Thirty-five years later, Alimentos Don Aldo employed 240 people across a single plant and a fleet of twelve delivery trucks, and Aldo still personally approved every purchase order above ARS 2 million, still held the company's only banking signature, and still carried in his own memory which supplier would extend terms and which retail buyer needed a personal phone call. His daughter Lucía, 34, ran the commercial side of the business. His son Tomás, 31, ran the plant. Neither had ever been given the authority to sign a cheque without him.

## The Decision

Four questions stood between Aldo and a signature.

The first was what was actually stopping the company from growing. It could be a capital problem: find the ARS 1,500 million and the ARS 860 million the build required, and the rest would follow. It could be a demand problem: the listing itself was the prize, and capacity and structure were details for afterward. It could be a capability problem: one founder holding the only banking signature could not run ARS 4,200 million of new demand. Or it could be a margin problem: an 11% EBITDA margin left too little room to grow further on its own. He also had to price the runway — a comfortable eighteen to twenty-four months, since the contract's own revenue would soon cover the build; no immediate threat, since current cash flow covered near-term operating needs; or a tighter nine to twelve months, once the tunnel and the trapped working capital landed before the new cash did — and how clearly he kept the two questions apart.

The second was who would run the company once he stepped back, with the surgery weeks away. He could hire an outside general manager for ARS 90 million a year and hand over the signature and the purchase-order approval too. He could keep doing it all himself, as he had for thirty-five years, trusted by the buyers and suppliers. He could promote Lucía or Tomás straight into the top job, and settle succession and management together. Or he could hire that same general manager to run daily operations while keeping the signature and the approval to himself. Whichever leader he chose, he still had to say how much real authority — not just a title — he actually handed across, plus two more: whether the account kept his as the only name on it, gained a second authorized signatory with tiered limits, or raised the threshold for his own approval; and whether Hiper Litoral ran through a full key-account team with dedicated electronic ordering, through the existing sales desk exactly as every other account, or through a single named account manager without the ordering system yet.

The third was how to pay for the roughly ARS 2,360 million the build would cost, and what to recommend on the contract. He could take a regional third of the volume instead, and leave the national rollout for later. He could sign the national listing in full and fund the whole build. Or he could decline the listing this year and hold the balance sheet where it was. The money could come from bank debt on the tunnel, which kept the company entirely his but pushed leverage toward roughly 3.1 times EBITDA if it carried the whole load; from supplier and retailer financing, which eased the 75-day cash trap directly if they were willing to extend it; from retained earnings generated by the business itself, free of new debt or dilution but slow and limited to what the company actually earned; or from the ARS 1,200 million a regional private-equity fund had offered for a 30% stake, which solved the cash question but diluted the family's ownership (see Exhibit 2) — and carried its own choice: negotiate the board seat, the capital-spending veto and the exit clause first, or accept the fund's headline terms exactly as offered, to close quickly.

The fourth concerned timing. He could put a protocol, a council and a continuity plan in place before June. He could name, once, which child he leaned toward, without yet putting anything in writing. Or he could set the whole question aside until the contract had bedded in. Beside that timing sat the governance itself — leave succession exactly as an informal understanding among the family, with nothing put in writing; put a protocol, a shareholders' agreement, a family council and an outside board seat in place; or draft the protocol and the shareholders' agreement first, then build the council and board as trust grew — and the question of Lucía and Tomás themselves: name one of the two as heir now, and expect the other to accept the decision; leave both of their roles undefined so that neither one felt passed over or singled out; or set out a merit-based path for each, with milestones and a plan for who covered the surgery. Underneath it sat one more thing: how firmly he treated succession as a business discipline, not a private family matter.

## The Company and its Setting

Alimentos Don Aldo S.A. made dry pasta, bottled sauces and premium frozen ready-meals, sold to supermarket chains across Argentina's Litoral region and Buenos Aires province. Revenue had grown 22% in real terms over two years, almost entirely on the frozen line, reaching ARS 9,600 million in 2025 — about US$8.0 million — at an 11% EBITDA margin and ARS 700 million of free cash flow a year. The company carried debt of only 0.8 times EBITDA, financed conservatively. A family-owned rival in Córdoba was bidding for the same listing, with the plant capacity to take it first.

## The Situation

The offer's terms complicated the headline number. Supplying it meant doubling frozen capacity within about nine months, costing ARS 1,500 million for a second freezing tunnel and cold-storage expansion. Its 75-day payment terms, against a 40-day average today, would trap an estimated ARS 860 million in additional working capital — a combined build of roughly ARS 2,360 million against free cash flow of only ARS 700 million a year. The retailer wanted a signed letter of intent in forty-five days and full supply by the fourth quarter of 2026. The dashboard in use opened at 12 months of cash runway, a management-depth score of 18 out of 100, an owner-control score of 100 out of 100, and a family-alignment score of 55 out of 100 (see Exhibit 1). If nothing changed before June: a single signature could not clear the retailer's electronic orders from a hospital room; ARS 700 million of annual free cash flow could not by itself carry a ARS 2,360 million build; and no protocol yet existed to say who ran the company while Aldo could not.

## What Aldo Controlled

Aldo would answer the four questions in order — diagnosis, leadership, financing and succession. Whatever he decided would be read back on four measures at once — months of cash runway left, real decision-making authority delegated below him, ownership and control the family retained, and how aligned the family stayed.

## Closing

Aldo set the letter of intent down, still unsigned, and drew a single sheet of paper toward him. Forty-five days remained to sign the contract. The surgery was already on the calendar for June. He picked up his pen.
