# Maquinaria Industrial: Four Days to Quito

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-611 · **Companion simulation:** Express 611 — *Multimodal Logistics and Risk Mitigation* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Wednesday, April 15, 2026, at 10:30 in the morning, Natalia Rojas Quintero stood on the loading dock of Maquinaria Industrial Ltda. in Bogotá and watched a crane lower a crated textile spinner onto blocks. The machine weighed 8,500 kilograms, was worth COP 500 million, and was due at a customer's plant in Quito, Ecuador, by Sunday, April 19. Nothing had yet been booked to carry it there.

Rojas, the company's logistics manager, had until the end of the business day to choose how the spinner would travel and whether to ask the customer for more time. The rest of the shipment would follow from that choice: the insurance, the handling on the way, the answer to whatever the road or the border produced, and in the end what was left of the order's margin.

## The Protagonist

Natalia Rojas Quintero was thirty-eight and had run logistics at Maquinaria Industrial for four years, after eight years with a freight forwarder in Cartagena. She had shipped machines to Peru, Mexico and Ecuador before, almost always by truck. She reported to the general manager, who had told her that morning that the spinner was "the order of the quarter" and that he wanted it delivered on time and profitable.

## The Decision

Rojas had four moments ahead of her. That day, the route and the conversation with the customer. Before departure, the cargo insurance and, if the machine went by rail, the terminal that would handle it in Cali. On the road, how to respond to the rain and to the border. And on arrival in Quito, the reconciliation of what the shipment had cost against what the order had been meant to earn (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Maquinaria Industrial Ltda. designed and built industrial machinery to order: textile spinners, paper-processing lines, screening systems for mining. It had 95 employees, revenue of about COP 18 billion a year, and 60% of its sales in Latin America. On export orders like this one, the company paid for transport to the customer's site and carried the risk of the cargo until it was delivered.

The customer was Hilanova Andina S.A., a textile manufacturer in Quito that had built its production schedule around the new spinner. The sales contract set delivery at the Quito plant by Sunday, April 19, four calendar days from the planned dispatch. It set liquidated damages of COP 100 million for each day of delay.

## The Situation

The spinner was a difficult load (see Exhibit 1). Its precision bearings and optical alignment could be ruined by a hard jolt. Its electronic controls and lubrication system could corrode if water got into the crate. And on parts of the road south, valuable cargo attracted theft. April was the rainy season in the Cauca Valley, with landslides on the mountain roads, and Ecuadorian customs at the Ipiales crossing had been running enhanced inspections for weeks.

The order carried a margin of about COP 100 million before logistics. Every peso spent on freight, insurance, handling or fees came out of that margin, and so did any penalty paid to the customer or any loss the insurance did not cover. A single uninsured total loss would cost the company the full value of the machine.

Two freight options were on Rojas's desk. The first was a truck-only route: one specialized trailer with relay drivers, four days door to door, for COP 50 million. It met the contract date, but the forwarder put the combined risk of shock damage and theft on that route at 7% to 10%. The second was multimodal: truck to a rail terminal, a rail leg into Cali, a transfer back to truck, and on to Quito. It cost COP 35 million, and the forwarder put the shock-and-theft risk at 3% to 4%. It took six days, two more than the contract allowed.

Rojas tracked every shipment on four figures, and all of them stood at their starting values. Cumulative logistics cost stood at zero. Delivery delay against plan stood at zero days. Expected loss, the risk-weighted value of damage or theft the company was carrying, also stood at zero. Projected net profit on the order, the margin left after everything else, stood at COP 100 million.

If Rojas booked nothing, the spinner would stay on the dock and the April 19 date would not move. Each day of delay after it carried COP 100 million in liquidated damages.

## What Rojas Controlled

That day, Rojas would choose the route: the four-day truck-only route at COP 50 million, or the six-day multimodal route at COP 35 million. She would also decide how to handle the customer. She could leave the contract as it stood and accept its deadline. Or she could call Hilanova Andina before dispatch and ask them to approve a six-day timeline.

Before departure, she would choose the cargo insurance. A named-peril policy cost COP 2.5 million. It covered listed perils such as collision, shock and theft, but not moisture damage; the broker put the chance of moisture damage at 2% to 3%, with a loss of about COP 200 million if it happened. A standard all-risk policy cost COP 4 million and covered moisture, shock and theft. An all-risk policy with an added shock-and-vibration rider cost COP 5 million. If the machine went by rail, she would also choose the transfer terminal in Cali. Depósito Central, a long-established terminal, charged COP 600,000 and had a damage record of about 2% on comparable machinery. LogíMax, a newer private hub, charged COP 1.5 million and claimed about 0.5%.

On the road, she would decide how to respond if heavy rain hit the Cauca Valley. On the truck route, she could continue on the main road, divert to a secondary road that added about eight hours, or stop and wait for the weather to clear. On the rail route, she could accept the train's delay, pay a priority surcharge to hold the schedule, or halt the train until the rain eased. She would also decide how to respond if customs at Ipiales held the machine for inspection. She could accept a delay of 18 to 24 hours at no cost, or pay a COP 1.2 million fee to expedite the inspection.

On arrival in Quito, she would close the project. That meant reconciling the logistics costs, any liquidated damages owed to the customer and any insurance claim against the order's margin.

After each moment, she would see the round's cost, the cumulative cost, the delivery delay in days, the expected loss she was carrying and the projected net profit on the order.

## Closing

At 11:15 the general manager put his head round the door of the dock office. "Truck or train?" he asked. "And does Quito know?" Rojas had two quotes on her screen and the customer's number on a sticky note beside them. The forwarder needed an answer by five.
