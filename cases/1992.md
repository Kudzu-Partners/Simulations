# Meridian Insights Group: One Stream, One Cycle

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1992 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1992 — *Revenue Resilience* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, March 3, 2026, Priya Nandakumar read the non-renewal letter twice and then read the paragraph in the middle a third time. A federal government department that had bought Meridian Insights Group's flagship subscription every year since 1998 was not buying it again. The department's procurement officer had written that a generative artificial intelligence (AI) research assistant now produced summaries of the same public data that were, in the officer's phrase, good enough. The contract was worth A$4.1 million a year. It was the largest single account Meridian had.

Nandakumar had been the company's first Chief Innovation Officer for eleven days, after four months reviewing the flagship line. Before the end of the fiscal year she had to put a plan in front of the board showing a credible path to bring Meridian's dependence on that one product line below 60% of revenue within 24 months. She had A$12 million, ring-fenced, to do it with. If the board did not believe the plan, its stated alternative was to sell Meridian's data assets while they still commanded a premium.

## The Protagonist

Priya Nandakumar, 46, had joined Meridian in October 2025 from a Sydney analytics firm, where she spent six years building and then selling a subscription benchmarking product to the same corporate buyers Meridian served. Before that she had been a research director at Meridian, from 2011 to 2016, and had left after a new-product proposal she championed was deferred for a second year. The board created the Chief Innovation Officer role for her and gave it a seat at the executive table and no operating headcount. Her contract tied much of her incentive to the group's revenue mix at the end of the second year, not to the current year's profit.

## The Decision

Nandakumar had to decide how to spend A$12 million across one planning cycle, and to defend it to a board that had already named its fallback. Four questions sat inside it: what Meridian's real fragility was, which new offerings to fund and how heavily, whether to build a standing capability that outlasted the cycle, and what to do about the risk she could not out-build.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Insights Group Pty Ltd, founded in Brisbane in 1994, sold business information: subscription access to industry data, benchmarking reports and a research library covering 28 verticals across Australia and New Zealand. It had turned over A$96 million at an 18% operating margin, or roughly A$17.3 million (see **Exhibit 1**).

Seventy-four percent of that revenue — about A$71 million — came from one product line, the "Sector Reports" subscription. For most of three decades Sector Reports had renewed above 90% and had needed almost no defending. The company had launched no material new revenue stream since 2014.

Underneath the reports sat 30 years of proprietary data across the same 28 verticals, collected through Meridian's own surveys and client submissions and held nowhere else. Some of what Sector Reports contained, though, was public data its analysts collected, cleaned and wrote up.

The competitive picture had changed twice. Generative AI assistants had begun summarising public sources at no cost to the user. Separately, a venture-backed firm called DataLive had raised A$35 million to deliver live analytics through an application programming interface (API) at roughly one-third Meridian's price, into Meridian's own mid-market subscribers.

## The Situation

The cancellation removed 4.3% of group revenue in one renewal cycle — the first material loss the flagship line had ever taken. Renewal across the line had fallen from 92% to 86% in the latest quarter. Two further enterprise clients, worth A$5.6 million between them, had told their account managers they intended to pilot DataLive at renewal.

A confidential board paper circulated in February had modelled a 15% erosion of the Sector Reports line inside 24 months and called it plausible rather than pessimistic. On A$71 million that was about A$10.7 million of revenue — and because the cost base carried the analyst teams whether or not the subscriptions renewed, the paper had that erosion halving operating profit, about A$8.6 million.

Sector Reports' general manager, Warren Coutts, argued the money belonged to the flagship: 86% renewal was still a franchise, and faster, deeper reports would hold more revenue than products Meridian had never sold.

Meridian's planning team had built an internal index, scoring the business from 0 to 100 on how spread its income was, how much new value it was creating, and how much of its exposure was covered. Entering the cycle it read 38; the board wanted 70. A second measure put 68% of current revenue in the direct path of AI-summarised or API-delivered substitution.

If Nandakumar changed nothing, the arithmetic was undramatic: renewals would keep sliding at their current rate, the two signalled defections would land, dependence would stay in the seventies, and the board would reach its deadline with a strong margin on a shrinking base and a trade sale on the agenda.

## What Nandakumar Controlled

She controlled the diagnosis first. She could name the fragility as the concentration of revenue in one product line; or as the concentration of that revenue in a small number of large accounts, answerable by replacing the departed logo and winning others; or as the renewal rate itself, answerable by defending churn; or she could record that the cancellation was one buyer's judgement rather than a pattern. Alongside it she had to say which asset was genuinely defensible — the 30-year proprietary datasets, the Meridian name and reputation, or the editorial craft of turning public data into a report clients paid for — and how to frame the A$12 million: on defending the current business, spread across the current business, near-term new offerings and long-dated options, or entirely on a long-dated bet. She also had to set the cycle's ambition on a nought-to-hundred scale: how much change she was asking a comfortable company to absorb in one year.

She controlled the portfolio. Five candidate offerings had been costed (see **Exhibit 2**), each with its own buyer and its own relationship to Sector Reports. She could put the money into upgrading Sector Reports itself and fund no new offering; fund the analytics API alone; fund three with staggered timing; or fund all five at a smaller amount each. She had to state a position on the AI assistant built on Meridian's own datasets, which would take some Sector Reports revenue with it: build it deliberately, decline to build it, or leave it out of the cycle. And she had to name the figure she was committing this round, knowing the A$12 million covered the whole cycle.

She controlled whether Meridian bought a standing capability or only offerings. She could fund a function that scanned for disruption early, a stage-gated pipeline that turned scanned opportunities into funded experiments, a unit that industrialised new offerings from the datasets, or none of them. She had to decide whether the new ventures were governed and measured on the terms that ran the core — its 18% margin, its quarterly targets — on separate terms of their own, or on no terms at all; whether they were reviewed annually, on a fast cadence against leading indicators, or on billings with a two-quarter cut-off; and how much of the remaining budget the capability received.

She controlled the hedge and the pitch. She could partner with an AI provider and place Meridian's verified data inside its assistant, acquire a smaller analytics firm, combine a partnership with multi-year flagship contracts and expansion into further verticals, or hold no hedge at all. She could sign multi-year lock-ins on Sector Reports now, run it as it stood, or wind it down quickly. She had to choose what the board heard first: what the flagship had become, the single new offering the plan rested on, or the mix. And she had to settle how hard to press the argument, on the same nought-to-hundred scale, that the answer lay away from the flagship rather than inside it.

## Closing

At 7:15 that evening Nandakumar wrote three numbers on the whiteboard in the empty innovation office — 74, 60, 12 — and sat looking at them. The board met at the end of the fiscal year. She had one cycle, one budget and a company that had not launched anything new in twelve years.
