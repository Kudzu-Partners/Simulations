# Catalyst Learning Partners: Learning that Sticks

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1851 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1851 — *Learning that Sticks* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On a Monday morning in March 2026, Renata Cole, a program director at Catalyst Learning Partners in Miami, read the letter from Meridian Energy's newly hired chief human resources officer and found the deadline unambiguous, even if the assignment was not. Meridian, a $6 billion utility and Catalyst's second-largest account at $1.6 million over three years, had frozen every dollar of further learning spending. The CHRO was giving Catalyst one opening to keep the relationship: design a single flagship program for 120 of Meridian's leaders, deliver it inside a $480,000 all-in budget, launch it within ten weeks, and show, in a report due four months later, that the program had changed something real.

## The Protagonist

Cole, 44, had directed custom programs at Catalyst since 2019, after seven years designing leadership curricula inside a Fortune 500 utility's own corporate university. She had built her reputation less on programs that impressed on day one than on programs that survived their first renewal. Her compensation, like that of Catalyst's ten other directors, moved in part with the health of the accounts she carried past a first contract into a second and third year.

## The Decision

Cole's assignment ran against a clock that would not pause for good intentions. Over the ten weeks before launch and the months that followed, she would have to settle, in sequence: which single problem among the ones Meridian's CHRO had named would become the program's target; how the $480,000 would be built into an actual program; how much of that budget would be set aside to prove the program had worked, rather than simply run it; and, once results arrived four months later — some favorable, some genuinely ambiguous — what she would tell the CHRO and her CFO about what the program had and had not accomplished.

## The Company and its Setting

Catalyst Learning Partners was the custom executive-education unit of a top-tier U.S. business school, operating out of Miami since 2014. It generated $14.2 million a year from roughly 42 corporate engagements serving about 3,800 participants, running lean on eleven full-time program directors and a roster of sixty contracted faculty and executive coaches, at a contribution margin of 28% after faculty fees, platform costs, and delivery. Two years before, Catalyst had staked its position on a promise most competitors would not put in writing: every custom program shipped with a measured report on whether participants' behavior had changed and whether the client's business had felt it, not a satisfaction score alone. The promise had won marquee accounts — and raised the bar the unit now had to clear on every one of them, including Meridian.

## The Situation

Meridian's brief was deliberately unresolved. Its CHRO had named three problems and ranked none of them: leaders recently promoted into roles that required influence beyond their own function; managers who were slow to use the analytics and AI tools already available to them; and business units whose silos were stalling a major grid-modernization effort. She told Cole directly that she would not choose among the three herself.

The numbers Cole inherited when she opened the brief were not encouraging (see Exhibit 1). Catalyst's internal tracking on the still-undesigned program showed a behavior-change index — a ninety-day measure of whether the target behavior had actually moved on the job — at 12 out of 100, against a renewal-grade target of 60. A second measure, of how well any new skill was likely to carry into daily work, stood at 10. A third, of how defensible the eventual results would be to a skeptical finance function, stood at 15. None of the $480,000 had yet been committed, though $90,000 of design time was already spent, pre-contract, and not recoverable if Meridian declined. If none of the three numbers moved in the months ahead, the report reaching the CHRO's desk would show a finished program with nothing to prove it had changed anyone's behavior.

## What Cole Controlled

Cole's first decision was which of Meridian's three named problems would become the program's single target — the silo-bound business units stalling a major grid-modernization effort, a program built to advance all three problems at once, the newly promoted leaders struggling to lead beyond their own function, or the slow adopters of analytics and AI tools already available to them — and how concentrated, on a dial she set herself, the design would be on that one target rather than spread across several. Alongside that choice sat what would count as proof at ninety days: a satisfaction score and completion rate of the kind most competing programs already reported, a scored index built from manager and peer observation of the behavior on the job, leaving that choice for later once the program itself was designed, or a system-data signal drawn from tool-usage logs or cross-unit project activity.

Her second decision was how to build the program against the $480,000 cap (see Exhibit 2): a two-day in-person intensive bringing all 120 leaders together at once, at $165,000; a blended eight-week cohort mixing live virtual sessions, self-paced modules, and a team project, at $210,000; on-the-job action learning paired with executive coaching across the ten weeks, at $240,000; self-paced digital modules and AI-simulation practice for the whole cohort at once, at $95,000; or a manager-as-multiplier cascade training each participant's own manager to reinforce the material on the job, at $130,000. She could add any combination of a real business project with sponsor reviews ($55,000), spaced reinforcement nudges over twelve weeks ($25,000), a pair of executive coaches for participants after the program ($60,000), and a peer-practice lab for rehearsing the behavior ($40,000) — and separately decide how many dollars to reserve now for measuring the result later, a figure Catalyst's own planning materials put at $60,000 to $80,000.

Her third decision was how to spend that measurement budget. She could fund all four levels, from a satisfaction survey through measured business results; fund only a satisfaction survey collected at the end of the program; or fund that survey together with a short knowledge quiz. She also had to choose how to separate the program's own effect from everything else happening at Meridian that quarter: a comparison group and trend analysis to separate the program's effect from other causes; attribution of the full measured gain to the program, since it was the most visible cause; or expert estimation of the program's own share of the gain, stated with a confidence range. Two further dials were hers to set: the dollars spent capturing the ninety-day data, and how firmly to state upfront what the data would not claim.

Her fourth decision came four months later, once results were in. She had to decide how to characterize them to the CHRO and a CFO already questioning the numbers: presenting every signal as a confirmed win for the program; leading with the strongest numbers and setting the more contested business-impact result aside; or stating what the data proved, what it suggested, and what it left unsupported. She also had to choose which dollar figure, if any, to put in front of the CFO: a $600,000 gain the team could isolate from Meridian's reorganization, its price change, and its grid-modernization program running the same quarter; no dollar figure at all, paired with the behavior-change evidence and a note that the business-impact result was still pending; or the full $3,000,000 productivity gain Meridian had recorded that quarter, on the reasoning that the timing lined up. And she had to make a recommendation: redesigning the program before renewing, on the grounds that the behavior signal was still too thin to defend; declaring the launch and its satisfaction score a full success and asking Meridian to expand the program; or renewing with one specific design change and a named indicator to track. A last, private dial was how firmly she would defend whichever call she made if the room pushed back.

## Closing

Cole closed the CHRO's letter, opened a blank document titled "Meridian — Program Design," and looked at the ten weeks now counting down on her calendar. Sixty contracted faculty were available to teach nearly anything she asked them to; none of them could tell her which of Meridian's three problems was the one worth $480,000 and a $1.6 million relationship to solve.
