# ESCA Executive Learning: One Flagship, Three Rooms

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1856 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1856 — *Global Cohort* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 April 2026, Camille Aubert read Novalté Group's signed contract a second time. Novalté, an industrial group and ESCA's second-largest client, had paid EUR 1.4 million for the school's acclaimed leadership program, "Leading Across Borders" — but not for the version that had built the program's reputation. Ninety of Novalté's managers would sit in one cohort split simultaneously across three countries: thirty in France, thirty in Germany, thirty in the United Arab Emirates. The design had to be locked by 1 August.

## The Protagonist

Camille Aubert, 45, had directed ESCA's custom-program unit for three years, after seven years running the school's open-enrolment catalogue. She had shepherded "Leading Across Borders" from a single elective into the six-module program corporate clients now asked for by name, and inside ESCA she was known for defending a program's numbers as fiercely as its content. Her bonus was tied to the contribution margin she delivered on the accounts she ran, and to whether those clients renewed. Novalté's account had never before asked her to run the same program in three countries at once.

## The Decision

Over the fifteen weeks before the pilot, Camille faced three connected choices: how much of the flagship to localize versus standardize across France, Germany and the UAE; how to staff and deliver the program given a bench of only two core faculty and one certified affiliate; and how far to intervene if the pilot's first module underperformed. The design had to be locked by 1 August, with the pilot running on 1 October.

## The Company and its Setting

ESCA Executive Learning was the executive-education arm of a French business school, running custom and open-enrolment programs against a company-wide contribution-margin target of 22 percent on roughly EUR 9.6 million of annual revenue. Its reputation rested on little besides "Leading Across Borders," delivered identically to French corporate cohorts for years, to consistent acclaim. It had affiliated faculty elsewhere in Europe, but had never delivered the identical flagship in three national markets at once.

## The Situation

Novalté had guaranteed 75 of the 90 seats; the remaining 15 depended on how the program's first module landed. That module carried a contractual bar: satisfaction of at least 4.2 out of 5.0, in each of the three markets separately, not on average. Miss it in even one and Novalté's contract allowed it to withhold 10 percent of the fee — EUR 140,000 — and to walk from the renewal that mattered more than the fee itself. Against that fee, ESCA still had to clear its 22 percent margin floor: EUR 308,000 that every euro spent on translation, adaptation or new faculty would eat into (Exhibit 1).

The cultural terrain was not symmetric. ESCA's own diagnostics, run in advance, suggested Germany's cohort would expect more structure and evidence than the French design supplied, and that the UAE cohort would expect more relationship-building and hierarchy-sensitivity — including in a role-play built around direct feedback that had always worked in a French classroom. If Camille changed nothing and delivered the French program unchanged in all three rooms, ESCA would spend almost none of its EUR 308,000 cushion — and would be betting the entire contract that a program calibrated for one culture would clear 4.2 in two others it had never been tested against.

## What Camille Controlled

She would start by deciding, market by market, what was durable and what depended on culture. One option standardized everything and delivered the French design unchanged, trusting that a proven program would travel as it was. A second localized the frameworks while keeping the original French cases and role-plays intact. A third kept the six modules' objectives and frameworks standard everywhere, localizing only the cases, role-plays, feedback exercises, language and pacing that culture touched. A fourth rebuilt each market's program from the ground up, assuming little of the existing design would transfer. Alongside that split she had to decide how to treat the three markets: build one adapted "international" version meant to work reasonably well across all three; assume that whatever energized the French cohort would energize any cohort; or design each on its own terms, since Germany and the UAE seemed to want opposite things. She also had to name the single highest cross-cultural risk to the 4.2 bar: the discussion-led style of the German modules, against a cohort wanting more structure and evidence; language alone, assuming a clean translation of the slides addressed the risk; the direct-feedback role-play in the UAE, where hierarchy and relationship-building mattered more than in France; or no real risk at all, since a flagship this proven needed no reassessment. How rigorously she pressure-tested that diagnosis was itself a dial to turn up or down.

Once diagnosed, she chose, per market, the delivery mode and adaptation depth. Delivery could be a blended mix of in-person and virtual sessions at moderate cost; fully virtual and synchronous, which protected the margin but capped relationship-building; or in-person, which lifted engagement most but added venue and travel cost. Layered on top was a cultural-adaptation dial, priced in three steps: light, for minor edits only, at EUR 10,000; moderate, for updated cases and examples, at EUR 35,000; or deep, for new cases and new guest speakers, at EUR 70,000 (Exhibit 2). Six choices in all, drawn against the same EUR 308,000 pool.

Then came faculty, and the constraint was people, not money: ESCA had only two core faculty who could deliver "Leading Across Borders" in French, one German-fluent affiliate certified on the program, and no standing faculty in the UAE. Per market she could fly a core faculty member in alone at roughly EUR 4,800 a trip, accepting the drain on her bench; recruit a local adjunct who had never taught the flagship, cheaper but untested against the 4.2 bar; or pair a flown-in core faculty member with a local co-teacher, closer to the original fit but the highest cost of the three. In Germany alone a fourth path existed: the certified affiliate could teach alone, at no trip cost. The same two core faculty could not cover all three markets in the same week. She also had to decide how much formal machinery would keep the program recognizable under different teachers: certification, shared master materials and direct observation of the pilot; the shared slide deck alone, trusting whoever taught it; or no formal mechanism at all, trusting a good local teacher to improvise. For the UAE, she could build a stronger or weaker contingency against underperformance.

The pilot's first module would run on 1 October, in front of Novalté's learning-and-development head. Whatever the pilot returned, five modules remained for her response. If a market underperformed, she would diagnose why: the direct-feedback exercise misfiring against sensitivities already flagged; a purely linguistic problem that a clean translation pass would resolve; or a cohort that graded harder than the others, needing no design change; and decide how far to intervene: swap the exercise for a relationship-led alternative and add a certified local co-teacher, at EUR 38,000; rebuild that market's program and fly both core faculty out to deliver it, at EUR 120,000; apply a lighter touch of coaching and edited slides, assuming no structural fix was needed, at EUR 6,000; or change nothing, hoping the remaining modules recovered the average on their own, at no additional cost (Exhibit 2). Finally, she had to decide how to present the design to Novalté's L&D head: as a deliberately selective effort, the core held, the surface localized per market, the margin defended, any fix costed; as a program that protected its economics by standardizing wherever possible, since a flat fee for a proven flagship should not fund open-ended customization; or as a program rebuilt for quality everywhere, since a client this important was worth defending on relevance even if it reopened the margin question later; and how much conviction to defend the result as both culturally and economically sound.

## Closing

At her desk on 14 April, Camille drew two columns on a blank page — one for what would travel unchanged across France, Germany and the UAE, one for what would not — and started, market by market, to fill them in. The design was due in fifteen weeks. Novalté's contract did not care which column she got wrong.
