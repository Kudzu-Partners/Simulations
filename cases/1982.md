# Cape Management Development Centre: The Fifty-Point Gap

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1982 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1982 — *Develop the Managers* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Thursday, February 12, 2026, Nomvula Sithole read the two numbers on the cover sheet of Aurora Group's independent evaluation and recognised both of them as her own. Eighty-eight per cent of the managers Aurora had sponsored through the Cape Management Development Centre's flagship diploma had completed it. Thirty-eight per cent of them had a line manager willing to confirm that they now managed differently. The evaluators had set the two figures one above the other and added no commentary.

Riaan de Villiers, Aurora's chief human resources officer, had attached a one-page letter. Aurora would fund one further cohort of the Postgraduate Diploma in Management Practice — nine months, intake in March — and would settle the renewal on evidence of changed management capability in its own people. Without that evidence the contract would move to a provider selling an embedded, work-based alternative. Sithole, the diploma's academic coordinator, had that single cycle, and four points inside it at which the design would be fixed.

## The Protagonist

Nomvula Sithole, 46, had run stores and depots for a Western Cape retail group for nine years before joining CMDC in 2014 as a faculty convenor, and had taken over coordination of the PGDip in 2019. She held the curriculum, the assessment regulations, the faculty roster and the cohort calendar. She held nothing that happened after a manager shut a laptop on Tuesday evening and went back to work on Wednesday morning.

## The Decision

Sithole had to decide how the diploma would build management capability, and how that capability would be evidenced to the organisation paying for it. The decision arrived in four instalments rather than as a single document: a diagnosis and a starting point in month zero; the division of learner time and the standing of classroom against workplace in the first quarter; the order of the nine months and the parties inside the design; and, in month nine, the measurement and the proposal she would put in front of de Villiers (see **Exhibit 2**). Each instalment closed before the next opened, and a cohort had already been recruited.

## The Company and its Setting

CMDC was the management-development arm of a Cape Town business school and had run programmes for working managers since 2005. Annual programme revenue was R95 million (South African rand). The portfolio ran from short certificates up to the PGDip, and served both open-enrolment learners and corporate clients who put cohorts of their own managers through it. Every learner held a full-time job. CMDC's own admissions material told them to expect about six hours of study a week.

Aurora Group, a South African logistics and retail conglomerate, sponsored 120 managers a year and paid R18 million for them — roughly 19% of CMDC's programme revenue, and its largest single contract. Other corporate sponsors bought on the same reasoning, and in a market this size from the same short list of references.

## The Situation

Aurora's evaluators had not disputed that CMDC taught well. Learner satisfaction was high and the pass rate was 88%. What they reported was that when they went back to the sponsored managers' own bosses, 38% of those bosses could point to something the manager now did differently — fifty percentage points below the pass rate (see **Exhibit 1**). CMDC's internal sponsor-confidence rating, a 0-to-10 measure maintained by its client team, had Aurora at 4.6.

Two features of the existing design were not in dispute. About 70% of scheduled learner time was classroom and 30% was workplace. And the draft calendar for the March intake assumed nine hours a week of learner time, against the six the managers said they had.

If the design stayed as it was, the arithmetic was not dramatic. The March cohort would complete at about 88%. Aurora would re-measure with the same instrument and get the same answer. R18 million would leave with the contract at the end of the cycle and, with other sponsors reading the departure of the flagship reference client, CMDC's finance director put the total exposure above R30 million.

## What Sithole Controlled

At the first point she settled where the redesign began: refining the modules CMDC already taught; adding two further academic modules to the diploma; or beginning from Aurora's evaluation, mapping what the sponsored managers could not yet do at work, and building the programme back from there. She recorded a root cause for the fifty-point gap — that the managers were not yet capable enough and the academic standard had to rise, that they were not applying themselves and assessment had to be stricter, or that the programme, the classroom and the workplace were not connected to one another. She fixed the frame she used to describe capability itself: recall of the material taught, a standard checklist of management competencies, or adaptive judgement tied to live workplace outcomes. And she set the share of the identified gaps she tied to a specific, observable on-the-job outcome.

At the second point she divided the learner-time budget. The split between scheduled classroom time and structured workplace time could stay at 70/30, move to 80/20, move to about 50/50, or move to 90% workplace with the classroom all but removed. She set the relationship between the two halves: parallel streams sharing one calendar; all the teaching first and the application afterwards; or each concept applied immediately to a live workplace problem. She decided whether coaching, peer groups and structured reflection were left out, offered as options, or built into the pathway as a modality in their own right. And she committed the total hours a week the design assumed, anywhere between three and twelve.

At the third point she ordered the nine months: all the teaching front-loaded with assessment at the end; free-standing monthly blocks with no carry-over; or repeated cycles of teaching and application. She placed the learner's line manager outside the design, on the syllabus distribution list, or inside it as sponsor of an agreed application project. She set what a learner had to produce to pass each stage gate — a written examination, attendance and participation, or a workplace deliverable — and the number of formal learner–manager touchpoints across the cycle: none, two, or four.

At the fourth point she chose what to measure and report: completion and satisfaction; learner self-ratings of confidence; or transfer measured directly. She decided what happened to the pass rate while she pursued transfer — let it fall, raise the examination standard so that fewer passed, or make the workplace deliverables themselves the assessment. She chose what the renewal pitch led with: CMDC's academic standing, a lower price, or the projected capability uplift. And she stated, in writing to a CHRO who would quote it back to her, how firmly she held the position she had taken.

Four numbers followed the redesign from one point to the next: the transfer-to-job rate, meaning the share of sponsored managers whose own bosses confirmed changed behaviour; the pass rate; Aurora's confidence rating out of ten; and the learner hours a week the design consumed against the six available.

## Closing

That evening Sithole wrote the two numbers at the top of a clean page — 88 and 38 — and under them the date of the March intake. De Villiers had asked for one thing before the cohort started: the shape of the programme Aurora would be buying. She had nine months, and six hours a week of somebody else's time.
