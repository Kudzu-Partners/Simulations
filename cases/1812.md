# Grupo Alimentos Bárcena: Creating Value the Family Could Approve

On Tuesday, 9 June 2026, Marisol Elizondo Vega, chief financial officer of Grupo Alimentos Bárcena, S.A. de C.V., took three proposals off her desk and realized none could wait for the other two to resolve first: a U.S. retailer's offer of a listing across 1,400 stores, a new production line ready to build in Querétaro, and an automation upgrade for the company's original Guadalajara plant. Whatever combination she chose, the company could deploy only MX$120 million of cash today against options costing MX$90–240 million, and the Family Council that governed Grupo Bárcena would vote on her plan in eighteen days, on 27 June.

She would have to build that recommendation in stages: establish what actually limited the firm before evaluating anything else, choose one investment from the three on offer, design a financing structure the Council's own rules would allow, and then win a vote from an ownership group that did not agree among itself about what mattered most.

## The Protagonist

Elizondo, 45, had been CFO of Grupo Alimentos Bárcena since 2022, after four years as Treasury Director and eleven years before that in Mexico City corporate banking. She held no shares in the family holding and was not related to the Bárcena family; her mandate, set by the CEO — Don Esteban Bárcena's son and the senior operating voice of the second generation — was to grow the firm's value without breaking the compact that held its owners together. Nothing in four years in the finance seat had tested that mandate as directly as the three weeks now ahead of her.

## The Decision

Elizondo's task fell into four parts. First, she had to state, in a one-page baseline for the Council, what fact actually bounded the case: a cash problem sitting under a healthy income statement, an EBITDA problem separate from the cash position, a race to secure the U.S. listing that had to be funded, or a situation with no real constraint on available financing. She also had to say which limit she believed would bind hardest — the leverage covenant and the dividend together, the covenant alone, the dividend alone, or neither — and how candidly her baseline stated the cash position and covenant headroom rather than rounding them off.

Second, she had to recommend exactly one investment to the Council (see **Exhibit 2**), name the primary lever she expected to create value — growth through the U.S. listing, margin through product mix, or asset turn through working capital released — and decide how much of the listing's required volume to fund immediately, knowing a won listing that was under-supplied would trigger a MX$60 million retailer chargeback and a threat of de-listing.

Third, she had to structure the financing. Beyond the MX$120 million of cash already available, three external sources could be mixed in any proportion: a bank term loan priced at the interbank rate plus 3.5 points, which raised leverage; a placement of new shares with a non-family investor, which diluted family ownership; and a sale-and-leaseback of the Guadalajara plant, freeing roughly MX$200 million but giving up outright ownership of the plant. She also had to decide the fate of the MX$180 million dividend paid without interruption for forty-six years — preserve it in full and fund the gap from cash, debt or the leaseback; propose a credible, time-bound reduction after securing the dividend-dependent branch's buy-in; or reduce it below MX$180 million from retained cash without the branch's buy-in — and how heavily to weigh the family's preference for control against the cheapest available capital.

Fourth, for the close, she had to frame the recommendation to the Council: around three-year value created alongside how the plan treated family governance; around the EBITDA added, setting the capital's cost to one side; or around speed, arguing the fastest and cheapest money should decide. She had to answer the dividend-dependent branch's question about its own income — frame any change to the payout as an investment in the future value of its own shares with a path back to MX$180 million, tell the branch plainly that firm value came first, or push the vote through on a simple majority without the branch's support — how explicitly to defend the leverage headroom she had kept in reserve, and how much conviction to put behind the claim that an approvable plan beat one that only looked better on paper.

## The Company and its Setting

Don Esteban Bárcena had founded the company in 1979 in Guadalajara, Jalisco, making premium salsas, moles and ready meals for Mexican supermarket chains and, increasingly, the U.S. Hispanic market from a second plant in Querétaro. By 2026 the 1,150-person company was owned across the second and third generations through a family holding, governed by a Family Council and a shareholders' agreement signed two years earlier. Grupo Bárcena had generated 2025 revenue of MX$4,200 million on EBITDA of MX$588 million, with net debt at 1.2 times EBITDA — comfortable room under the 2.5-times ceiling the agreement allowed. The estimated cost of capital for an investment of this kind was about 13.5% a year in peso terms (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two clauses in the shareholders' agreement mattered most. Any single investment above MX$200 million required the full Council's approval, and one family branch — four cousins with no operating role in the business — depended on the annual dividend for its income, having let it be known it would consider forcing a buyout of its shares, estimated at roughly MX$900 million, if the payout were reduced without its agreement.

## The Situation

Grupo Bárcena entered the decision already creating value, which was why its incremental EVA and plan NPV both stood at zero: each measure tracked the value a new decision added above what the business already earned, and none had yet been made. Two further readings described the firm's position that Tuesday. A liquidity ratio, set at 100, compared the cash and credit lines Elizondo could still call on against near-term claims on the business. A family governance fit score, set at 60 out of 100, reflected how likely the Council was to approve whatever she proposed. All four numbers would move, decision by decision, with everything she chose before the vote.

If Elizondo brought the Council nothing, the calendar would not wait for her: the vote was fixed for 27 June regardless, the MX$120 million cash position would not grow on its own in eighteen days, and a choice among a MX$90, a MX$180 and a MX$240 million option would still have to be made by someone in that room, with or without her numbers behind it.

## What Elizondo Controlled

Across the weeks ahead, Elizondo controlled the diagnosis she put before the Council, the single investment she recommended and how fully she funded it to serve the U.S. opportunity, the mix of debt, outside equity, sale-and-leaseback and dividend policy used to pay for it, and the case she made in the room to close the vote. She would be measured on the incremental EVA and plan NPV her choices created, the liquidity ratio that started at 100, and the family governance fit that started at 60.

## Closing

At nine that morning, Elizondo cleared her calendar through the end of the week and opened a blank page headed with the date of the vote. Eighteen days stood between her and a Council that had never had to choose between its income and its future in quite this combination before. She had three ways to spend the firm's capital, four ways to pay for whichever one she picked, and one afternoon to decide where to start.
