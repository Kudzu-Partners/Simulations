# Petrolia S.A.: Eighteen Days to an Operating Model

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1531 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1531 — *AI in HR: From Pilots to Operating Model* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, April 21, 2026, Patricia Calderón-Mora read the typed page Andrés Vargas-Quesada had handed her the previous afternoon and counted the sentences. There were four. The Audit Committee of Petrolia S.A. had reviewed the human-resources technology spend disclosed for FY2025 and the first quarter of 2026, had counted eleven separate artificial-intelligence (AI) initiatives inside the HR function, and had found no written description of how they fitted together. It asked that the Chief Human Resources Officer present, at the next regular session, a written operating model identifying which decisions were made by people, which by software, and the basis on which the line between them had been drawn. A line below the signature asked that no new pilot be started in the meantime.

The next regular session was eighteen days away, on May 9. Calderón-Mora had cleared the morning. By the end of it she meant to have committed, under her own name, to a disposition for each of the eleven pilots, to an allocation of HR work between software and people across the function, and to a vendor architecture and workforce plan she would defend to five readers who would each open it looking for something different.

## The Protagonist

Calderón-Mora, 51, had been CHRO of Petrolia for nineteen months. She had spent ten years at a consumer-goods group in Mexico, six years running total rewards at an Iberian utility, and two years as Petrolia's senior vice-president of people before Vargas-Quesada promoted her. In the cabinet she argued in the chief financial officer's vocabulary, which was why Mariana Solís-Ramírez took her calls. She had inherited nine of the eleven pilots; two had started on her watch.

## The Decision

Calderón-Mora had to decide which of the eleven pilots continued and in what form; then, for every category of work the function performed, whether it would be done by software with people handling exceptions, done by people with software assisting, or kept with people and closed to software by written policy; and then how vendors, internal engineering and the workforce plan would follow.

## The Company and its Setting

Petrolia S.A., headquartered in San José, Costa Rica, was an integrated energy group with FY2025 revenues of approximately USD 4.8 billion and an EBITDA margin of 17.4%: upstream interests in two Brazilian onshore basins, a midstream pipeline and storage joint venture in Mexico, downstream retail in Costa Rica and Spain, and a green-hydrogen unit in Andalucía. Its 7,000 full-time employees split roughly 2,400 in Costa Rica, 1,950 in Brazil, 1,400 in Mexico and 1,250 in Spain.

The HR function ran to about 110 people: regional business-partner teams in each country and a San José shared-services centre that handled payroll, benefits and first-line employee questions for all four populations. The core people system was Workday, live since 2022; recruiting ran on a mixture of legacy and Workday-native tooling; learning ran on a separate platform.

The four jurisdictions did not share an employment-law regime. Brazil's labour courts reinstated readily; Mexico had reformed its federal labour law in 2024; Spain sat inside a strong works-council framework and inside the European Union's AI regulation; Costa Rica was employer-friendly and closely watched politically. One operating model had to survive in all four.

## The Situation

The eleven pilots had accumulated over fourteen months (see **Exhibit 1**). They totalled USD 8.9 million in direct vendor and build spend; with internal time and integration, USD 11.4 million — the figure Solís-Ramírez carried and the Audit Committee had now seen.

Some of them worked. Onboarding ticket volume was down 47% and new-hire satisfaction up 11 points; time to shortlist was down 38%; recruiter throughput was up 22%; a retention model had flagged 14 of the previous year's 22 regrettable departures in advance.

Some had produced complaints. A benefits assistant in Costa Rica had misanswered a question on retirement-fund vesting, drawing a formal grievance and a written apology. A manager-feedback tool in Brazil had been paused after a works-council objection and stayed paused. The retention model used email-metadata features in Spain that counsel had not cleared. The résumé screener showed an adverse-impact signal in the Mexican plant-operator pipeline that nobody had interpreted. Performance-summary drafts were reaching final review documents word for word, which meant managers had stopped editing them.

Three pilots overlapped — an internal-mobility engine and two talent marketplaces, all recommending moves to the same employees. The Spanish marketplace had 8% of eligible employees logging in twice or more in ninety days; the Costa Rica pilot of 400 had 22%.

One pilot had never been approved. An exit-interview synthesizer had been contracted to a small Costa Rica software firm owned by the brother-in-law of Esteban Quirós-Madrigal, the HR director who sponsored it — a fact Calderón-Mora had learned three days earlier. It had produced four thematic reports the leadership team had read and acted on. No employee had consented to the processing, and the Spanish population sat inside it.

Nothing in the arithmetic forced her hand by May 9. If she wrote nothing, the eleven initiatives would keep running on their existing budgets, the complaints would stay open, the duplicate marketplaces would keep invoicing, the consent gap would stay unclosed, and the request that no new pilot be started would remain a line in a letter with no name against it.

Five people would read it. Vargas-Quesada wanted a story he could tell outside the company without later retracting a number. Rodrigo Ureña-Vargas, the chief operating officer, wanted to know which plant managers would work differently and where software entered his operating rituals. Solís-Ramírez wanted the run-rate saving. Don Eugenio Mora-Salazar, the lead independent director, read one page per topic and asked one question per meeting. Marisol Jiménez-Cordero, of the workers' council at the Costa Rica refinery, wanted to know who a plant operator spoke to when software said no, and how long they waited.

## What Calderón-Mora Controlled

She worked in three sittings.

In the first she gave each of the eleven pilots one of four dispositions: **kill** it and write the memo; **scale** it to further populations or countries; **re-scope** it to a narrower, defined use with the exposure addressed; or **hold** it pending review, unchanged.

In the second she put each of nineteen categories of HR work (see **Exhibit 2**) into one of three buckets. **Agent-led** meant the software did the work end to end, its output was the record, and a person handled flagged exceptions only. **Agent-augmented** meant a person owned the work and the software drafted, summarised or recommended into it. **Human-only** meant software was closed out by written policy, with the reason and the review date on the page.

In the third she committed to three things at once. A vendor posture: consolidate onto the Workday platform and its ecosystem; keep Workday as the system of record with two or three named specialist vendors around it; or buy the best product in each category and integrate internally. An internal-build envelope from eight candidates Federico Solano-Aguilar's data and AI team could build rather than buy: performance-summary drafting with an edited-by-a-human check, a Costa Rica Spanish-language guardrail for benefits questions, onboarding ticket wrappers, calibration analytics, exit-interview synthesis rebuilt with a consent layer, bias and fairness dashboards, an internal-mobility engine, a safety stand-down assistant. And a change plan, built from seven statements she could include or leave out: recruiter population down about 30% over eighteen months; first-line shared services down about 50%; business partners becoming talent strategists at constant headcount; people analytics growing, with the new roles named; a director of AI ethics in HR reporting jointly to her and to Solano-Aguilar; Jiménez-Cordero seeing the draft before the Audit Committee did; a public commitment to start no new pilot before a review in October 2027.

She would be marked on one operating-model score out of 100, built from how disciplined the audit was, how defensible the matrix was, and how coherent the architecture and change plan were. Beside it ran five alignment readings, one per reader, each at 50 that morning and moving with what she wrote.

## Closing

At 9:15 a.m. Calderón-Mora put the Committee's page face down, opened a blank document, and typed the eleven pilot numbers down the left margin. Eighteen days. One page for Don Eugenio.
