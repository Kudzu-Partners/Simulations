# CoEditor.io: Three Kinds of Yes

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1422 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1422 — *Cultural Distance in Negotiation — Same Deal, Three Cultures* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At 06:14 on Monday, April 27, 2026, Elena Arrieta opened her laptop at her kitchen table in Madrid and found an email from Tokyo. Aoki-san, the sponsor of CoEditor.io's deal at Mitsuyama Trust Bank, wrote that the proposal was receiving serious consideration and that the Compliance and IT Security departments had raised some clarifying questions. Below it, in red, CoEditor's Tokyo cultural-intelligence advisor, Yumi, had added one line: she read the email as a polite signal that the deal was at material risk.

By 06:48 two more updates and a message from the chief executive, Andrés Quintero, had arrived. Quintero wanted a fourteen-day plan: a recovery for Mitsuyama, a clear read on a Brazilian customer, and a board memo on whether CoEditor should run one global negotiation playbook for its next thirty international deals or three country-specific ones. Arrieta led the pod that would write it.

## The Protagonist

Arrieta, forty-one, had run enterprise sales in Spain and Latin America before Quintero asked her to build CoEditor's Global Enterprise Negotiation pod six months earlier. The pod was small: Arrieta, the commercial leads on each deal, and advisors who knew each market. She reported to the chief revenue officer and, on this question, directly to Quintero. She had closed deals on three continents, though never one in Japan.

## The Decision

Over fourteen days, Arrieta had to read three signals correctly, design a move for each of the three deals, and then defend a protocol for the next thirty deals to the chief executive, the chief financial officer and the country general managers (see Exhibit 2). Each deal was the same deal. What differed was what "yes" meant.

## The Company and its Setting

CoEditor.io sold a collaborative document-editing platform to enterprises. It had about US$88 million in annual recurring revenue and some 520 employees in San Francisco, Madrid and Bogotá. Quintero had promised the board that international business would reach 30% of bookings by the end of 2027; it stood at 12%. The Mitsuyama deal, in his words, anchored the Asia-Pacific story for the next funding round.

Three enterprise buyers had answered the same request for proposals in February, and all three were in contracting, with decisions expected within four weeks. Each had been offered identical terms: US$2.1 million a year for three years, US$6.3 million in total, a fourteen-month deployment, a 99.95% service level, an optional European data-residency setup and a dedicated customer-success manager (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

**Mitsuyama Trust Bank, Tokyo.** A large Japanese trust and banking group with about 22,000 employees. No single person could sign. The decision moved through a circulating approval document on which department heads stamped their personal seals in turn. After eleven weeks, eight of eleven seals were in; Compliance, IT Security and Risk remained. The department general manager, Tanaka-buchō, orchestrated the consensus from a hierarchical position. Aoki-san, a section manager, was the operational sponsor.

**Hartmann-Schreiber Maschinenbau, Stuttgart.** A fourth-generation, family-owned precision-machinery maker with 1,400 employees and €480 million in revenue. The managing director, Dr. Klaus Hartmann-Schreiber, decided alone; his finance director, Beate Lohmann, co-signed anything above €1 million. At the March kickoff he had asked for no slides with hockey-stick charts, only the service levels, the data-protection specification and the migration plan. Eight weeks of weekly technical sessions followed. On Friday his lawyers returned the master agreement with 78 pages of redlines, most on the data-protection annex and the penalty clauses for missed service levels. His covering note, in German, asked for CoEditor's position by Wednesday and said that, if the parties reached agreement, the contract could be signed on Friday. Manuel Ortega-Vives, the Madrid commercial lead, had forwarded it without comment beyond the translation.

**Grupo Aramburu Varejo, São Paulo.** A family-controlled retail group with US$4.2 billion in revenue across supermarkets, pharmacies and e-commerce. Beatriz Aramburu, director of digital transformation and granddaughter of the founder, was the decision-maker on paper. Her uncle Ricardo, seventy-one, chief executive and patriarch, had no formal role in the deal but came up in every meeting. There had been three meetings, two at the family's restaurant, and no redlines. At 06:32 Catarina Alves, CoEditor's São Paulo commercial lead, reported on dinner the night before. Beatriz had said "vamos fechar isso, podem confiar" (we will close this, you can trust us) twice, with a hand on Alves's arm, and Ricardo had joined for the last hour. Alves read it as a verbal yes and asked whether to push for paper that week or let it breathe.

Above the three deals sat the board question. The chief financial officer wanted one global playbook, on cost. The country general managers in Tokyo, Frankfurt and São Paulo wanted three, on fidelity. Quintero wrote that he was torn. If nothing changed, the pod would keep treating the three deals as one, with the same deck, the same demo and the same follow-up rhythm, and Quintero would face the board with no protocol at all.

## What Arrieta Controlled

In the first phase, three **readings**, one per deal. Aoki-san's email: routine progress, a polite signal of material risk calling for a recovery move, or a demand for technical answers by tomorrow. The 78 pages of redlines: resistance, engagement from a counterparty preparing to close, or a stalling tactic to be met with a threat to walk. The warm dinner: a confirmed yes to convert into a signature within 48 hours, a polite holding pattern while the team moved on, or a probable yes that still needed verifying.

In the second phase, three **moves**. At Mitsuyama: a 40-page contractual response answering each Compliance concern; a trip to Tokyo by the chief revenue officer, Arrieta and Yumi for a listening session with no slides and a two-week response window; or a 20% cut to the year-one price. At Hartmann-Schreiber, by Wednesday: a placeholder on each redline to keep options open; a clause-by-clause response in German, accepting some redlines, countering others and declining the rest with reasons; or a hard push-back calling the redlines excessive and threatening to walk. At Aramburu: sending the full master agreement for Beatriz's lawyers to redline by Friday; a long lunch, a ninety-minute working session and Quintero himself in São Paulo for the close; or waiting for Beatriz to make the next move.

In the third phase, three **defences**. To Quintero: one global playbook at almost no incremental cost; three country playbooks at about US$720,000 a year; or one global protocol with mandatory country annexes at about US$240,000 a year. To the chief financial officer, who had red-penned the retainers for cultural-intelligence advisors: cut them and rely on the country managers; defend them on the grounds that a manager under deal pressure could not also be a detached cultural auditor; or keep them for the top three markets only. To the country managers, who asked whether this was a partnership or a Madrid script: Madrid imposes the protocol; annexes co-authored by each country manager and advisor, with the chief revenue officer owning the protocol and the country managers holding a veto on annex content; or three independent regional teams with no central protocol.

The pod tracked seven numbers. Its own **survival estimate** for each deal stood at 25% for Mitsuyama, 80% for Hartmann-Schreiber and 70% for Aramburu. **Cultural fidelity**, how accurately the pod was reading each counterparty, stood at 50 out of 100. Quintero's **confidence** in the recommendation stood at 50, and the chief financial officer's **cost score** at 50. **Protocol coverage**, the share of the thirty-deal pipeline the recommended protocol could handle, stood at 30. The board memo would be judged on all of them together.

## Closing

At 06:55 Arrieta blocked two hours on her calendar and opened a blank document titled "Fourteen days." The German deadline was Wednesday. The Tokyo seals were still three short.
