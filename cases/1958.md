# Aurelia's Entrepreneurship Office: One Budget, Two Targets

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1958 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1958 — *Launchpad LatAm* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, May 12, 2026, Mariana Quiroga read the Presidential Directive twice before she called her directors in. Two pages. It ordered the National Entrepreneurship & Innovation Office (ONEI) to deliver a National Entrepreneurship Plan that raised two things at once in the Republic of Aurelia: the rate at which new firms were formed, and the share of new ventures whose founders meant to grow them. It fixed the money at AUP 1,800 million — the office's existing discretionary program budget — forbade new borrowing to top it up, and set the submission date at June 30, 2026.

That left seven weeks. Quiroga, ONEI's Director-General, had to decide whom the Plan was for, how to divide the AUP 1,800 million across the six spending channels her office ran, which single regulatory reform to attach to it, and what to say when the cabinet pushed back. A Plan arriving after June 30 missed the fiscal cycle, and a missed cycle forfeited the whole AUP 1,800 million for the year.

## The Protagonist

Mariana Quiroga, 44, had run ONEI for nineteen months. She came to it from the Finance Ministry's budget office, where she had spent six years costing other people's programs and refusing most of them. She had never founded a company and did not pretend otherwise; what she had was a record of plans that survived a finance ministry. Two of the four directors around her table had applied for her job.

## The Decision

Quiroga had to settle four questions in sequence, and each answer closed off options in the next: what kind of entrepreneurship Aurelia had; whom the Plan should aim at; what the money should buy and which reform should ride with it; and how the package should be argued in cabinet. The Directive named the two numbers the Plan would be judged on. It did not say how to move them, or which mattered more.

## The Company and its Setting

ONEI was a small agency with a large cheque. It made grants, bought training, guaranteed loans and drafted entrepreneurship legislation for the Presidency. Its discretionary program budget of AUP 1,800 million was the largest single instrument the Aurelian state pointed at business formation, and it spent that budget through six standing channels (see **Exhibit 2**).

Aurelia was a middle-income Latin American economy of 34 million people with gross domestic product (GDP) per capita of about AUP 210,000: small firms, a large informal sector, exports concentrated in a few commodity and assembly industries. Two of the last three administrations had promised an entrepreneurship strategy; neither submitted one inside a fiscal cycle.

## The Situation

ONEI's indicators had barely moved in four years (see **Exhibit 1**). Early-stage entrepreneurial activity — TEA, the share of working-age adults starting or newly running a business — stood at 14%. The share of those ventures whose founders said they intended significant job creation stood at 8%. Of the firms formed three years earlier, 42% were still trading. Only 31% of Aurelia's businesses were on the state register at all. Youth unemployment was 19%.

Under the headline rate sat a figure the office had published for years without building a program around it: about 62% of early-stage activity was necessity-driven, started because there was no job rather than because someone had spotted an opening.

The directors read the same page four ways. Teodoro Salcedo, the innovation lead, read Aurelia as two economies — a thin ambitious layer and a vast survival base — responding to different instruments. Rosa Belmonte, the microenterprise lead, read the split as overdrawn: firms moved between the categories, and lifting activity across the board would lift the ambitious end with it. The chief economist read the level rather than the mix as the problem, and wanted the headline rate pushed up. The Reform Counsel read necessity microbusiness as a symptom of unemployment, not a source of growth, and wanted the money behind firms that could scale.

They disagreed as sharply about what held the ambitious firms back: Salcedo, growth finance and exit — firms past a first product could not raise a second round and had nowhere to sell; Belmonte, the cost and time of registering, which kept firms small and invisible; the chief economist, management skill, and founders who could not read their own accounts; the Reform Counsel, the size of the domestic market, which no ministry could change.

Three voices outside the office wanted different things. Finance Minister Elías Bonilla had written to say that AUP 1,800 million was the number, that there was no supplementary budget and no borrowing authority, and that he would read the costing before the strategy. Deputies from the industrial provinces wanted visible new firms and jobs inside the year, and had begun calling accelerator funding money for the capital's technology elite. A regional development bank co-financing two ONEI programs wanted to know where Aurelia's scalable, exporting firms would come from.

The Plan would be marked on five things. Two were the Directive's: the formation rate, then 14%, and the share of ventures intending to grow, then 8%. A third was survival — the 42% still trading after three years. The review board added two of its own, each scored out of a hundred and each sitting at 50 for a plan not yet written: how much outcome each peso bought, and whether the cabinet would sign the thing at all. If ONEI submitted nothing, or submitted late, none of the five moved: formation stayed near 14%, the growth-intending share near 8%, survival near 42%, the register kept covering under a third of the country's businesses, and the AUP 1,800 million returned to the Treasury unspent.

## What Quiroga Controlled

Quiroga wrote the Plan, and the Plan was the decision. She had to state in it which reading of Aurelia's entrepreneurship the office was acting on, name the constraint she believed held back the firms the Directive wanted more of, and choose how much of the document to spend separating one kind of entrepreneur from another rather than presenting a single national picture.

She then had to set a posture: concentrate the Plan on ventures with growth ambitions, spread it across necessity microbusiness, write a stated split between the two ends, or fund everything at a similar level as a general lift in entrepreneurship. If she wrote a split, she had to say what share of the money went to each end, and name which of the Directive's two numbers the posture was built to move — the formation rate, the growth-intending share, both by design, or neither, in which case the Plan carried no stated target.

Then the money. She set an amount for each of the six standing channels and chose exactly one of four reforms to carry into the legislature (see **Exhibit 2**). The reform's cost came out of the same envelope: every peso of reform was a peso not spent through a channel, the two together had to close at or under AUP 1,800 million, and there was nothing to borrow against.

Last, the defence — four minutes in front of the cabinet. She could build it on both of the Directive's numbers, on jobs and inclusion, on productivity and exports, or on the ambition of the Plan, with the costing held back. Bonilla would press on the envelope, and she could answer with a costed line-by-line, a statement that the Plan ran slightly over, a request for a supplementary budget or a loan, or a commitment to settle the figures later. The development bank would ask where the scalable firms came from: named instruments, a general rise in activity, a later year, or the private sector. And she had to decide how hard to hold her line when all three pushed at once.

## Closing

At seven that evening Quiroga wrote two numbers on the whiteboard in her office — 14% and 8% — and under them June 30 and AUP 1,800 million. The Directive wanted both up. Her directors had given her four readings of the same country and four candidates for what held it back. She had seven weeks, one envelope, one reform, and no draft.

**Exhibit 2. The instruments available to the Plan**

| Instrument | What it provides | Fiscal cost |
|---|---|---|
| Seed grants and matching capital | Cash matched against private money a venture has already raised | Set by the Plan |
| Incubators and accelerators | Places on cohort programs providing mentoring, workspace and a stipend | Set by the Plan |
| Access-to-finance guarantees | A state guarantee covering part of a lender's loss on loans made without collateral | Set by the Plan |
| Entrepreneurship training | Short courses in bookkeeping, pricing and selling, delivered through municipal offices | Set by the Plan |
| Formalisation and registration support | Fee waivers, filing assistance and a grace period on compliance costs for firms entering the register | Set by the Plan |
| Research and development vouchers | Vouchers a firm redeems against university and laboratory services | Set by the Plan |
| One-day digital business registration | Registration collapsed to a single online step completed within a day | AUP 80 million |
| Simplified-tax regime for microbusinesses | One low rate replacing the standard filing burden below a turnover threshold | AUP 120 million |
| Insolvency and fresh-start reform | Discharge timelines that let the founder of a failed firm trade again | AUP 180 million |
| Startup equity and talent-visa regime | Standard equity instruments, share options taxed on realisation, and a fast visa for specialist hires | AUP 260 million |

*Note: the first six instruments are ONEI's standing spending channels and the Plan sets each amount. The last four are the candidate regulatory reforms, of which the Plan may advance exactly one; its cost is charged against the same AUP 1,800 million envelope.*
