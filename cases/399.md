# GAIBSA: The Arm's-Length Question

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-399 · **Companion simulation:** Express 399 — *El Impuesto: International Tax Planning & Transfer Pricing* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, February 3, 2026, a registered letter from Spain's tax agency, the Agencia Estatal de Administración Tributaria (AEAT), reached the Madrid headquarters of GAIBSA. It opened a transfer-pricing inspection of the group's 2021–2023 tax years. The inspector questioned two things: the profit the group's Moroccan factory kept, and the royalty its Dutch subsidiary collected on every sale in Germany and France. The estimated exposure was €4.8 million, plus interest and a possible penalty of 15–20%.

Inés Valcárcel Ruiz, GAIBSA's Group Tax Director, had until the first working meeting with the inspector, on Friday, February 13, to put the group's own analysis on the table: the margin she believed the Moroccan plant should earn, and how the group would defend the Dutch royalty. The rest of the year would bring a negotiation, a decision on where the group's patents should sit, and a tax policy that the board and the group's largest customers could read.

## The Protagonist

Valcárcel was forty-four. She had joined GAIBSA in 2022 from an advisory firm, where she had spent eleven years preparing transfer-pricing files for industrial clients. She reported to the Chief Financial Officer, Tomás Orbegozo Lasa, who had signed off the Dutch structure in 2017. The two of them would take every decision of the year to the board together. Valcárcel had defended files before. She had never negotiated one in which her own employer's structure was the subject.

## The Decision

The inspection would not close in one move. Valcárcel faced four sets of decisions across the year, each taken with what the previous one had produced. The first was due in ten days: an arm's-length margin for the Moroccan plant that the group could defend, and a strategy for the royalty. Every later choice, whether to fight or settle, where to move the patents and how much to disclose, would be made against whatever her first analysis put on record.

## The Company and its Setting

GAIBSA (Grupo Auxiliar Ibérico) was a mid-sized Spanish multinational that made precision components for the automotive and aerospace industries. Consolidated revenue was about €184 million and consolidated EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes) €22.0 million. The parent in Madrid held the group, ran research and development (R&D) and set strategy. A plant of 820 employees in Morocco made the components. Two commercial subsidiaries, in Germany and France, sold them. A fifth company, GAIBSA Netherlands B.V., held the group's registered patents (see Exhibit 1).

Money moved among them on three internal prices. The distributors bought components from the Moroccan plant at production cost plus 8%. They paid GAIBSA Netherlands a royalty of 6% of their local sales for the use of the patents. The parent charged every subsidiary for management services at cost plus 5%.

Most customers were German and French carmakers. In late 2025 the largest of them had added a request to their supply-chain audits: a written statement of responsible tax policy from each major supplier. With revenue far below the €750 million threshold, GAIBSA had no legal duty to prepare a country-by-country report of its profits, taxes and employees by jurisdiction. Nobody had asked it for one either.

## The Situation

The inspector's argument on Morocco was simple. The plant worked to the parent's instructions and carried limited inventory risk; in the AEAT's reading it was a low-risk contract manufacturer. Comparable independent manufacturers earned 4–8% on cost. The policy said cost plus 8%, but the Moroccan company had reported EBIT of €14.8 million on roughly €120 million of intragroup revenue, about 12%. Every point of margin taken away from Morocco was profit the AEAT would tax in Spain at 25%. GAIBSA's finance team read the plant differently: it bore real production risk, its managers had local autonomy, and its lower labour cost was a competitive advantage that Spain had no claim to tax.

On the Netherlands, the argument was shorter. GAIBSA Netherlands B.V. had two employees and collected 6% of the distributors' sales. Under the international guidelines, a royalty follows the people who develop, maintain, protect and exploit a patent, and two people could not do all of that. The Dutch government was also tightening the substance rules of its Innovation Box, the regime that taxed the B.V.'s royalty income at 9%. Moving the patents out would trigger a Dutch exit tax on the gap between their market value, estimated at €28 million, and their tax basis of €4.2 million.

When the letter arrived, the group's effective tax rate stood at 18.4% and its cash reserves at €50.0 million. The tax team kept two further gauges for the board. One was a fiscal-reputation index, on a scale of 0 to 100, built from how investors, customers and the press read the group's tax conduct; it stood at 50. The other was an audit-risk reading, the team's estimate of the likelihood of further inspection; it stood at 80% (see Exhibit 2).

If GAIBSA defended the structure as it stood, nothing in that picture moved. The €4.8 million claim, the interest and the penalty stayed open. The Dutch company faced a substance review with two employees. The audit-risk reading stayed at 80%, and the customers' request went unanswered.

## What Valcárcel Controlled

For the first meeting she would settle two things. The **arm's-length margin** she proposed for the Moroccan plant, as a percentage on cost, anywhere from 4% to 14%. And the **royalty defence**: build real substance in the Netherlands by hiring fifteen or more staff there; cut the royalty from 6% to 3%; or end the royalty arrangement entirely.

Once the inspector came to the table, she would choose the **route to resolution**. She could litigate the full claim before the Tribunal Económico-Administrativo Central and then the Audiencia Nacional, a path of five to eight years. She could agree a negotiated adjustment in an *acta con acuerdo*, the AEAT's settlement form for a voluntary regularisation. Or she could combine a settlement of the past years with a request for an advance pricing agreement (APA) that would fix the method for the years ahead. She would set the **Moroccan margin she offered** in the negotiation, between 5% and 8%, and the **royalty rate she offered**, between 0% and 6%. And she would decide whether to open a **mutual agreement procedure (MAP)** with Morocco, so that profit taxed again in Spain would not also stay taxed in Morocco.

Then came the **patents**. She would choose where the group's intellectual property would sit: in Spain under the Patent Box, at an effective rate near 10% and with real R&D required in Spain; in Ireland under its Knowledge Development Box, at 6.25%; in a strengthened Netherlands, keeping the 9% regime and avoiding the exit tax; or in the Spanish parent at the standard 25%, with no special regime. With that choice came the **number of researchers to hire** in the destination, from none to thirty, at roughly €80,000 each a year.

Last came the **policy**. Whether to publish a country-by-country report voluntarily, prepare one for internal use only, or decline to prepare one. Whether to publish a full tax-transparency report, keep an internal policy document, or issue no formal statement. And the **stance** she would recommend to a board that had already asked whether the group was paying too much tax: conservative, putting compliance and reputation first; balanced, optimising within clear legal bounds; or aggressive, maximising savings and accepting more risk.

After each stage the board would see that stage's tax impact in euros and the change in the reputation index, next to the six figures it already watched: group EBIT, tax exposure, effective tax rate, fiscal reputation, audit risk and cash. At year-end it would judge the work on four things together: the effective tax rate the group carried forward, its fiscal reputation, its audit risk, and the total cost of getting there.

## Closing

On the evening of February 3, Valcárcel left the inspector's letter open on her desk beside the Moroccan company's 2023 documentation file. Cost plus 8% was printed on page four; 12% was in the accounts. Orbegozo wanted a number by Monday. The meeting with the inspector was ten days away.
