# Hellwig Antriebstechnik: The Nine-Day Sign-Off

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1875 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1875 — *Rationality Check* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 12 March 2026, Nora Vogt, Head of Controlling at Hellwig Antriebstechnik GmbH, received a dossier and an instruction. The dossier was a €24 million proposal to fully automate the assembly line at Plant 2, submitted by the company's chief operating officer, claiming a payback of 3.1 years and a positive net present value of €5.2 million. The instruction came from the new chief financial officer, who called Vogt in specifically to hand it to her: find out whether the number was real. The supervisory board would vote in nine working days.

The timing was not neutral. Recent project overruns, the CFO said, made it look as though sign-offs of this size had become a formality — a stamp applied on the way to a decision already made elsewhere. This proposal, the CFO told her plainly, was a test of whether that was still true.

## The Protagonist

Nora Vogt, 44, had headed controlling at Hellwig Antriebstechnik for six years, promoted from the plant-controller post she had held at the Bielefeld site since 2015. Trained as an auditor, she now ran a department that functioned, in the founding family's own description, as the internal check on management's numbers before the supervisory board saw them: a formal sign-off, known inside Hellwig by its German name, the Wirtschaftlichkeitsnachweis. Vogt's standing rose or fell with the board's confidence that a signature from her department meant a number had survived scrutiny, not merely arithmetic — the confidence the new CFO had just placed in front of her, alongside the €24 million.

## The Decision

Vogt's task unfolded in stages. She first had to decide how to approach the dossier: catalogue everything in it where the reasoning looked shaped to reach a favorable answer, before recalculating anything; rebuild the net-present-value model on her own assumptions; or accept the COO's framing and check a handful of figures later. She then had to decide which single distortion did the most damage if it went unchallenged: a volume-growth assumption of 6% a year against an order book and five years of actuals showing 1.5%; a savings figure of €7.6 million a year, sourced from the vendor's own sales materials; the €1.4 million already spent on a feasibility study and pilot cell, offered as a reason to keep going; or the possibility that none of it amounted to a material problem and the model held together. However she named it, she had to decide how far to go tying it to a specific line of the spreadsheet, from a light annotation to a full citation.

The second stage was what to do with each figure. The 6% growth line could become a range — base case 1.5%, upside 3% — tested for sensitivity; a flat 3% compromise; or stay at 6%, trusting the COO knew the order book better than controlling did. The €1.4 million already spent could be stripped out entirely, since it was gone either way; partly carried forward, since some of the work still had value; or left in, since abandoning it would waste what had been committed. The vendor's €7.6 million savings estimate could be sent for an independent check, at €40,000 and three of the nine days; discounted on her own judgment to near €4.9 million with no outside opinion; or simply accepted to preserve the clock. And instead of the vendor's assumption of full savings from month one, she could set a ramp-up from zero months to fourteen, against benchmarks putting comparable installations at nine to fourteen months to full run-rate (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Hellwig Antriebstechnik GmbH was a family-owned maker of electric drive systems headquartered in Bielefeld, North Rhine-Westphalia — a privately held, engineering-driven company of the kind Germans call the Mittelstand. It ran three plants, employed 2,300 people, and had closed the 2025 financial year on revenue of €540 million and an EBIT margin of 7.8%, a little over €42 million (see Exhibit 1). Controlling's sign-off on capital proposals of this size was not meant to be a formality: the founding family had built the department to be the point where a manager's numbers met an independent check before the board saw them — exactly the design the CFO's comment about rubber stamps had called into question.

## The Situation

The proposal was straightforward on its face: a robotic cell and a fleet of automated guided vehicles to fully automate the Plant 2 line, for €24 million, paying back in 3.1 years and adding €5.2 million of net present value on the COO's numbers. The board would vote in nine working days. Internally, controlling kept a running scorecard the CFO wanted updated at every stage: how rigorously the team demonstrated its reasoning, how close its eventual recommendation came to one the board could defend under later scrutiny, and how much standing controlling itself still had with the CFO and the board. Going in, all three readings were unflattering — standing at 40 out of 100, demonstrated reasoning at 20, the recommendation itself barely begun at 15. Controlling also had a separate allowance, up to €120,000, to spend commissioning outside checks on any figure it did not trust, and a clock: nine working days, none of them spent yet.

If the board voted the proposal through exactly as submitted, Hellwig would commit €24 million against a growth rate four times faster than its own order book had run for five years, a savings estimate written by the company selling the equipment, and an assumption that the full saving arrived from month one. Should any of those assumptions prove too optimistic, the resulting write-down would land in the same period Hellwig's lenders tested its leverage against a ceiling of 3.0 times net debt to EBITDA.

## What Vogt Controlled

Three days before the vote, Vogt would sit across from the COO. She had to choose a stance: a conditional yes requiring a smaller, €9 million pilot first, with the rest released as savings milestones were met; a clear no built on corrected numbers; sending the proposal back for redesign on defensible assumptions; or endorsing the original dossier, since further verification would cost more time than the nine-day clock allowed. She also had to decide what to lead with when he pushed back — her own analysis, the CFO's mandate behind the assignment, or his closer knowledge of plant operations — and how to answer the €1.4 million already spent: reject it as irrelevant, acknowledge it as one factor without letting it decide, or concede that finishing what had been started carried real weight. Underneath those choices sat a fourth, continuous one: how hard to protect the working relationship with the COO, on a dial where caring too little risked detonating it and caring too much shaded into capitulation.

Whatever happened in that room, the sign-off was hers to write: a corrected, phased Wirtschaftlichkeitsnachweis built around the €9 million pilot with milestone gates; a formally escalated, documented dissent to the CFO and board instead of a signature; or the original €24 million proposal, signed as submitted. If she chose the phased route, she still had to choose a genuine staged commitment, capital released only as milestones were proven, or an effectively all-or-nothing structure — €24 million now, or nothing. Separately, she had to decide what, if anything, to recommend for every capital sign-off that followed: a template stripping out sunk costs and requiring independent verification and sensitivity testing as routine, an annual after-the-fact review of past approvals, or no new governance, since this proposal might be a one-off test. Last, she had to say, in a number from zero to a hundred, how firmly she stood behind whatever she recommended.

## Closing

At 9:10 that morning, Vogt closed the dossier, pulled a blank sheet toward her, and drew a single line down the middle — one column for what the COO's numbers assumed, one for what she could actually defend. The board would vote in nine working days. She had a €120,000 allowance she had not yet spent, a meeting with the CFO she had not yet had, and an empty page where the second column should be.
