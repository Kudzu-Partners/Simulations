# Sendoa Systems: Mobilizing What We Know

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1898 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1898 — *Mobilizing What We Know* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of 24 February 2026, Itziar Elorriaga watched the last slide of Sendoa Systems' annual strategy review stay on the screen longer than the others. It plotted one line: the share of company revenue earned from products launched in the previous three years. Four years earlier the line stood at 38%. It now stood at 19%. Two slides before it, the board had been told that a flagship control-system contract had gone to a rival that reached the customer with a comparable product first.

The chief executive gave Elorriaga the mandate before the room emptied. She had twelve months and €5.0 million to turn what Sendoa knew into new products again, and to bring that share of revenue back to at least 30%. The board would sit again on the same number in a year.

## The Protagonist

Itziar Elorriaga, 44, had been head of strategy for seven weeks. She had joined Sendoa in 2018 to run the energy product unit after nine years in industrial automation with a German group, and had been promoted into the first firm-wide role of her career in January 2026. She knew the engineering floor: in 2023 her own unit had spent four months rebuilding a sensor-calibration routine that a neighbouring unit had finished the year before, and she had written a post-mortem about it that no one had acted on. The knowledge platform she had inherited responsibility for had been bought by her predecessor.

## The Decision

Elorriaga had to decide where Sendoa's €5.0 million went over the coming year, and what to change about the way engineers were paid and promoted so that the money moved anything at all. Both parts had to be committed inside the twelve months; the board had made clear that it would judge the year on the new-product revenue number, not on the plan.

## The Company and its Setting

Sendoa Systems S.A., founded in Bilbao in 2006, designed industrial control software and connected-sensor hardware for manufacturing and energy customers in Spain, France and Germany. It closed 2025 with €86 million of revenue at a 16% EBITDA margin and employed 540 people, 310 of them engineers and developers, split across four product units (see **Exhibit 1**). For a decade the firm had been the technical reference in its niche, and had held that position on a steady cadence of patented releases.

## The Situation

Three senior architects had retired or left for competitors in the eighteen months to February 2026. Between them they had held the design patterns behind most of Sendoa's platform, and almost none of it was written down; an internal estimate put the cost of re-creating what they knew at €2.1 million. Two more architects were eligible to retire within the next eighteen months. The four product units ran their own backlogs and their own conventions, and engineers were assessed, bonused and promoted on the features their own unit shipped. A knowledge platform bought in 2021 had been rolled out to all 310 engineers and was largely unused.

The board asked for four readings on a standing dashboard. The first, an intellectual-capital index scored 0 to 100, combined what Sendoa's people knew, what was documented and built into its systems, and what sat in its relationships with customers and universities; it opened at 22. The second was innovation output — the same share of revenue from products less than three years old — at 19%, against the board's threshold of 30%. The third, knowledge-flow adoption, also 0 to 100, measured whether people were genuinely reusing each other's work rather than filing documents; it opened at 15. The fourth, tacit-knowledge capture, opened at 10 and tracked how much of the undocumented expertise still inside the building had been secured before its holders left. Against those, €0.0 million of the €5.0 million had been committed.

The management team did not agree on the reading. The engineering lead argued the 2021 platform had been under-resourced and never given curators. The people and culture director said the platform had failed because no engineer had ever been rewarded for putting anything into it. The finance controller wanted the three architect posts refilled at senior level, which recruitment priced at roughly €1.6 million in compensation, and pointed out that this was the only intervention with a known delivery date.

If Elorriaga committed nothing, the arithmetic was already visible. New-product revenue stayed near 19%, the two remaining architects retired with their design patterns undocumented, and the platform stayed idle. The board had said what it would do in that case: stop funding Sendoa to build innovation and start buying it.

## What Elorriaga Controlled

**The diagnosis.** She had to state where the binding gap sat. Four readings had been left on the table: that people and systems were failing together, because the architects had gone and nothing they held had been recorded and the units did not share; that this was a staffing gap alone, closed by rehiring at senior level; that the gap was external, in the thinned co-innovation with lead customers and with the universities in Bilbao and San Sebastián; or that all three were equally weak and should be worked at once. She also had to name the risk she would weight ahead of the others — the undocumented know-how of the two architects eligible to retire, the platform that had sat unused since 2021, the revenue percentage itself pursued through short-term selling, or none of them, treating the year as time to study the problem before committing. And she had to set how far the gap map was ranked on measured impact and urgency rather than settled by seniority in the room, on a scale running from nothing to complete.

**The money.** Five lines were costed (see **Exhibit 2**). She could put anything from nothing up to €2.0 million behind each one, provided the five together stayed inside €5.0 million. She also had to choose the order in which the year unfolded: leading with the capture of at-risk knowledge and building the rest alongside it, leading with the platform relaunch and letting everything else follow, or starting all five together on day one.

**The incentives.** She set the weight that sharing and reuse carried in every engineer's bonus and promotion case, on a scale running from nothing to half. She chose what the firm would actually recognise: documented know-how that other units built on, or the volume of posts, sessions and documents contributed, or nothing new, leaving the reward on features shipped by one's own unit. She decided how much money went to curation roles, protected documentation and mentoring time, and the rewriting of promotion criteria, up to €1.5 million. And she decided whether to cap the sharing weight and condition it on innovation results, or to leave it uncapped.

**The mid-year response.** Half-way through, platform take-up was running behind plan, a senior architect announced early retirement, and €1.0 million of the budget was still uncommitted. Elorriaga could move that money into expert capture before the architect left, put it into the platform, or hold the plan as written. She then had to fix what the board saw: innovation outcomes only, the new-product revenue share against the board's 30% threshold; a mix of outcomes and the activity counts the platform generated; or the activity counts themselves, 1,200 posts and 60 knowledge-café sessions. Finally she chose how to present the year and how hard to press the argument, on a scale from nothing to complete.

## Closing

Elorriaga left the boardroom with the strategy pack under her arm and the four opening readings written on the inside cover. The two architects were on the retirement register. The €5.0 million was hers from 1 March. In twelve months the board would ask for one number, and she had eleven months and three weeks to decide what had to move first.
