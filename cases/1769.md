# Larkfield Care Services: Activating Team Aurora

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1769 · **Companion simulation:** Express — *Activate the Best Self* (sim 1769), 4 rounds, EN/ES · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of Monday, 9 February 2026, Elena Marsh took a call from Larkfield's commercial director and wrote down, word for word, the sentence that ended it: the company's flagship energy client had put Team Aurora on notice, customer-satisfaction scores had sat below the contractual floor for two straight months, and the account had ninety days to recover before the client walked. Marsh had been running Aurora for seven weeks. The client's own language for her sixty advisors was "disengaged" and "robotic."

Finance had already closed off the lever Marsh most wanted to reach for. There would be no pay rise, no bonus pool, and no extra headcount to fix this. Whatever lifted Team Aurora's engagement inside the cure period had to come from how the work was designed and led, not from what it cost. Before she could touch onboarding, the phone system, or a single team meeting, she had one earlier decision to make: what, exactly, had gone wrong.

## The Protagonist

Marsh, 36, had spent eight years at Larkfield, starting as a frontline advisor at the Bradford site before running a twenty-five-person team supporting a home-insurance client for four years. That team's engagement scores had been the highest in the company for two years running, which was the reason Larkfield's operations director had moved her onto Aurora the previous December — three months before the client's warning call, and nine months after Aurora itself had been stood up as the company's newest, and supposedly best, account team. She had inherited a culture, a script, and a set of metrics that had all hardened before she arrived.

## The Decision

Over the ninety days of the cure period, Marsh would face four separate decisions, each closing off some paths and opening others: how to diagnose what had actually suppressed her team's engagement; how to redesign the first week a new advisor spent at Aurora; how much latitude to hand advisors inside their scripted calls; and what kind of recurring feedback conversation to build once the numbers started moving. None of the four came with extra money attached, and each one would show up in the same four numbers the client was already watching.

## The Company and its Setting

Larkfield Care Services ran inbound customer support for energy and insurance clients out of three UK sites, employing 1,350 people, 900 of them frontline advisors, on revenue of roughly £88 million a year at a thin 7% operating margin (see Exhibit 2). Team Aurora — sixty advisors serving the energy client alone — had been built from scratch nine months earlier to be the company's showcase account. Instead it now carried the worst engagement and the highest attrition of any team in the business, and the contract behind it was worth roughly £9 million a year, close to a tenth of Larkfield's total revenue.

## The Situation

Four numbers defined where Marsh was starting from (see Exhibit 1). Aurora's engagement score — a survey-based read on how committed and involved advisors said they felt — stood at 48 out of 100, against a company average of 67. A separate intrinsic-motivation index, tracking how much of advisors' own curiosity and initiative was showing up in their work rather than compliance under supervision, sat at 42 out of 100. Customer-satisfaction and performance, the number the client itself watched, was 61 against a contractual minimum of 65 — already in breach. And voluntary turnover was running at 52% a year, against 34% company-wide, with every advisor who left costing real money and roughly ten weeks to replace at full productivity. If none of these numbers moved before the ninety days ran out, the client's own contract language gave it the right to walk, and Aurora's story inside Larkfield would end where it had nearly ended already.

## What Marsh Controlled

Her first decision was purely diagnostic. Reviewing the same survey results, attrition figures, and exit-interview notes, she had to settle on one account of why engagement had collapsed. She could conclude that advisors were paid below the market rate, and the fix was a bonus and a full pay review; that the job's tight script, its handle-time clock, and its lack of connection to customers had switched off advisors' own drive to work well; that execution had slipped, and the fix was a second quality-assurance listen alongside a stricter adherence target; or that Aurora had hired the wrong people, and the fix was performance-managing the weakest-scoring quartile out of the team.

Three weeks later came the onboarding decision. New advisors joined Aurora every fortnight into a five-day induction of systems training, policy, and script drills, and every minute Marsh added or changed came out of phone time the contract was measuring. She could replace one afternoon of policy drills with a short reflection exercise on each recruit's own strengths and a personal introduction to the rest of the team; give every new starter a welcome voucher, a branded hoodie, and a handwritten welcome card on their first day; leave the induction exactly as it was, on the grounds that it already covered the required systems and policy content efficiently; or pair that same reflection and introduction with a half-day spent shadowing real stories of customers Aurora had helped.

By week six the job itself was on the table. Advisors were still working from a locked script, measured against adherence and average-handle-time targets the client contract specified directly, and Marsh had to choose one way to widen their room to manoeuvre. She could change nothing structural, and simply ask advisors to sound more enthusiastic and engaged on every call they took; she could let advisors personalise how they opened and closed a call and give each one ownership of one problem category, keeping existing adherence and handle-time targets; or she could remove the script altogether, let advisors improvise the whole conversation, stop measuring handle time, and stop reviewing call recordings. Each path drew the line between advisor discretion and contractual requirement somewhere different, and the contract itself did not move.

By week eleven, with the client due to hear a progress report in person, the last decision was what recurring conversation would keep whatever gains had been made from sliding back. She could install a short fortnightly check-in built around what had gone well, what each advisor wanted to try next, and one customer they had helped; a weekly review of every metric each advisor had missed against target, with corrective actions logged for each one; or a monthly cash bonus for the highest scorers, paid alongside the usual payroll cycle and announced at the next team meeting. Whichever ritual she chose, she would then have to stand in front of the client and account for whatever had happened, without pointing at money, because there had not been any to spend.

## Closing

At 9:14 a.m. on 9 February, Marsh closed the survey report, opened a blank page, and wrote the date at the top. Ninety days, four numbers, and no budget. Before anything else moved, she had to write down what had actually broken Team Aurora. Eleven weeks remained, and there was still no budget behind whatever she chose next.
