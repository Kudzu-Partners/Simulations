# Valuing the Intangible — Pricing Lumengrid Before the IPO

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1798 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1798 — *Valuing the Intangible* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Tuesday, 9 June 2026, Lumengrid SAS's sponsor banks set an indicative price range for its Euronext Paris listing: EUR 22 to 28 per share, valuing its equity between EUR 1.4 and 1.9 billion. At Aurial Securities, a Paris-based independent research and advisory house, senior equity analyst Élodie Marchand read the filing that morning, six trading days before the order book closed, with a number and a rating still to publish on a software company that, by its own balance sheet, was worth almost nothing.

That gap was the whole problem. Lumengrid's most recent accounts showed EUR 6 million of net tangible assets and an operating loss, against a market about to price the company in the billions. A reader who stopped at the balance sheet would call the deal absurd. Marchand's job, with her team, was to decide whether it was — and to publish, under deadline, a valuation and recommendation that Aurial's clients, and eventually the market, would hold her to.

## The Protagonist

Marchand, 38, had spent nine years on Aurial's technology and software desk, the last four as lead analyst on its highest-profile initiations. She had joined from a larger bank's research arm, drawn by Aurial's standing as one of the few sizeable Paris houses selling research without also underwriting the deals it covered. Lumengrid would be her fourth IPO initiation of the year and, by revenue, the largest company she had valued from a standing start.

## The Decision

Over six trading days, Marchand and her team worked the valuation in stages, publishing nothing until the final call.

The first task was to take a stance on a company whose worth was mostly invisible to accounting: build Lumengrid's value up from its intangible assets — the algorithms, the data, the retention economics — as assets real enough to judge on their own terms; measure it instead against the EUR 6 million of net tangible assets as the anchor; treat it as unsupportable given the absence of earnings; or peg it to the midpoint of the bankers' range on the view that the market would set the price. Marchand also had to split conviction — percentages adding to one hundred — across four lenses: discounted cash flow on long-horizon growth; revenue and gross-profit multiples against comparable software companies; a sum-of-the-parts build pricing platform, customer base and data assets separately; and a real-options view of the platform's optionality — and to set, zero to a hundred, how bluntly the team stated the question its clients needed answered.

The second stage priced what the accounts left out. The 1,900-customer base, retained at 124 percent of revenue net of churn, could be built from cohort economics — retention, expansion, margin and a discount rate turned into cash flow; priced as a flat figure times the customer count; or left out on the view that recurring revenue was already captured in the income statement, leaving no separate cash flow to model. The EUR 71 million of expensed research and development could be capitalised where it had built a durable asset, with an amortisation life tied to the platform's economics; capitalised in full over a standard ten years; or left as expensed, on the view that the spending had not yet produced an asset separable enough to capitalise. The team also had to prevent double-counting — one method per intangible with the overlap netted out; a standalone figure kept alongside a multiple already pricing in the same retention; or the intangible work dropped and the multiple trusted alone — and to translate the moat and switching costs into one figure, zero to twenty percent added to the growth-fade assumption.

The third stage set the assumptions the model would run on: a discount rate between seven and fourteen percent, a terminal growth rate between one and six percent, a terminal margin, once growth spending normalised, between five and forty percent. Then came the choice of how to present the answer — a bear-base-bull range built around one named assumption carrying the most risk; a single point estimate from whichever method the team trusted most; or a figure worked backward to land inside the bankers' EUR 22–28 range. Separately, Marchand had to name the one assumption — growth durability, the discount rate, or none at all — carrying the most risk to the published number.

By the sixth day the team had to decide how to treat any new information reaching the desk before publication: re-test the growth and concentration assumptions and adjust the range, hold the number on the view that the new information did not shift the range the team had tested, or move to the most conservative reading available. Because Aurial sold advisory services as well as research, keeping the call independent of the firm's own commercial preferences was, under MiFID II, part of the job: publish what the analysis supported and put that independence on record, soften the rating toward the top of the range for the sake of a banking relationship, or withdraw coverage altogether rather than issue a call the relationship might be seen to have shaped. Finally came the call itself — hold, near the lower-middle of the range as fairly priced for the risk; buy, on the view the intangible-rich growth case justified the top of the range and beyond; or sell, on the view that not even the intangibles could defend it — with a price target, a twelve-month horizon, and, again zero to a hundred, how firmly to defend the valuation, the key assumption and the rating against challenge.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurial's research desk occupied an unusual position in Paris: because it did not underwrite the deal, its call carried no fee attached to the outcome, and clients paid for a view the syndicate banks could not offer without conflict. That independence was also Aurial's most fragile asset — the same firm advised corporate clients too, and under MiFID II the two sides had to operate at arm's length, a distance that depended on what an analyst actually did when interests diverged.

Lumengrid was a French business-to-business software company selling an AI-assisted energy-optimisation platform across Europe: EUR 132 million of revenue, growing 38 percent year on year, at an 81 percent gross margin, alongside a EUR 9 million operating loss as it reinvested in product and sales. Its filing gave Aurial's desk a normal company's accounts, and then, separately, a harder set of facts: a customer base, a data asset and a research programme the balance sheet was never built to price.

## The Situation

Against Lumengrid's EUR 6 million of net tangible assets sat the sponsors' own indicative valuation of EUR 1.4 to 1.9 billion — eighteen to twenty-four times forward revenue (see Exhibit 1). Aurial's working file that morning showed the distance still to travel: a first-pass estimate anchored to historical earnings put Lumengrid's equity at roughly EUR 0.80 billion, below the range's low end. The desk's scorecard for the note in progress read 20 of 100 on valuation quality — how rigorously the analysis would hold up — 15 of 100 on assumption robustness — how well a range of scenarios, not one guess, could defend the number — and a provisional 50 of 100 on recommendation credibility, the trust a client would place in whatever Aurial eventually published (see Exhibit 2). None of the team's 120 budgeted analyst-hours had yet been spent.

If the desk did no more than accept that first-pass number, the note would understate even the low end of the range the market had already set — a call clients would need reasons for that the earnings-anchored math could not supply, with valuation quality and assumption robustness stuck near where they started.

## What Marchand Controlled

Across the six days, Marchand's authority ran from the opening stance on Lumengrid's worth to the published rating and price target, with every assumption in between. She was measured on the quality and robustness of the valuation work, and on the credibility of the call once it reached a client's desk — the one asset, colleagues reminded her, an analyst actually owned.

## Closing

On the Tuesday morning the range came out, Marchand pulled up Lumengrid's filing next to a blank valuation memo, the comparable-company set open in a second window and six trading days already running. She began to choose.
