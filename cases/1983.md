# Zambeka Energy Regulation Authority: Light Up Zambeka

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1983 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1983 — *Power the Grid* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of April 1, 2026, Mutinta Chanda read the Cabinet directive twice before she accepted that its three numbers were binding at the same time. Zambeka was to add 600 megawatts (MW) of new generation within 24 months. National electricity access was to rise from 43% to 60% within five years. And the Treasury subsidy that kept the state utility standing — ZMK 4.1 billion a year — was not to rise by a single kwacha. The directive was titled "Light up Zambeka," and it gave the Zambeka Energy Regulation Authority (ZERA) 120 days to publish a procurement-and-tariff plan. If the plan did not appear, the authority's auction mandate returned to the Ministry.

Chanda was ZERA's Director-General. Inside those 120 days she had to fix, in one published document, the terms of the country's first competitive renewable-energy auction and the tariff regime that would pay for the power it bought — and then defend both to a Cabinet and donor review panel while the lights were still going out six to eight hours a day.

## The Protagonist

Chanda, 49, had run ZERA since 2023. She had joined the authority as a tariff economist in its early years, spent nine years at a regional development bank appraising generation projects — several of them signed, announced, and never brought to financial close — and returned to Mukela to take the Director-General's chair. Her name was on the cost-of-supply study that had established, three years earlier, exactly how far Zambeka's retail tariff sat below the cost of the electricity it delivered. The study had been accepted by everyone and acted on by no one.

## The Decision

Chanda had to decide what Zambeka would pay for new power, who would carry the risk of the utility not paying for it, and how the country's electricity bill would be redistributed between consumers and the Treasury. The three decisions went into the same document on the same day (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Company and its Setting

ZERA was the independent economic regulator of Zambeka's electricity sector, created by statute and based in the capital, Mukela. It set retail tariffs, licensed generators, and approved the procurement plans of Zambeka Power Company (ZPC), the state-owned, vertically integrated utility that generated, transmitted and sold almost all the electricity in the country. ZERA was a small institution regulating the sector on which every other sector in Zambeka depended.

One thing separated it from the ministry it had been carved out of: a recently granted statutory mandate to run competitive renewable-energy auctions on its own account, independently of the utility. ZERA had never used it. The first auction had to launch inside the 120-day window.

## The Situation

Zambeka had 2,800 MW of installed capacity, roughly 80% of it hydropower. A multi-year drought had cut firm hydro output by 35%. The shortfall was met by rolling load-shedding of six to eight hours a day and by emergency diesel at ZMK 3.20 per kilowatt-hour (kWh) — a price no tariff in the country came close to covering.

ZPC was technically insolvent. The average retail tariff of ZMK 0.92/kWh recovered 71% of the cost of supply, leaving a 29% cost-reflectivity gap that the Treasury closed with the ZMK 4.1 billion subsidy (see **Exhibit 1**). The single-step increase under discussion was about 30%, four months after a contested by-election, with consumer associations already organising.

Two facts shaped the procurement. The only new generation that could be built inside 24 months was renewable independent power producers, bought competitively. And ZPC's standing as the buyer of that power was sub-investment grade: developers signing a twenty-year contract with it would price the risk of not being paid into their bids unless something in the plan covered that risk — and anything that covered it drew on the same ZMK 4.1 billion.

The access target carried its own arithmetic. Moving from 43% to 60% meant 17 percentage points, roughly 1.9 million households, most of them rural. Extending the grid cost ZMK 9,500 per connection. Mini-grids and standalone solar systems reached remote households at ZMK 3,200 per connection, but sat outside the central network and did not register in the grid-reliability figures the Cabinet followed.

If ZERA published nothing, the arithmetic ran on without it: no new megawatts, access held at 43%, the diesel bill at ZMK 3.20/kWh pushing through the ZMK 4.1 billion ceiling inside a year, and the auction mandate back at the Ministry after 120 days.

## What Chanda Controlled

Her first task was the plan's opening page: naming what actually bound "Light up Zambeka" — the supply of generation, the tariff and the utility's creditworthiness, or the geography of reaching rural households; naming which of the three Cabinet targets was most exposed if the authority changed nothing; stating whether the auction, the tariff path and the credit package were one interdependent system, three workstreams run in parallel, or a strict sequence finished one at a time; and deciding how much of the 120 days to spend quantifying the 29% gap before committing to any of it.

Then the auction term sheet. How much of the 600 MW to tender in the first round: 150 MW, 400 MW, or the whole 600 MW. The reserve price, the ceiling above which no bid would be accepted: ZMK 0.55, ZMK 0.85, or ZMK 1.20 per kWh. The credit-enhancement package standing behind ZPC's offtake: none, an escrow account, or a partial risk guarantee with a sovereign backstop, the last two drawing on the same ZMK 4.1 billion. Bidder qualification: price-only screening open to the widest field, or proven track record, committed financing and a performance bond. And the local-content and grid-connection obligation, anywhere from 0% to 60% of project value.

Then the tariff and access plan. The path to cost-reflectivity: a single step of about 30%, a phased path reaching the same point by Year 3, or no change. The lifeline block: a subsidised first tranche of consumption for low-income households, or one flat tariff for everyone. The split of the connection budget between grid extension at ZMK 9,500 and off-grid systems at ZMK 3,200, anywhere from all of one to all of the other. And the basis of ZPC's allowed revenue: full cost recovery whatever the utility's efficiency, or revenue tied to loss reduction, collection rates and connection targets.

Finally the review panel, where one live event would land on the table. The lowest bidder might be revealed as a politically connected shell with no plant and no financing, and Chanda would either award it and fill the volume or disqualify it and take less capacity that round. Consumer protests might demand the increase be reversed, and she would either concede the rollback or hold the tariff path and bring the lifeline block forward. Or the drought might deepen and take a further 10% of hydro, met either by firing up emergency diesel or by pulling more auctioned renewable capacity and storage into the schedule. She would then choose what to lead a three-minute defence with — the single strongest number, the megawatts procured, or capacity, access, affordability and fiscal balance shown together — name the one metric on which she staked the programme, whether megawatts contracted, delivered megawatts reaching the grid within the ceiling, or the lowest bid achieved, and register how firmly she held the position.

Five figures would follow the plan through every review: megawatts actually delivered against the 600 MW target; national access against 60%; the projected clearing price of the winning bids, in ZMK per kWh; the subsidy drawn against the ZMK 4.1 billion ceiling; and ZERA's standing with investors and Cabinet, a credibility judgement that on April 1 stood at fragile.

## Closing

At the end of the day Chanda wrote three figures on the whiteboard in her office — 600, 60 and 4.1 — and beneath them the line she had not yet been able to fill in: the price, in kwacha per kilowatt-hour, at which Zambeka would agree to buy electricity for the next twenty years. The plan was due in 120 days.
