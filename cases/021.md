## Abstract

Grupo Montoya, a third-generation Latin American family conglomerate with four operating divisions and sixty-one years of history, entered 2026 with its founder-generation chairman in declining health and its succession process unresolved. Four scores would, by the family protocol drafted in 2019 and never tested, define the outcome of the transition: Governance Quality (GQI), Family Cohesion (FCS), Business Viability (BVS), and Stakeholder Legitimacy (SLI) — each out of twenty-five, composite out of one hundred. The case opens as Sofía Montoya, 38, third-generation heir and Logistics-division president (EBITDA +34% under her tenure), prepares for the first of five family-council meetings that will sequence a mandate decision, a CEO selection, a governance-architecture design, a response to a Textiles-division crisis (the division founded by her late grandfather, 400 jobs, EBITDA −12%), and a generational capital allocation across core, adjacent, and disruptive horizons. The case ends at the mandate decision — before the CEO choice is made — and sets up Round 1 of the simulation.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Distinguish between the three decisions that most family successions collapse into one — mandate, selection, and architecture — and explain why sequencing matters.
2. Evaluate the trade-offs between family cohesion, governance quality, and business performance under a weighted, bounded scoring regime.
3. Diagnose the role of external directors and independent professionals in legitimizing succession in a founder-anchored family system.
4. Analyze the interaction between a legacy-division crisis and the governance architecture designed one round earlier.
5. Recommend an opening mandate and articulate the falsifiable assumptions behind it.

On the afternoon of **March 3, 2026**, **Sofía Montoya** stood in the boardroom at Grupo Montoya's headquarters, in the country's second-largest commercial city, and read, for the fourth time, the one-page agenda her cousin Mariana had circulated that morning. The agenda carried a single item: *"Succession — Mandate to Lead."* Below it was Mariana's handwritten note: *"Tío Ricardo wants control. Felipe wants a vote. The independents want a process. Which one do you want?"* The first family-council meeting was at 4:00 p.m.

---

## The Protagonist

Sofía Montoya, 38, was the elder daughter of the second-generation CEO, Enrique Montoya, who had stepped aside from operational leadership in 2023 and now served as honorary chairman. A chemical engineer by training, with an MBA from a leading U.S. school, Sofía had run the group's Logistics division since 2021. Under her, Logistics revenue had grown 28% and EBITDA 34%, overtaking the historical champion, Alimentos. She had joined the group in 2013 at the insistence of her mother, the family's strategic nerve, and had spent her first three years rotating through four divisions against her own initial preference to build something of her own outside the family. She had declined the CEO job twice — in 2019 and 2023 — on the same principle: *"Not until the question of who gets to decide has been decided."*

---

## The Decision

Sofía had to declare, by 4:00 p.m. on March 3, which of three opening mandates she would support in the family-council vote. The mandate — not the CEO, not the architecture — was the first of five sequential decisions that the 2019 family protocol required. A *control* mandate would hand the succession design to Ricardo Montoya (Sofía's uncle, 71, current chairman, majority of voting shares inside the family trust) unilaterally. A *democratic* mandate would put the CEO selection to a family vote weighted by economic interest, which would give Felipe Montoya (Sofía's cousin, CFO, 12% of voting shares, privately opposed to her) a near-blocking position. A *process* mandate — the one favored by the three external directors who had joined the holding board in 2021 — would install a nomination-and-governance committee with two independents, a family representative, and an external chair, and would submit *every* subsequent decision to that committee before family-council ratification.

The three mandates were not equal in any single score. A control mandate would buy short-run GQI (clarity of authority) at a steep cost in FCS and SLI. A democratic mandate would buy FCS (every branch represented) at a severe cost in GQI and, probably, BVS. A process mandate would buy SLI and a legitimate GQI ceiling but at the cost of speed and at the real risk that Ricardo, the largest voting block, would refuse to ratify the very committee he had nominally agreed to in 2019.

## Company Background

Grupo Montoya had been founded in 1965 by Sofía's grandfather, Don Arturo Montoya, as a textile workshop supplying uniforms to regional schools and hospitals. Over six decades the group had diversified into four divisions: *Textiles* (the founding business, now 8% of revenue, −12% EBITDA in 2025), *Alimentos* (packaged foods, 36% of revenue, the historical cash engine), *Logística* (contract logistics and warehousing, 22% of revenue, the fastest grower under Sofía), and *Inmobiliaria* (commercial real estate, 34% of revenue, the largest balance-sheet item). Consolidated 2025 revenue was USD 1.9 billion with a group EBITDA margin of 11.4%. The group employed 8,400 people directly, of whom roughly 400 were in the Textiles division's two legacy plants, one of which carried Don Arturo's name on the gate.

The family owned 100% of the holding company through a 2011 trust structure that assigned voting shares across three branches: *Ricardo's branch* (31%), *Enrique's branch — Sofía's* (38%, of which Sofía personally held 14%), and *the late Fernando's branch — Felipe's* (31%). The trust required a two-thirds vote of voting shares for CEO appointment and for any amendment to the governance protocol. No single branch held a blocking minority alone. Any two did.

## Industry and Market Context

The case sat at the intersection of two well-documented family-business patterns. First, the *third-generation mortality problem*: fewer than one in eight Latin American family conglomerates survived the third generation as a unified group, and those that did had — in the five country-level studies the external directors had commissioned in 2023 — four features in common: (a) a written protocol tested before it was needed; (b) an independent majority or near-majority on the holding board; (c) a separation of family employment from family ownership; and (d) an explicit mandate for professional leadership at the operating layer. Grupo Montoya had (a) on paper and none of (b), (c), or (d) in practice.

Second, the *legacy-division trap*: in regional studies of diversified family groups, the founding division was on average the slowest to restructure and the first to drag composite performance below the public-benchmark threshold. Textiles, at −12% EBITDA and 400 jobs, was a textbook case. Any architecture Sofía helped design in the third family-council round would be tested, in the fourth, against the legacy business her grandfather had founded.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Don Arturo had died in 2011. Enrique had led the group from 2012 to 2023. Ricardo, Don Arturo's second son, had chaired the holding board from 2023 forward. The 2019 governance protocol — drafted by an external family-business consultancy and ratified unanimously during a moment of unusual family accord — had specified five sequential succession decisions but had never been invoked because no transition had been triggered. On February 20, 2026, Ricardo's oncologist had delivered a prognosis that, while not terminal in the short run, made continued chairmanship through 2027 implausible. The family council had been convened within twelve days. The mandate decision had been scheduled for March 3.

### What Sofía knew

The three external directors — Dr. Andrés Suárez (former CEO of a regional consumer-goods company), Patricia Lemus (former partner at a global strategy firm), and Luisa Arango (former securities regulator) — had privately told her that they would support a process mandate and would resign, together, if a control mandate were ratified. Mariana, her younger cousin and the only second-generation voice aligned with her, would vote with her. Her father Enrique, as honorary chairman, had told her at dinner on February 28: *"I do not want to be the vote that keeps my brother out. I also do not want to be the vote that keeps you out."*

### What she did not know

Whether Ricardo, under a process mandate, would in fact ratify the nomination committee's eventual CEO recommendation — or would, as he had in 2019 in a smaller matter, accept the committee in principle and block its output in practice. Whether Felipe's opposition was principled (he believed, or said he believed, that the CEO job required a CFO's discipline) or positional (he wanted the job himself). Whether the Textiles crisis, known to her through her seat on the divisional audit committee but not yet to the full family, would accelerate or derail whichever architecture she helped build.

### What others were saying

**Ricardo Montoya**, chairman and holder of the largest single voting block, argued for a **control mandate**: *"The group needs one voice. The protocol is a luxury we can invoke in Year 2. Year 1 is survival. I name the CEO. We keep moving."*

**Felipe Montoya**, CFO and holder of 12% of voting shares, argued for a **democratic mandate**: *"Every branch has an economic interest. Every branch has a voice. The 2019 protocol is exactly what it looks like: a consultants' artifact. Let the shareholders vote."*

**Dr. Andrés Suárez**, lead external director, argued for a **process mandate**: *"The group will trade at a family-discount or at a governance-premium. The mandate decision, made in public and legibly, is the hinge. Nothing else you decide in the next eighteen months will matter as much."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Sofía had narrowed the space to three options.

- **Option A — Control Mandate (Ricardo's plan):** Ratify Ricardo's unilateral authority to name the CEO and design the governance architecture in Round 3. Expected first-round score impact: GQI +3, FCS −5, BVS +1, SLI −4, composite net −5. Speed high; external-director retention risk: high (all three likely resign). Felipe's branch: likely to formalize opposition in writing.

- **Option B — Democratic Mandate (Felipe's plan):** Put CEO selection to a branch-weighted vote. Expected first-round score impact: GQI −4, FCS +4, BVS −2, SLI −1, composite net −3. Ricardo's branch likely votes Ricardo's candidate; Felipe's branch likely votes Felipe; Sofía's branch splits unless Enrique delivers. Near-certain CEO-selection gridlock in Round 2.

- **Option C — Process Mandate (the external directors' plan):** Install a nomination-and-governance committee (two independents, one family representative rotated, external chair) with binding recommendation authority, subject to council ratification requiring a two-thirds vote. Expected first-round score impact: GQI +4, FCS +1, BVS 0, SLI +5, composite net +10. Speed moderate; committee output in 6–8 weeks; Ricardo's public acceptance in the room on March 3 required to bind him in Round 3.

Sofía suspected that Option C maximized composite expected value across all five rounds, but that its Round-1 fragility — Ricardo walking out of the room, or nominally accepting and later refusing — was higher than the one-round arithmetic suggested. Option A would settle Round 1 cleanly and lose Round 5. Option B was the one her mother had warned her, on the phone at noon, "looks democratic and ends in silence."
