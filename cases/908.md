# Veltrana Sistemas: The Package on the Table

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-908 · **Companion simulation:** Express 908 — *ExitPackage — Negotiating the Severance They Already Wrote* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Wednesday, April 15, 2026, at five in the afternoon, Cristina Ferrer, the human-resources director of Veltrana Sistemas, closed the door of Javier Montalbán Ruiz's office behind her and left a folder on his desk. His role, she had said, was being eliminated as part of a restructuring. The folder held a separation package and a signature page.

Montalbán, Director of Strategic Accounts, had seven days to sign or to answer. The package was worth €60,000. His wife, Elena, told him that evening to sign it and move on. His friend Íñigo Labarta, who had been through the same thing two years earlier, told him to always counter. Montalbán had until Wednesday, April 22, to decide which of them was right.

## The Protagonist

Montalbán was forty-two and had spent six years at Veltrana, the last three as a director with a team of nine account managers and a portfolio of the company's largest clients. Before that he had spent a decade in sales at two larger software firms. He had negotiated hundreds of client contracts. He had never negotiated his own exit, and on the Wednesday he was, by his own account, still in shock.

## The Decision

Montalbán had to decide whether to sign the package as written or to counter it, and if he countered, with what. He worked through it in four steps over the week. First, he would read the package line by line and mark what was missing or below market. Second, he would map what he could bring to the table. Third, he would write the counter-letter. Fourth, he would answer whatever reply came back from HR.

## The Company and its Setting

Veltrana Sistemas S.L. was a Madrid-based company that sold planning and billing software to utilities and telecom operators in Spain and Portugal. It employed about 850 people and had been restructuring its commercial organisation since January 2026. Several directors had already left. None had said publicly what they had signed.

Montalbán's base salary was €120,000 a year. He held restricted stock units (RSUs), company shares that vested over time, some of which would vest within the next twelve months. His family's private health cover came through the company. He had used the same company laptop for six years. His clients knew him by name, and two large renewals he had led were still in negotiation.

Spanish law, under the Workers' Statute, set severance for a dismissal found unfair at 33 days of salary per year of service. For Montalbán, with six years of service, that came to about €65,100 (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The package offered six months of base salary, €60,000; the pro-rata share of the year's bonus; a standard release of all claims against the company; and a twelve-month non-compete clause. It said nothing else. The covering letter from Ferrer called it "our standard and final offer for your level" and asked for his signature within seven days.

Montalbán read it twice. The number sounded reasonable. He also knew that Ferrer's team had done this several times since January, and that he had not.

If he did nothing, the seven days would run out on April 22 with the package as written: €60,000, the bonus share, a release of claims and a year out of his industry. If he signed early, the result was the same, a week sooner.

## What Montalbán Controlled

In the first step he went through the package and decided which of seven items to flag as missing or below market: outplacement support to find his next role; a written commitment to a reference; full acceleration of his unvested RSUs; a waiver or a paid buyout of the non-compete; a bridge for his family's health cover; an agreed narrative for how his departure would be announced to clients and staff; and keeping his company laptop. He could flag as many or as few as he judged right.

In the second step he chose what to bring to the table. The list in front of him had seven entries: the client relationships he had built; his knowledge of the deals in flight; references colleagues had offered to give him; a clean handover of documentation and intellectual property; his knowledge of the risks the company was exposed to; a threat to take his story public; and a claim that he had a competing offer, which he did not. Again, he could use any combination.

In the third step he wrote the counter-letter. He chose its tone: professional and firm, an emotional appeal to six years of service, or assertive and threatening. He chose how many months of base salary to ask for, anywhere from six to twenty-four; his working draft said twelve. And he decided on three further moves, together or separately: asking in writing for a fourteen-day extension to review the package; anchoring his ask explicitly on the statutory figure; and engaging an employment lawyer, at a fee of €1,500.

In the fourth step, once HR replied, he chose his answer: hold his position politely and restate his asks; accept whatever partial offer was on the table; escalate the matter to legal counsel; or sign the original package and end it.

Montalbán tracked four things on a single page as the week went on (see Exhibit 2). The value of the package as it then stood, in euros, which began at €60,000. The statutory figure beside it. His credibility with the company, from 0 to 100, a rough gauge of how seriously HR, his former colleagues and future references would take him, which he put at 100 on the Wednesday. And whether he had a lawyer. After each step he wrote down the package value and his credibility. He would judge the week by how far the package had moved from €60,000 and by what was left of his credibility at the end, in an industry where everyone knew everyone.

## Closing

At 10:40 that night Montalbán opened the folder on the kitchen table and read the first page a third time. Elena had gone to bed. His phone showed a message from Íñigo: "Don't sign anything tonight." The signature page was on top.
