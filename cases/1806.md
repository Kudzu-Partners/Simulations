# Building the Global TMT: Staffing Helvetia's Executive Committee for Horizon Asia

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1806 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1806 — *Building the Global TMT* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of February 9, 2026, Livia Meier read the board resolution a second time at her desk in Helvetia Precision Group's Winterthur headquarters. A week into her tenure as chief executive, she had been handed "Horizon Asia," a board-approved plan to triple the company's Asia-Pacific revenue from CHF 230 million to CHF 690 million within three years through entries into Japan, South Korea, India and Southeast Asia. The first two of those four entries had to be operating within 12 months, before a well-capitalized regional competitor already established in the region could lock up the distribution relationships Helvetia needed. Before Meier could decide which market to enter first, she faced a narrower question: whether the nine people who ran Helvetia with her could run an Asia strategy at all.

## The Protagonist

Livia Meier, 51, had joined Helvetia in 2011 and spent the six years before her promotion running the company's Americas division from Chicago, rebuilding a stagnant unit without gutting its culture. The board had passed over two longer-serving committee members to make her chief executive, effective February 2, 2026, partly because she had no fingerprints on the committee's composition. She had never operated a business in Asia herself. Her compensation was tied to the Asia-Pacific revenue trajectory the board would review at the 12-month and 36-month marks, and the two executives she had been chosen over still held seats on the committee she now had to redesign.

## The Decision

Meier had to decide how to rebuild the nine-person executive committee she had inherited into a team capable of executing Horizon Asia, and then how to point that team at four specific markets, in what order and under whose leadership, without missing the 12-month deadline on the first two. The board expected a staffed committee, a market sequence, and a defense of both by the time results came in.

## The Company and its Setting

Founded in Winterthur in 1971 and family-controlled for nearly five decades, Helvetia Precision Group had listed on the SIX Swiss Exchange in 2019. It manufactured high-end measurement and process-control instruments for pharmaceutical, food and semiconductor customers, and by 2026 employed 8,900 people across 14 countries, generating CHF 2.3 billion in revenue at a 16% EBIT margin. Its revenue, though, was heavily concentrated: 68% came from Europe, 22% from North America, and only 10% — CHF 230 million — from Asia-Pacific, even as European industrial demand stagnated. The executive committee reflected that same imbalance: eight of its nine members were Swiss or German nationals, seven had spent their entire careers inside Helvetia's European organization, and none had ever run a business in Asia.

## The Situation

The board's mandate came with four numbers Meier would be measured against at every review, alongside two constraints. On the 0-to-100 scale the board used to track the committee's nationality and experience diversity, Helvetia's nine scored 12. On the same scale, the committee's cohesion — how quickly and cleanly it had historically reached shared decisions — stood at 82. Its readiness to launch two Asia entries inside 12 months registered at 18. And projected Asia-Pacific revenue, the number the other three were meant to move, sat at CHF 230 million against the CHF 690 million target. The two constraints were headcount and money: the committee could grow from nine seats to a maximum of 12, and Meier had a CHF 18 million annual budget for senior leadership hiring and relocation — enough to fund roughly six or seven of the eleven senior moves on its wish list (see Exhibit 1). If Meier changed none of this and asked the current nine to travel more and lean on outside advisers for local knowledge, Horizon Asia would be carried out by the same team that had built Helvetia's European business, with no Asia operating experience among them, entering four unfamiliar markets against a competitor that already had the local knowledge Helvetia lacked.

## What Livia Controlled

Her first task was diagnostic: to name what actually separated the committee she had from the team Horizon Asia needed. She could call it a capability gap — no Asia operating experience or local networks on the committee; a matter of the current team needing more travel and effort; a reporting-line problem a redrawn organization chart would fix; or a gap outside consultants could close while the committee stayed as it was. Having named the gap, she had to decide how different the committee should become — add nationality and Asia experience while keeping a core of European continuity, leave the committee close to as it was and add one Asia adviser, or replace most of the nine seats with the most diverse slate she could recruit — and to name the one capability, among real Asia operating experience, deep expertise in Helvetia's own instruments, or comfort with Winterthur head-office culture, she would not trade away. She also had to put a number on how candidly her team accepted that it could not execute Horizon Asia unchanged.

The staffing decision that followed was the center of the job. Within the 12-seat ceiling and the CHF 18 million budget, Meier decided how many seats to fill with external Asia-experienced executives of varied nationalities at CHF 1.4 million each, how many with internal European leaders relocated to Asia at CHF 0.6 million each plus an 18-month ramp-up, and how many with internal European veterans promoted into new seats at CHF 0.3 million each (see Exhibit 2). She set, as a percentage, how much of the nine-person European core to protect rather than displace, and for every external hire chose whether to optimize for speed of filling the seat, for fit with the existing culture, or for a mix of Asia experience, cultural fit, and onboarding support.

Once the committee took shape, Meier pointed it at the four markets. She could lead with Japan and South Korea as the first wave and defer India and Southeast Asia to years two and three; concentrate the first year on Japan alone, treating one fully mastered market as the template for the rest; or open all four markets together, on the logic that simultaneous momentum would beat a staggered rollout. She assigned an executive to lead each entry — matching each leader's own experience to the market, assigning leadership by seniority, or concentrating leadership of all four entries in one Asia-experienced executive — and committed a specific budget, in CHF millions, to integrating the committee through onboarding, shared decision norms, and repair of whatever fault lines a more diverse committee created. Alongside that spending, she set, as a percentage, how firmly she protected the committee's decision speed while that work was under way.

At the 12- and 36-month marks, Meier had to read the results and offer the board her own diagnosis: that the diversity she had added matched what Horizon Asia required, that the diversity she had added left the committee still Europe-centered, or that the diversity she had added had outpaced the committee's ability to decide together. From that diagnosis she chose how to recalibrate — deepen integration and add the next capability, freeze the committee's composition and let it settle, or reshuffle it again in pursuit of a more diverse slate — and she had to answer, before the board, what her design of the committee had ultimately served: the Asia revenue trajectory it produced, a diversity index striking enough for the annual report, or keeping the committee comfortable. She also had to state, as a percentage, how firmly she would defend the idea that the committee's makeup should be judged by that revenue trajectory.

## Closing

At nine o'clock, Meier's assistant put through the first of eleven calls from candidates and internal contenders who had heard a rebuild was coming. Meier looked at the organization chart taped to her whiteboard — nine boxes, eight of them nearly identical on paper — then at the calendar, which gave her 12 months to have two of four Asia entries running before the competitor got there first. The board would review her committee, and her strategy, at the 12-month mark. She picked up the phone.
