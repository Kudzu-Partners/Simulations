# Lumio: Rebuilding the Engine Without Burning the Brand

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1793 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1793 — *AI in the Marketing Mix* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday morning, 9 November 2026, Meera Ashcroft, Chief Marketing Officer of Lumio, opened her phone to a screenshot already shared 60,000 times in two days: a paid social ad, produced by an AI content tool the marketing team had been trialling for six weeks, claiming the brand's best-selling serum "reverses ageing by 40 percent" — a claim Lumio had never substantiated and the regulator did not permit. Underneath it, a comment thread was gathering around the tag "#HonestBeauty my foot."

By the time the board met that week, it had read the same screenshot, and it gave Ashcroft two instructions at once: rebuild the marketing operating model around artificial intelligence within the quarter, and bring the blended cost of acquiring a customer down from £24 to nearer £19 within two quarters — without a second incident like the one that had just cost the company an estimated £1.2 million and pulled its brand-trust score down from 71 to 64. She had until Black Friday trading opened on 27 November — eighteen days away — to have a plan running, and two quarters beyond that to show the number was real.

## The Protagonist

Ashcroft, 43, had run Lumio's marketing function since 2022, arriving after nine years in brand management at a global personal-care company, where she had spent her career defending other people's promises rather than building one of her own. Lumio's founder had built the company on a single idea — clinically backed claims, stated plainly, nothing oversold — and Ashcroft had spent three years turning that idea into a growth engine: an in-house team of 45 alongside two outside agencies, a customer base past two million, and a marketing budget grown to £38 million a year. Her bonus was tied to the cost of acquiring a customer and to the same brand-trust score the incident had just damaged. Both now pointed the wrong way, in the same quarter, going into the busiest selling season of the year.

## The Decision

Ashcroft had to decide, in stages, how far artificial intelligence would reach into three parts of the marketing engine — who saw an advertisement and what was bid for the placement, what content a machine could draft and publish unsupervised, and what price it could set for an individual shopper (see Exhibit 2) — and how to divide a fixed £4.0 million across it. She then had to put the resulting rules in writing, specific enough for a lawyer and a marketer to work from the same page, and finally stand before the chief executive and the board's risk chair to commit to one acquisition-cost number, not a range, and defend it.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumio was a London-headquartered direct-to-consumer beauty and personal-care brand, founded in 2017, selling skincare and haircare across the UK and three other European markets through its own site, online marketplaces, and a growing retail presence. In its 2025 financial year the company generated £240 million in revenue and spent £38 million on marketing — about 16 percent of revenue — against a customer base of 2.1 million and a blended marketing-driven contribution margin of 34 percent. The brand's identity rested on a promise it called "honest beauty": transparent ingredients, clinically backed claims, and a loyal community built on the idea that Lumio would never oversell what its products could do. That same promise made the brand unusually exposed to an AI system that overstated a claim, mis-served a customer segment, or set a price it could not defend — a misstep that would barely register for a brand without Lumio's reputation to lose.

## The Situation

The fabricated-claim incident had cost an estimated £1.2 million in pulled spend, rework and goodwill, and had pulled the brand-trust tracker — a quarterly score out of 100 measuring how much the customer base trusted the brand — down from 71 to 64 (see Exhibit 1). Blended CPA, the average cost of winning one paying customer across every channel, stood at £24 against a board target of £19. A separate measure, conversion lift, tracked how much more of the brand's traffic was converting into buyers than before the AI programme began, and stood at zero. Every further verified incident carried an internal cost of £150,000, and two more this quarter would freeze the AI programme entirely. The £4.0 million transformation budget was fixed, with no overspend. The regulator had not yet opened a formal case, but it was watching, and the board had made clear that a second collapse would put more than the marketing budget in question. If nothing changed, the acquisition cost stayed at £24, the seven points of spent trust stayed spent, and the regulator's interest did not disappear on its own.

## What Ashcroft Controlled

On each front she first had to make a first-cut call. For targeting and bid optimisation: roll it out across the full account now, test it in one channel and extend it once she saw the result, or leave it exactly as it stood. For the content tool that had produced the fabricated claim: let it keep drafting behind a person who checked every claim before publication, let it draft and publish at full scale without that check, or stop using it for content until the checks existed. For the pricing tool: leave pricing untouched for now — one price for every shopper — test personalised offers openly at the level of a customer segment, or let the system set an individual price based on predicted willingness to pay. Underneath those three calls sat a broader question: three separate decisions, each judged on its own value and risk; adopting AI across every front; or pulling back from all of it.

Having made that call, she then had to set, for each front, how much of the running decision the machine would make alone: a person setting the direction with the machine assisting; the machine proposing the action with a person approving before anything ran; or the machine running without either check. Alongside that she had to split the fixed £4.0 million three ways — tools and platforms doing the AI work itself, people and process checking their output, and campaigns rebuilding the trust the incident had spent — with the three lines required to total exactly £4.0 million.

She then had to write the governance itself: a rule for what a human had to approve before an AI-drafted claim could publish — legal sign-off restricted to specific efficacy claims, legal sign-off on every AI-generated asset, or no sign-off beyond spot-checks after publication; a check on who the targeting system served and excluded — a regular automated audit, an occasional manual look at the audience reports, or no check; a rule for pricing — consistent segment pricing with no hidden individual prices, personalised pricing disclosed at the point of sale, or individual pricing with no disclosure to the shopper; and a stated readiness level, from nothing in place to a named owner with spend-pulling authority and an hour-one plan, for the next viral moment.

In the boardroom, she then had to commit to an acquisition-cost figure for two quarters out — anywhere from £14, the lowest figure on the board's scale, to £24, where the company already stood — and state an incident tolerance to the risk chair: one position held claims and fairness near zero while treating routine creative more loosely; a second applied that same near-zero standard everywhere without exception; a third accepted a higher running rate of incidents as the price of moving quickly. Asked whether the cut was real or borrowed against trust, she had three ways to answer: argue that efficiency and brand protection were complements and show the recovery evidence, claim that the programme delivered full efficiency without costing the brand any trust, or say plainly that the number came first and trust was next year's problem — then state, as a percentage, how firmly she would hold that line if challenged.

## Closing

Ashcroft opened a blank page, wrote the three fronts down one under the other — targeting, content, pricing — and beside them wrote "£4.0M." Black Friday was eighteen days away. The board wanted a rebuilt engine, a specific number, and no second screenshot, and it expected all three to come out of the same £4.0 million.
