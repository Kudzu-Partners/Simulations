# Lumina Media Group: Signal to Service

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1763 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1763 — *Signal to Service* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On a Wednesday morning in late March 2026, Elena Bassi opened the churn report her data team had finalized overnight. Monthly subscriber churn at Lumina Media Group had climbed from 2.6% to 4.1% in the three weeks since a global rival cut its price to €4.99 a month, undercutting Lumina's own €10.99. At 3.8 million subscribers, the jump meant roughly 57,000 additional cancellations every month, and if it held for a year, about €7.5 million of revenue the company would not get back.

Bassi was Lumina's Chief Marketing Officer. Finance had already ruled out matching the rival's price outright — cutting the standard subscription that far would gut the margin the company needed to keep operating. Instead, that morning the board gave her a fixed sum and a mandate: exactly €6.0 million, capital and operating spend together, for the whole year, to turn Lumina's own customer data into a service subscribers would pay to keep. She had ninety days to bring back a funded plan, and two quarters after launch for the board to see churn bend — or the money would revert to the ad-sales unit that had run the analytics group until then.

## The Protagonist

Bassi, 41, had run Lumina's marketing organization since 2023, after six years building the company's customer-insight function out of a two-person team that had reported into ad sales. She had argued for years that analytics should be treated as something the company could build products from, not a dashboard service for the sales floor, and the board's mandate was, in effect, that argument taken up — on terms she now had ninety days to prove. Her own performance goals for the year were tied in part to subscriber retention, the same number the board would be watching.

## The Decision

The board's mandate came in four parts, and Bassi would work through all of them before the ninety days were up. First, she had to decide which of Lumina's candidate data signals were worth activating, since instrumenting each one drew down the €6.0 million cap. Second, she had to commit to exactly one funded service — the board had explicitly ruled out spreading the money across several ideas to hedge. Third, once the bet was chosen, she had to divide what remained of the cap across the work of building it and set a target for how many subscribers she expected to actually use it. And if her plan ran into trouble partway through launch, she would have to decide how to spend whatever reserve she had not yet committed, and how to describe the service's value to a board that would be measuring her against the same subscribers who were leaving.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumina Media Group S.p.A. was a Milan-based media company founded in 2009, running a subscription music-and-podcast streaming platform alongside an in-house branded-content studio. It served 3.8 million subscribers across Italy and southern Europe and had generated €214 million in revenue in 2025 at a 9% EBITDA margin, or about €19.3 million, with 540 employees (Exhibit 1). Lumina could not out-catalogue or out-spend the global streaming platforms it competed against, and it had never tried to. What it had instead was a scale of behavioral data most rivals its size did not: every skip, every replay, every playlist edited by mood, every search that returned nothing worth playing, logged against 3.8 million listeners. Most of it sat unused. Until Bassi's promotion, the analytics team had mostly produced dashboards for the unit that sold advertising against the platform's audience.

## The Situation

The price cut was not a war Lumina could win on scale, and everyone from the board down understood that matching it was not really an option — the arithmetic did not close at Lumina's size. What the board wanted instead was proof that Lumina's data was worth more to a subscriber than a discount was. The €6.0 million was fixed for the year regardless of what Bassi found in the data or which service she chose, and it would not be raised if her first estimates ran short. Whatever she launched would also have to survive whatever happened after it went live: if early adoption came in well below what she had planned for, or if the rival kept escalating, she would not be able to go back to the board for more money. She would have only the part of the €6.0 million she had not yet spent, and a choice about how to spend it and how to explain the result. If nothing changed — if churn simply stayed where the price cut had pushed it, at 4.1% a month — Lumina would keep losing on the order of 57,000 subscribers a month it would otherwise have kept, and the board had already said what would happen to the analytics budget if two quarters passed without a visible bend in that number.

## What Bassi Controlled

Lumina's data team had catalogued eight candidate signal streams for Bassi to choose from, each with its own coverage of the subscriber base, a quality score, and an estimated cost to activate (Exhibit 2). Activating a signal was optional, and each one drew against the same €6.0 million; no combination was chosen for her in advance.

Once she knew which signals she could put to work, Bassi had to choose exactly one service to fund. A creator/curator marketplace would let subscribers follow human curators instead of an algorithm, built on the social-graph sharing signal. A discovery-guarantee retention service would promise to surface something a subscriber would love every week, built on search-intent and skip signals. A mood-and-context personalization layer would use skip/replay behavior, listening context, and mood-playlist edits together to decide, moment to moment, what to play next. A B2B audience-insight product would package anonymized listening patterns for sale to the content studio's advertisers, built on podcast-completion and listening-context signals. Whichever she picked, a service built on signals she had not activated would launch without the data it needed to work.

With one service chosen, Bassi had to divide what remained of the €6.0 million across three lines — data engineering and signal quality, building the service itself, and go-to-market spend to get subscribers to actually use it — and set a target adoption rate against which the board would later measure her. And if the launch ran into trouble, she would choose how to spend whatever she had held back: redirecting it into data quality to strengthen the service's foundations, pushing it into go-to-market to reach more subscribers directly, or holding it back and protecting the reserve while the launch continued as planned. At the same time she would decide how to describe the service to the board: as the list of new capabilities the team had shipped in ninety days, or as the one reason a subscriber would choose to stay rather than leave.

## Closing

Bassi set the churn report next to a blank sheet: one column for the signals she would fund, one for the single service she would defend, and one for a budget that had to add up to exactly €6.0 million and not a cent more. The board's ninety days had started that morning.
