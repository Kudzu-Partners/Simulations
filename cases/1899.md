# Reviva SAS: Closing the Bridge Round

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1899 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1899 — *The Renewal Venture* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, September 14, 2026, Camille Brun read the lead investor's message twice before she replied to it. The regional impact fund had held its term sheet open since July and would not hold it past October 5. It would not close below €750,000. Reviva SAS had €520,000 soft-committed against a €900,000 seed, which left €230,000 to find in twenty-one days.

The message carried a second condition. The fund would not finance three businesses with eleven people. Reviva sold refurbished appliances under its own brand, refurbished retailers' stock under their brands, and a repair subscription. The fund wanted one of the three named, and the other two put down.

## The Protagonist

Brun, 38, had spent six years as a product manager at a regional appliance maker in the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes region before she founded Reviva in 2024. The refurbishment concept had begun there as an internal pilot she ran for eighteen months. When the parent company declined to scale it, she left and took the idea with her. She held 62% of Reviva's equity. She had a newborn at home and had been working roughly seventy hours a week since the spring.

## The Decision

Brun had twenty-one days to settle three things at once: which single business Reviva was, where the remaining €230,000 came from and on what terms, and how the workshop and the founder would carry whatever she promised the fund. She would present the answer to the lead fund's partner as one case, and the fund would test whether its parts agreed with each other.

## The Company and its Setting

Reviva SAS, founded in Grenoble in 2024, collected small household appliances, repaired them, certified each unit for two years, and resold it. Eleven people worked there. Seven were technicians, and five of those were women returning to paid work after career breaks; Reviva had built its intake, training and certification routines around them. In the trailing twelve months the workshop had refurbished 9,200 units and booked €1.40 million of revenue at a 28% contribution margin, against an operating loss of €180,000 (see **Exhibit 1**). The loss came from capacity Reviva had built ahead of the demand that was supposed to fill it.

Three revenue lines ran through the same workshop and the same seven technicians (see **Exhibit 2**). Reviva's accounts did not split contribution between them; every unit passed through one intake bench and one certification step regardless of whose brand went on the box.

## The Situation

Reviva held €350,000 in cash and was spending €70,000 a month. The lead fund had put €750,000 as the floor for its own participation, valuing Reviva at €3.0 million after the money; at that price Brun's 62% would fall to roughly 46%. A second investor had offered a further €250,000. That offer came with a board seat and a clause obliging Reviva to appoint a professional chief executive within eighteen months. A public innovation loan of €150,000 from BPI France was available and carried no equity, though the repayments would sit in the monthly burn. Existing angels would put in a bridge with no board seat, no clause on the chief executive and nothing to repay out of the burn — but a small one: enough to top the BPI France loan up to the close, and not enough on its own to reach €750,000 without cutting the burn and raising again later at a higher price.

The workshop had its own arithmetic. Absenteeism had risen 19% over the previous quarter. Two of the seven technicians had told Brun directly that they would leave if the weekend shifts continued into the winter. Two additional technicians could be hired at €2,400 a month each; an automated intake and diagnostics bench was quoted as capital expenditure now against fewer hands later; and the volume the workshop accepted could be held at what the current seven could do in five days.

The lead fund tracked four things on its own monitoring sheet, each scored out of 100 except the money, and it had told Brun it would keep tracking them after the close. The first was whether Reviva had one coherent, fundable business model that fit what it claimed to be. The second was how much of the €750,000 was actually committed. The third was whether the founder and the workshop could sustain the plan being sold — the fund used Brun's own hours, currently about seventy a week against a stated target of fifty or fewer, as its proxy. The fourth was whether the team was still there and still willing. Alongside those four it read two further figures: the share of the company Brun still held, and how many months of cash the plan left her.

If October 5 passed without a close, the term sheet lapsed. The €520,000 was soft, not signed, and roughly 40% of such commitments had historically evaporated once a round stalled. Reviva would then be raising with five months of cash and a €180,000 loss behind it.

## What Brun Controlled

She controlled the diagnosis first: whether the binding constraint was the €230,000 itself, the three-way split of an eleven-person company, the resale volume the workshop was not yet selling, or the €3.0 million price. She controlled the runway arithmetic she put in front of the fund, the order in which she ranked the three lines on margin, on the capital each would absorb and on how each sat with what Reviva said it was for, and how she described the five returning technicians — as the workshop's cost structure and the reason its certification held, as both at once, with the P&L to show where it sat, or as the part of Reviva's story that distinguished it from every other refurbisher.

She controlled the commitment: own-brand resale, white-label refurbishment for retailers, the repair subscription, or two of them run in parallel. She controlled the route to €750,000 — the second investor's €250,000 as offered, the same €250,000 with governance and mission terms she would have twenty-one days to negotiate, the BPI France loan with a top-up from the angels, or a smaller angel bridge with the burn cut and a later raise — and how hard she pressed on her stake and on the board's composition.

She controlled the operating answer to the weekend shifts: two hires at €2,400 a month, the automated intake bench, a cap on volume, or the current roster carrying the growth the round was meant to fund. She controlled how she would lead — holding the work herself, hiring a chief operating officer and stepping out of daily operations, or distributing decisions across the team — the hours target she committed to, and how much of that target she put money behind.

And she controlled the pitch: whether to lead with the three decisions and how they fitted together, with the scale of the opportunity, or with the mission and the unit economics behind it; how to answer the fund when it asked whether inclusive employment was Reviva's cost structure or its story; which single metric she would be held to twelve months out; and how firmly she would press, in front of the partner, the case for the path she had chosen.

## Closing

Brun printed the term sheet, the cap table and the workshop roster and laid them side by side on the bench at the front of the shop, where the intake trolleys came in. Twenty-one days. €230,000. Three businesses, and a fund that would finance one.
