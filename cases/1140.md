# Kestrevan Software: The Year in Three Slides

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1140 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1140 — *Storytelling for Exec — Three Slides, Four Minutes* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 14:52 on Wednesday, April 15, 2026, Niamh Ferreira-Holt, Director of Product at Kestrevan Software, opened the deck she had shown at the company's all-hands meeting in March and counted the slides. There were nine. The executive review began at 9:00 the next morning, and every functional lead had the same allocation: three slides, four minutes, then questions.

She had a year of work to compress and one request to land: two more product managers and €450,000 released from the third-quarter budget. Between then and the review she had three working sessions with Hélène Marsh, the communication coach Kestrevan retained for its directors, at eighteen, twelve and six hours before she stood up. By the end of the third she had to have settled what the year was about, what the three slides would carry, and how the four minutes would sound.

## The Protagonist

Ferreira-Holt was thirty-seven. She had joined Kestrevan in 2019 as a product manager and had been promoted to Director of Product in February 2025, fourteen months earlier. She ran a function of 38 people, seven of them product managers. She had presented to the executive team before, at quarterly check-ins with no cap on slides, but this was her first annual review as a director. She knew the year's numbers in detail; the all-hands deck had been built to show them.

## The Decision

The request would be decided in the room. Declan Oyelaran, the chief executive, could approve it, send it to the following week's resource meeting as a proposal, or defer it to the fourth-quarter review. What Ferreira-Holt had to decide was not whether to cut, because the template settled that, but what to keep, in what order, and in what words.

## The Company and its Setting

Kestrevan Software Ltd., founded in 2016 and headquartered in Dublin, sold approval-workflow software for purchasing and spending decisions to mid-sized and large companies across Europe, on annual subscriptions. It employed about 410 people and closed fiscal 2026, the year to March 31, 2026, with €41.8 million in annual recurring revenue (see Exhibit 1).

Oyelaran had introduced the three-slide format after the half-year review in October 2025. At that review five of the seven functional leads had brought more than seven slides, and two had been stopped at the four-minute mark before they reached their requests. Both requests went out afterwards by email and took six to eight weeks to be answered. Presenters in that review had been interrupted between one and three times before their last slide. The template for April went out on Monday, April 13, and was not open to negotiation: three slides, with optional appendix slides after the third that would not be presented but could be pulled up during questions.

## The Situation

In fiscal 2026 Product had placed its main bet on enterprise self-serve: letting teams inside large customers start a trial and buy without a sales call. Enterprise trial-to-paid conversion rose from 5.0% of trials in fiscal 2025 to 6.7%, a 34% increase on a baseline that had been flat for two years. In the second quarter, July to September 2025, the customer mix moved. The share of new enterprise accounts that arrived through a self-serve trial rather than a sales contact went from 22% to 38%, and Product re-pointed its roadmap from supporting sales-led deals to product-led growth.

The year held more than that. The team had also pushed the small-business tier, which finished at 88% of its new-account plan. It had launched an approvals module, migrated the platform to a new hosting region, and delivered the product side of the company's rebrand. The all-hands deck carried all of it: a timeline graphic, three customer screenshots and a thank-you slide. The request sat in a bullet on slide 7.

Before the first session Marsh read the draft and put the chance that the request would be approved, with the deck as it stood, at 20%. If nothing changed, the first three slides of the all-hands deck would go into the template, the request would not reach the room, and it would follow by email, the route that had taken six to eight weeks in October.

Advice was not in short supply. Pilar Achterberg, the chief financial officer, who chaired the resource meeting, had told directors that a plan which changed mid-year could read as a plan that had been wrong, and that she preferred not to meet new figures for the first time in the review. Tomás Riordan, head of marketing, opened every review he gave with a customer story and had been approved twice.

## What Ferreira-Holt Controlled

In the first session, eighteen hours out, she settled three things. **The one-sentence year**, at most 25 words, which the deck would hang from: a list of deliveries, *"We delivered three things — the launch, the migration, and the rebrand"*; a two-part sentence, *"In 2026 we bet on enterprise; we also pushed SMB; both are tracking"*; a general one, *"In 2026 we made progress on our strategic priorities"*; or a single bet, *"In 2026 Product bet on enterprise self-serve; conversion +34%, so 2027 doubles down."* **The mid-year fork**: tell it as a year that ran the original plan; as continuous improvement through many small adjustments; or name it, the second-quarter mix shift and the move from sales-led to product-led. And **the kill list**: how many of the year's items to move out of the main slides into the appendix, from none to two, four, six or eight.

In the second session, twelve hours out, she settled the slides. **The headlines**: the generic set, *Strategy Update / Mid-Year Update / Results and Next Steps*; long titles describing each slide in detail; or short headlines of at most eight words naming the bet, the fork, and the number with the request. **The body text**, as average words per slide: 15, 30, 45, 60, or 90, a full memo. And **where the request lived**: nowhere explicit; in the appendix; on slide 1; on slide 2; or on slide 3.

In the third session, six hours out, she settled the delivery. **The first thirty seconds**: thanks and an agenda preview; the agenda alone; an anecdote; or the one-sentence year first, then slide 1. **The split of the 240 seconds** across the three slides: 60, 80 and 100; 70, 80 and 90; 80 each; 40, 60 and 140; or 90 each, four and a half minutes in all. **The phrasing of the request**: *"It would be great if we could explore the possibility of additional support"*; *"We are looking for some help on resourcing in Q3"*; *"I need 2 PM hires and a 450k EUR Q3 unlock"*; or *"2 PM hires + 450k EUR Q3. Yes, proposal next week, or defer to Q4?"* And **the prepared bridges**: how many thirty-second answers to likely interruptions, each leading back to the request, she would build in advance, from none to one, two or three.

Marsh kept a sheet (see Exhibit 2). After each session she scored that session's work, the spine after the first, the discipline of the deck after the second and the delivery after the third, with a signed number that could fall below zero. She wrote one line of feedback and revised her estimate of the probability that the request would be approved. In the room the request could end in one of four ways: a yes; a proposal for Achterberg's meeting the following week; a deferral to the fourth-quarter review; or a slot that ended at minute four with the request unmade.

## Closing

Ferreira-Holt opened a blank document beside the nine-slide deck, typed the year as a sentence, and deleted it. Marsh's video call was due at 15:00. In eighteen hours Oyelaran would start the clock.
