# Trattoria Radici: Plate to Plan

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1877 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1877 — *Plate to Plan* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 5 May 2026, Elena Conti watched her announcement go out to the press and the booking page: in thirty-five days, her one-Michelin-star trattoria in the hills outside Bologna would relaunch on a fully regenerative menu, built around soil-restoring local farms and near-zero food waste. Within the week her cost controller brought her the draft menu, costed against those same thirty-five days. Food cost against food revenue came out at 41 percent — eight points above where the kitchen had run all year and six points past the 35 percent ceiling he meant to defend.

Conti had thirty-five days to get a regenerative menu onto the pass without breaking the restaurant that paid for it. Across four working sessions before the first service, she and a small kitchen leadership team — a sous-chef for creativity, the cost controller, a sourcing and sustainability lead, a front-of-house manager — would turn a dazzling, over-budget draft into a menu the numbers could carry, without losing what made it dazzling.

## The Protagonist

Elena Conti was chef-owner of Trattoria Radici, built on an improvisational style of cooking that answered to whatever the land produced that week rather than to a fixed recipe. That instinct had won the restaurant its star, and it was now colliding with the promise she had just made in public: that Radici could source regeneratively, waste almost nothing, and still turn a profit. Her cost controller had spent the year defending a food-cost ceiling; her sourcing lead had spent it courting three small regenerative farms that could not yet supply the kitchen at scale. Both reported, that same week, that their piece of the plan did not close.

## The Decision

Over the thirty-five days, Conti moved the menu through four working sessions, each narrower than the last (Exhibit 2): a diagnosis of where creativity and cost collided, a sourcing plan under a supply ceiling the farms could not exceed, a re-engineering of the menu back inside the cost ceiling, and a defense of the finished plan to the critic and the award jury on service night itself.

## The Company and its Setting

Trattoria Radici was a 64-seat trattoria in the hills outside Bologna, built on modern dishes rooted in Emilia's own pantry and carrying one Michelin star. FY2025 revenue was €2.4 million; the kitchen ran on a food cost of 33 percent of food revenue, for a net margin of 9 percent, roughly €216,000 — thin, but real. The average check stood at €115 (Exhibit 1). Regenerative ingredients — heritage grains, pasture-raised meat, soil-restoring produce — cost 22 to 60 percent more than the conventional supply the kitchen had always bought, and the three regenerative farms lined up for the relaunch could guarantee only 60 percent of the volume the menu would need in a given week, including one heirloom bean that might not arrive at all.

## The Situation

The draft menu's 41 percent food cost was the headline number, but not the only one. Food waste ran at 9 percent of purchases, about €71,000 a year, against a public promise of 4 percent or below. A customer panel had scored the draft 8.4 out of 10 on novelty, yet flagged two dishes as "worthy but joyless." Inside the kitchen's own tracking, a separate reading of guest craving — not just novelty — stood at 55 out of 100 against a target of 80; a reading of the menu's regenerative integrity, which the jury's supplier audit would test, stood at 30 out of 100. A food critic and a regional sustainability award jury were both booked within the month.

The arithmetic was not hidden. If food cost stayed at 41 percent, the 9 percent net margin that kept the restaurant solvent would be gone within two quarters, as a loan covenant on the kitchen's recent refit tightened. If margin were instead protected by quietly blending conventional ingredients behind the regenerative claim, the critic and the jury — both intending to audit the kitchen's suppliers — would find that out on the night they sat down, in front of guests who had booked a regenerative meal.

## What Conti Controlled

Thirty-five days out, three choices opened the first session. Where creativity and economics collided on the draft could be named as the dishes that added cost without adding anything guests noticed; as nowhere real, the draft already right as it stood; as the regenerative ingredients themselves, stripped back to protect margin; or as the price, treating the gap as something a higher check would close. The team also had to split the menu between what moved with the week's harvest and what stayed fixed: lock all eight courses down as a fixed menu that promised consistency, leave the whole menu to improvise nightly with no fixed structure, or split it so volatile-supply courses moved while the anchor dishes protecting margin stayed put. A third dial set how rigorously the team mapped cost, supply risk, waste and novelty per dish, from a fast pass to an exhaustive audit.

Twenty-eight days out, sourcing came due. On every key ingredient, the kitchen could commit fully to the regenerative farms, accepting higher cost and lower volume for the full story; blend regenerative and conventional product and say so openly; blend the two without disclosing it, to protect margin quietly; or substitute an abundant local alternative for the costliest, most volatile regenerative ingredients. Conti also had to set the regenerative share claimed publicly, from a fifth of the menu to all of it, and a contingency for the heirloom bean that might not show some weeks: a flexible course that changed to whatever the land gave that day, a fixed signature dish printed and hoped for, or a last-minute conventional substitute if the harvest failed. A budget decision sat alongside those calls — how much of the relaunch's €120,000 to commit to farm guarantees and cold-chain logistics, from nothing up to €60,000.

Fourteen days out, the menu had to come back inside the ceiling. One choice was how to re-engineer it: sort dishes by what they earned against how often they sold, promoting the strong ones, reworking the weak ones, and cutting the rest; keep every creative dish exactly as drafted, since re-engineering would kill the artistry; or keep only the cheapest dishes, since novelty could not be protected without breaking the ceiling. A second choice was how much of the kitchen's trim, off-cuts and surplus to convert into stock, ferments and plated garnish rather than the bin, from none of it to all of it. A third was what to do with the two dishes the panel had called worthy but joyless: rework them to be craveable and regenerative at once, keep them unchanged as the most regenerative dishes on the menu, or cut them entirely and replace them with conventional crowd-pleasers. A fourth was the average check itself, movable anywhere from €115 up to €160.

On service night, four decisions closed the plan. The menu's weekly structure could hold fixed anchor courses alongside a section that changed with the harvest, run as a fixed printed menu regardless of supply, or improvise the entire menu nightly with no fixed anchors. The story told to guests could be woven lightly into service so the food led and the story supported it, delivered as a detailed sustainability account with every course, or left unsaid, for guests to notice on their own. What Conti presented to the critic and the jury could be a repeatable twelve-month operating plan with all four measures owned and tracked, one dazzling launch night with next month deferred, or a financial case leading with the margin and playing down the regenerative claim. And she had to decide how firmly to argue that the constraints she had worked under sharpened the kitchen's creativity rather than smothered it.

## Closing

At 8:00 p.m. on service night, Conti stood at the pass with the critic's table and the jury's table both seated, a single page face-down beside her, and thirty-five days of decisions behind her that either did or did not reconcile on the same sheet of paper.
