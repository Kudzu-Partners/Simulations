# Designing the Long Life: Pennine Precision Group and the Knowledge Cliff

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1768 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1768 — *Designing the Long Life* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 12 January 2026, Grace Fenwick read the workforce-risk review that the board had commissioned six weeks earlier, after two senior machinists retired without warning and stalled an aerospace line for eight weeks. The review's projection was plain: on the current trajectory, Pennine Precision Group would lose a third of its critical machining capability to retirement within seven years, with no plan to replace the tacit knowledge involved or to extend the working lives of those still willing to contribute. The board gave Fenwick, its newly appointed Workforce Strategy Lead, a fixed labour budget of £142 million and ninety days to redesign the workforce model without growing that number.

## The Protagonist

Fenwick, 45, had joined Pennine in 2023 after a decade in HR and operations roles across UK manufacturing, most recently leading a shift-pattern redesign at a rail-components supplier. She had been hired specifically to modernise a career model that predated her tenure by three decades: hire young, train once, promote on years served, retire at 65. She reported to the Chief People Officer, who had sponsored the review and would carry its outcome to the board. Around her sat a Reward & Pensions Lead who managed the pay architecture the redesign would have to change, an Operations Director whose priority was keeping the aerospace lines running, and a Finance Business Partner who would not sign off anything that crossed £142 million.

## The Decision

Fenwick had to make four decisions in sequence, each closing off some of what the next one could do, and defend the combined result to the board before a £48 million aerospace contract renewal that carried its own supplier-capability audit.

The first was how to see her own workforce at all. Pennine's 2,400 people could be grouped into age bands by decade, mapped by life-stage and contribution potential — learning, peak output, mentoring, exploration — or managed as a single headcount figure. Alongside that came a decision on how deeply to map the critical skill concentrated in the over-55 cohort, and a choice of what to build the map from: a skills matrix, a stage map paired with a retirement projection, or the existing HR headcount file on its own. The full menu, for this and the three decisions that followed, is set out in **Exhibit 2**.

The second was how to fund a mid-career reskilling programme, costed at £6–9 million out of the £142 million ceiling. It required a programme design, a time horizon on which to justify the spend to the board, and a specific cut elsewhere in the budget to offset it — because the savings, in avoided disruption and retained knowledge, would not land in the current year's numbers.

The third was the shape of a phased-retirement policy: how generous its terms would be, what mechanism — if any — would transfer a retiring machinist's knowledge before they left, and whether pay would stay tied to tenure or be re-anchored to current contribution. Layered onto those three choices was a fourth: how many of the fewer than fifteen critical master machinists to enrol.

The fourth, arriving with the audit six weeks out, was how to close whatever gap remained against the £142 million ceiling, what story to tell the board about the redesign, whether to hold or abandon the reward decision made in the third stage, and how much of the remaining budget flexibility to direct toward the knowledge-transfer work protecting the aerospace lines specifically.

## The Company and its Setting

Pennine Precision Group, founded in Sheffield in 1961, machined precision components for aerospace, energy and rail customers from three plants across Yorkshire. It employed 2,400 people and posted 2025 revenue of £410 million at a 9% operating margin — £36.9 million. Labour was its largest controllable cost, running at £142 million a year, and the board had fixed that figure as a ceiling rather than a target that could grow with the redesign.

Pennine's workforce skewed old for its sector: median age 49, with 38% of machinists aged 55 or over. Its apprenticeship pipeline filled barely half the seats those retirements would eventually vacate. The firm's career architecture — hiring, training, reward, retirement — had been built for a working life that ended, without exception, at 65.

## The Situation

Fewer than fifteen master machinists held the tacit skill — the feel for a machine that lived in no manual — behind £31 million of Pennine's revenue. Each unplanned retirement among them risked six to ten weeks of line disruption while the firm re-hired and re-learned what had walked out the door. If the trajectory held, the review had already done the arithmetic: a third of Pennine's critical machining capability would retire within seven years, the £31 million tied to their lines would sit exposed, and the £48 million aerospace renewal would reach its supplier-capability audit with no continuity plan behind it.

Every pound spent on the future — reskilling, phased retirement, a redesigned reward structure — had to come out of the same £142 million already committed to today's payroll. Fenwick's opening position — her starting numbers going into the four decisions — is set out in **Exhibit 1**.

## What Fenwick Controlled

Across the four stages, Fenwick set the unit of planning, the depth of skill mapping, and how much attention concentrated on the over-55 cohort; the design, horizon and offsetting cut behind the reskilling spend; the generosity, transfer mechanism, reward basis and enrolment of the phased-retirement policy; and, at the end, how the remaining gap was closed, how the results were narrated to the board, whether the reward decision held, and how much of what remained went toward transferring knowledge on the aerospace lines. She was measured on five things: talent retention, the reskilling programme's return, age-diversity productivity, how far the knowledge-cliff exposure closed, and total labour cost against the £142 million ceiling.

## Closing

At 8:40 a.m., Fenwick opened a blank workforce map on her screen: 2,400 rows, no stages assigned, ninety days on the clock, and a board meeting already on the calendar to hear what she would do about the fifteen people the aerospace lines could not run without.
