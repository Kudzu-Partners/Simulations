# Cascade Regional Medical Center: The Margin and the Mandate

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-818 · **Companion simulation:** Express 818 — *Medicare Margins* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, October 6, 2025, Ruth Calloway opened the board packet for the first of five executive-committee sessions and found the same fact on three different pages. Medicare patients, traditional and Medicare Advantage together, now made up 48% of Cascade Regional Medical Center's volume. The commercial plans that had long paid above cost had slipped to 26%. Behind the last tab sat a non-binding letter of intent from a large multi-state non-profit health system offering US$264 million for the hospital.

Calloway, President and Chief Executive Officer, had five committee sessions to take fifteen decisions to the board — on payer contracts, service lines and capital, quality and value-based care, the drug-discount program and the workforce, and finally the offer itself. The board chair wanted the response to the letter at the fifth session, and every recommendation before then would be read against it.

## The Protagonist

Ruth Calloway, 56, had led the hospital since 2019, after eight years as its Chief Financial Officer. She had come up through managed care and still read payer contracts clause by clause. She reported to a volunteer community board whose duty under Washington non-profit law ran to the people of the Spokane region rather than to any owner. The board judged her on the same dashboard she used to run the hospital.

## The Decision

Calloway had to decide how a 180-bed non-profit community hospital would earn enough to keep its services open, which services those would be, and whether it would stay independent. Her committee — the chief financial officer, the chief medical officer, the vice president of managed care and the vice president of quality — would argue each item; she would carry one recommendation per item into the board minutes.

## The Company and its Setting

Cascade Regional Medical Center (CRMC) was a 180-bed 501(c)(3) community hospital in Spokane, Washington. The offer valued it at 1.1 times net patient revenue and 12.2 times EBITDA, which put revenue at roughly US$240 million and EBITDA at roughly US$21.6 million. Its operating margin stood at 2.80%, against a 3.5% median for comparable non-profit hospitals published by the National Hospital Finance Council — about US$6.7 million of operating income. It held 138 days of cash on hand, against roughly 210 days at the median of the rating-agency peer set.

Commercial insurance produced 37% of revenue from about a quarter of patients and carried much of the rest of the book. Service-line margins were split in two: orthopedics earned 22% and cardiology 18%, while obstetrics lost 11%, pediatrics 14% and behavioral health 19%. Spokane County had only three labor-and-delivery units, and CRMC ran one of them.

A national integrated insurer-and-provider had announced a 90-bed hospital in the Spokane Valley, due to open in eighteen months.

## The Situation

The pressure arrived from every payer at once (see **Exhibit 1**). The region's largest commercial insurer had signaled a 3% cap on 2026 rate increases. Palouse Health Plan had offered a place in its narrow-network tier: 7% more volume at a 4% lower rate. One Medicaid managed-care plan paid 61% of the cost of the care it bought.

Federal quality programs were taking money back. CRMC sat in the Medicare readmissions penalty group, at a 1.8% payment reduction worth about US$1.1 million a year, and in the worst quartile for hospital-acquired conditions, a further 1% worth about US$0.6 million. Registered-nurse vacancy was 14.8%, and travel nurses had cost US$6.2 million in the year.

The federal 340B drug-discount program contributed US$14.6 million of drug margin, which funded much of the hospital's charity care. More than thirty manufacturers now restricted replenishment through contract pharmacies, federal auditors were active, and Congress was examining reform. A fair-market-value review of physician pay, a billing dispute under the federal surprise-billing law, an emergency-department boarding complaint and the community health needs assessment were all due.

Calloway's dashboard tracked seven measures. **Operating margin** was operating income as a share of revenue. A **quality composite** of 62 out of 100 combined readmissions, hospital-acquired conditions and patients' survey ratings. An **access and community** score of 75 measured whether the region kept its services and whether the hospital met its charitable obligations. **Days cash on hand** counted how long the hospital could pay its bills from reserves. Smaller indicators showed **value-based care readiness** at 45, **workforce health** at 55 and **open regulatory flags** — live compliance exposures — at two. A **hospital performance score**, a weighted composite of these measures that the board used, summarised them on a scale of 0 to 100.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was visible from the packet: commercial volume would leak to the new Spokane Valley hospital, the two penalties would recur, travel-nurse spending would continue, and a margin already below the median would carry a cash cushion already below it.

## What Calloway Controlled

The agenda ran in a fixed order (see **Exhibit 2**).

At the first session she would set payer posture. With the commercial insurer she could accept the 3% cap as offered, counter at 5.5% with quality data, or walk away and go out of network. On the narrow network she could stay out of the tier entirely, join for orthopedics and cardiology only, or join the tier across all lines. With the Medicaid plan she could keep it and widen charity screening, renegotiate it under standard collection rules, or exit it and collect balances aggressively.

At the second she would decide service lines and capital. For obstetrics: partner with the bidder under joint operation, close it and stabilize and transfer, or grow it through midwifery and outreach. For pediatrics: close the inpatient pediatric beds, keep full inpatient pediatric care, or shift to telehealth-and-transfer with a children's network. For a US$12 million capital envelope: an orthopedic robot plus cath-lab upgrade, a new oncology linear accelerator, a medical-surgical bed refresh only, or orthopedics, a behavioral-health clinic and cath lab.

At the third she would choose quality and risk. The vice president of quality had proposed a US$2.4 million program covering transitions of care, infection bundles and a sepsis alert; Calloway could fund the full package now, stage US$1.2 million on readmissions, or defer new quality spending. For about 18,400 attributed Medicare lives she could choose an upside-only shared-savings track, a two-sided shared-savings track, a full-risk enhanced track, a capitation model closing in 2026, or stay in fee-for-service payment. On patient experience: a US$400,000 hospitalist rounding protocol, a patient-experience team with narrower Advantage contracts, or no new program, Advantage mix unchanged.

At the fourth she would set compliance and workforce posture. On 340B: reduce dependence and diversify charity funding, expand the contract-pharmacy footprint further, or maintain it and strengthen compliance. On nursing: expand the float pool with partial retention, sign an outsourced agency agreement, hold the current staffing mix, or fund retention and pursue Magnet. On physicians: productivity-weighted pay with disputes settled quickly, fixed pay plus a quality bonus and tighter valuation files, or employment under professional-services agreements with a refreshed needs assessment.

At the fifth she would answer the letter. Recent non-profit transactions had cleared at a median of 0.9 to 1.1 times revenue; strong systems had drawn 1.3 to 1.5 times. She could run a broader market process, counter at 1.3 times with guarantees, accept the offer at 1.1 times, stay independent inside a clinical network, or partner instead with the integrated competitor. She would also set the community-benefit structure — minimum charitable compliance only, community board seats and shared governance, or a foundation with five-year service guarantees — and the communication posture: confidential until the deal closes, a town-hall process with early input, or announce the intent publicly at once.

## Closing

At 7:15 a.m. Calloway laid the payer table beside the letter of intent and wrote the number 26 at the top of a blank page. The commercial insurer's renewal deadline fell before the board's next meeting. Her committee would assemble at nine.
