# Ridgeline Freight Systems: Nothing Off the Record

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-2020 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2020 — *Nothing Off the Record* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

---

On Tuesday, 12 January 2027, Julia Brandt read the Quaybook post-mortem for the third time. Fourteen employees had seen the defect coming. Two had raised it above their own team. The day before, Ridgeline's founder and chief executive, Wes Halloran, had told the all-hands what that meant: "Fourteen people knew. We don't have a process problem. We have a courage problem." He announced that within 90 days every meeting would be recorded, everyone would rate everyone, and nothing at Ridgeline would be off the record.

Brandt was his Chief of Staff, and the rollout was hers. She had until the board meeting on 15 April 2027 to build the programme inside a budget of USD 1.2 million and to recommend whether the board should extend it, hold it or end it. Her first deliverable, due that week, was a reading of why the fourteen had stayed quiet and the one number the programme would answer to.

## The Protagonist

Brandt, 39, had joined Ridgeline in 2018 as a product manager and had run customer operations before Halloran made her Chief of Staff in 2024. The role carried no line authority. It worked as long as the founder backed her, and the leadership team tracked that backing on a 0–100 scale. In January it stood at 85.

## The Decision

The quarter held four decision points. On 12 January she would weigh the causes of the silence and choose the programme's yardstick. On 2 February she would set the design that went live. On 16 March, six weeks into the pilot, she would review it and could adjust the design. On 15 April she would take a recommendation to the board. Before each readout, independent director Marion Achebe wanted Brandt's predictions sealed in writing, to be opened beside the results.

## The Company and its Setting

Ridgeline Freight Systems, founded in Pittsburgh in 2015, sold a cloud platform that shippers and logistics providers used to book carriers, schedule dock slots and track loads. It had 640 employees, 310 of them in product and engineering, USD 118 million in annual recurring revenue growing 31% a year, and a Series D raised in 2025.

The culture was polite and consensus-seeking. Attrition was 11% a year, below the sector norm. General Counsel Abigail Moss had reminded the leadership team that Pennsylvania required every party's consent before a conversation was recorded.

Halloran had spent the holidays studying firms that recorded meetings and scored every contribution. His argument was simple: politeness had just cost Ridgeline USD 7.5 million, and a company that never said the hard thing in the room would keep paying. "If you have nothing to hide," he told Brandt, "a camera costs you nothing."

## The Situation

In November 2026, Quaybook 3.0 double-booked dock slots for nine days at peak season. Two anchor customers worth USD 6.4 million a year gave notice, and service credits cost a further USD 1.1 million. Eleven of the fourteen had described the defect correctly, in writing, in their own team channels (see **Exhibit 1**). Launch go/no-go meetings kept no written record of objections.

Four interviews sat in the file. A Payments engineer had raised it in a sprint review, was told it was the release team's call, and did not know whom there to tell. A support lead recalled that the last person to question a launch date at an all-hands was moved off the project a month later. A QA analyst had flagged it twice in a channel "nobody upstream reads." A senior engineer said: "Was I scared? Not really. I just didn't think saying it again would change anything."

The engagement survey scored "I can raise a problem without fear" at 49 out of 100, against a sector benchmark of about 70. Eighteen percent of employees had ever disagreed with a vice-president in a meeting.

The leadership team disagreed. Chief People Officer Priya Raman asked, "Before we fix the people, can we look at what happened to the ones who did speak?" VP Engineering Tomás Lindqvist warned: "My best engineers think out loud in rough drafts. Record the drafts and you will stop getting drafts." Halloran answered that every partial version he had studied had drifted back to the old habits within a year.

Halloran's draft, left unchanged, would put all 640 people under the full system: every meeting recorded, partner calls included; public ratings of people's traits; fully believability-weighted decisions; individual pay open to all; two hours of tool training; and a one-line policy saying meetings might be recorded. It would commit USD 966,000 of the budget (see **Exhibit 2**). Without any programme, Ridgeline would carry its January numbers into the next launch.

## What Brandt Controlled

For the diagnosis, she split 100 points across four causes: skill (people misread the evidence), nerve (they saw it and chose comfort), cost (speaking up felt risky or pointless) and route (no forum carried a warning to the decision). She then chose the single metric the programme would be judged on: ratings and issues logged each month, days from first sighting of a risk to someone who could act, the share of major decisions with a dissent written into the record, or the engagement index re-run quarterly.

The design had six settings, each with five levels: how much was recorded, who saw peer ratings, whether ratings addressed ideas or people, how decisions were made, what information was opened, and how many people were in scope. She also chose the training — none, two hours on the tools at USD 400 a person, or two days on giving and taking critique at USD 1,100 a person — and whether to rely on a policy notice for recordings or build a consent architecture of notices, opt-outs and legal review for USD 60,000. In March she could move any setting again.

The charter also had to say how Ridgeline would respond if the programme was challenged, from inside or outside the company.

For the board she would recommend one of four courses: extend the system company-wide by mandate from 1 May, extend it unit by unit over twelve months as units opted in, hold the current scope for a quarter, or stop. She would name the zones kept off the record: one-to-ones and coaching; HR, health and personal matters; early drafts and brainstorms; calls with customers and partners; individual pay; and launch go/no-go meetings. She would assign ownership of ratings and bias audits to the Office of the CEO, the People team, a rotating panel of three managers and an employee representative, or an external vendor. She would set hiring policy: no change; disclosure in interviews with six weeks of onboarding and an opt-out for current staff; a personality screen for comfort with critique; or a requirement that every employee sign the new principles by June or leave with a package. And she would set a stop condition: a safety-index level below which the programme rolled back, or none, as Halloran preferred.

Five measures would follow the programme: the share of risks that reached someone who could act, then 2 of 14; the safety index ("I can raise a problem without fear"), next measured by a pulse survey in week 10; regretted attrition, annualised, from 11%; legal and reputational exposure in US dollars, then zero; and founder backing. Her sealed forecasts covered the week-10 safety index, how many of the next fourteen people to see a launch risk would raise it by month 12, and month-12 regretted attrition. Achebe had said the board would read those results alongside the quality of Brandt's reasoning: her diagnosis, her metric, her forecasts and the governance she built.

## Closing

At 9:15 a.m. Brandt opened the diagnosis sheet. Halloran's numbers were already in it: 20 on skill, 70 on nerve, 5 on cost, 5 on route. The board met on 15 April.
