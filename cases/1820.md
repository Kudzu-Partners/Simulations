# Marlowe & Crest: Reputation Under Fire

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1820 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1820 — *Reputation Under Fire* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

At 16:40 on Tuesday 6 October 2026, Claire Nesbitt read an email that gave her forty-eight hours. Its sender, a reporter at a national broadsheet, planned to publish in two days an investigation alleging that the flagship £40 million public-sector programme run by Nesbitt's practice had overstated its carbon-savings figures, and that a junior analyst had been pressured to "round up" the results, quoting an anonymous employee. Nesbitt was still drafting a reply when, three hours later, an anonymous channel across her 48-person team began carrying its own account: leadership knew, read one post; heads will roll, read another. Two versions of the same story were forming at once, one public, one internal, and Nesbitt had two days to decide what each would be told.

## The Protagonist

Claire Nesbitt, 44, had led the Energy Transition Practice since 2020, the year after a burnout episode drove three senior consultants out in one quarter and left the practice's standing inside Marlowe & Crest at its lowest point. She rebuilt it around one idea: people who felt safe raising a problem caught it before a client did. Six years on, the practice was the firm's fastest-growing unit, with an engagement score of 78, and Nesbitt reported to the Managing Partner, who had backed her rebuild publicly. The culture she had built was the reason the leak had reached an anonymous channel rather than staying silent — and the thing the next two days would test most directly.

## The Decision

Nesbitt's task did not end with a statement to the press. Over the following weeks she would decide, in sequence across four stages (see Exhibit 2), how to characterise the crisis to her leadership group and what to establish before saying anything publicly; what public posture to take and what to tell its public-sector clients before they read about it themselves; how much, and how, to tell her own 48 people while a formal review ran, and how to treat the named analyst while it did; and, once the review concluded, how to keep two wavering senior consultants, resolve the analyst's situation, and commit to changes that would stop a repeat.

## The Company and its Setting

Marlowe & Crest was a 600-person management and engineering consultancy headquartered in Manchester. It advised infrastructure and energy clients, billed roughly £92 million a year at an 18% operating margin, and had grown its reputation through word of mouth — a fact that made its reputation for rigour and integrity close to its only real asset.

The Energy Transition Practice, the 48-person unit Nesbitt led, was the firm's fastest-growing and most visible team, running three marquee public-sector programmes at once. Its flagship engagement — the £40 million programme now under scrutiny — was a multi-year contract far larger than anything else in the practice's portfolio; the three public-sector clients together were worth £26 million a year. The practice's culture of speaking openly about problems, which Nesbitt had spent six years building, was not a soft asset at the edge of the business — it was a large part of why clients trusted its numbers at all.

## The Situation

Part of the allegation touched a genuine methodology dispute on one workstream; part of it was already travelling as rumour. The dashboard was certain, even where the allegation was not: the Reputation Index (external standing and client trust, 0 to 100) stood at 55; Engagement, 78 before the email arrived, was forecast to fall below 60 within days if unmanaged; Rumour Spread — the share of the 48-person team that had absorbed the anonymous channel's story — was already 30% after three hours, forecast to reach roughly 70% within a day; and Wellbeing & Trust, covering the analyst's and the team's sense of safety and fair treatment, stood at 50 (see Exhibit 1).

The named analyst, identifiable to colleagues from the anonymous post, was reported to be considering resignation before the story even ran. Two senior consultants, whose combined £1.4 million of annual billing made them among the practice's most valuable people, had signalled they would leave if the firm spun the story or scapegoated the analyst. The allegation as published would name the practice, not yet the firm. If Nesbitt's public statements and what her people believed about the firm's handling pulled apart, the leak would widen, the story would end up naming the firm instead of the practice, and the two sets of numbers would move together in the same direction. Left alone, the trajectory already visible in the dashboard — rumour toward 70%, engagement under 60, a resignation, two departures — would simply run its course.

## What Nesbitt Controlled

Before any public word, Nesbitt had to frame the crisis for her leadership group: one connected problem, outside story and inside reaction feeding each other; a press matter to handle first, the internal conversation left for the following week; an internal disciplinary question about who had leaked; or a matter not warranting a distinct response at all. She also had to decide what to establish about the allegation: treat it as unresolved and separate what was confirmed, disputed and unknown, including a quiet check on the analyst's account; assume it false and prepare a rebuttal; assume it true and concede pre-emptively; or leave it uninvestigated. A further setting fixed, as a percentage, how much of her team's early attention went to the internal side rather than the press alone.

With the clock running, she had to choose the public posture: confirm an independent review and commit to publishing its findings; issue a limited statement taking the allegation seriously without detail; issue a flat, categorical denial ahead of the review's conclusion; or decline to comment and let the story run unanswered. She also had to decide what to tell the three public-sector clients beforehand — a pre-brief with a remediation plan; a bare reassurance without detail; or nothing, leaving them to learn of it from the newspaper — and how tightly the public position had to match what the internal review could later support.

Once the story ran, she had to decide what to tell her 48 people, and when: an early account of the allegation and the review under way; an explanation held back until after the story ran; silence; or full disclosure of every name and suspicion regardless of proof. The anonymous channel needed its own decision — answer its rumours directly and invite questions; monitor quietly while briefing team leads to respond in person; trace and warn whoever was posting; or leave it alone — as did the analyst: support and a fair process that neither blamed nor pre-judged; distancing leadership from them; or settling the question publicly, either way, before the review concluded. A further setting fixed how much she disclosed internally, from near-total silence to broadcasting everything she knew.

Once the review concluded, she had to decide what to offer the two wavering seniors: commitments about process and a real voice in what changed, without guaranteeing an outcome she did not control; a broad guarantee that no one would face consequences, paired with a promise of a specific outcome; or no concrete commitment at all — an acknowledgement of their concerns with no specifics on process, voice, or outcome. The analyst's case needed its own decision — act on the evidence, protect the person, and name the lesson for the practice; resolve it quietly and tell the team nothing; or discipline visibly — alongside a governance fix (independent sign-off, an audit trail, a protected route for raising concerns; an updated written policy and an all-hands briefing; or no structural change) and a percentage decision on wellbeing and re-engagement investment for the weeks ahead.

## Closing

At 16:55 Nesbitt closed the email and opened a blank page instead. The journalist's clock read forty-seven hours and change. Somewhere in the building, forty-eight people were already reading something she had not written, about a decision she had not yet made.
