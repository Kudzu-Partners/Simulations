## Abstract

The Chief Strategy Officer at the case-universe Mexico-City-headquartered Mexican-Brazilian mid-cap technology-services firm — approximately USD 480m in 2025 revenue, 2,400 employees, a legacy customer book representing 60% of revenue and the entire commission structure, a digital-transformation strategy approved by the board in January 2026 with three pillars (platform modernisation, industry verticalisation and talent-density upgrade), an ERP migration already eighteen months in and twelve months from cutover, and a Q1 2026 read-out showing four departments each interpreting the same strategy differently — had been asked, in the March 2026 CEO directive, to run the April 23 three-round execution-cascade exercise and deliver a cascade commitment the quarterly business-review rhythm could carry. The strategy was not the problem. The executive endorsement was not the problem. The problem was that Sales was reading the strategy as protect-the-legacy-book-and-also-sell-the-new-platform, Operations was reading digital-transformation as buy-the-new-software, Product was gold-plating a feature set the market had not requested, and People was telling everyone the talent market could not deliver the new capability profile before Q4. Every department said yes to the strategy and meant something different. The case opens at 2:42 p.m. on Thursday, April 23, 2026, at the firm's Mexico City strategy office as **Renato Campos-Albuquerque**, a 45-year-old Chief Strategy Officer, prepares the three-round cascade.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Design the Round-1 OKR cascade across Product, Sales, Operations and People — three Objectives per department, three Key Results each — and articulate why the coherence checker flagging seven conflicts on the first pass is the expected signal rather than a team-dysfunction signal, and why the conflict between Sales' 'protect existing revenue' and Product's 'sunset legacy SKUs' is the single most diagnostic conflict of the cascade.
2. Reallocate the budget from the 70/30 legacy/strategic starting mix toward the 40/60 target and articulate why every million moved has a name attached, why every name will be in the Round-2 room, and why the budget reallocation is the single strongest signal of whether the strategy will survive the Q2 QBR.
3. Navigate the Round-2 stakeholder pushback — Sales VP defending the legacy book, Operations citing the ERP migration, Product arguing scope, People citing talent-market constraints — and articulate why negotiation without strategic concession is the hard move, why caving loses five points of strategic coherence per concession, and why the quiet half of execution is the incentive-design half.
4. Redesign the sales commission, bonus pool and functional-KPI weighting so the strategic Key Results rather than the legacy functional KPIs carry the compensation signal, and articulate why a commission plan from before the strategy rewards exactly the behaviour the strategy is trying to stop and why a three-year phase-in of the strategic weighting is the durability move.
5. Commit to the Round-3 quarterly business-review rhythm — three KRs on track, two off, one red, variance explained without blaming, budget reallocated without rewriting strategy, one initiative killed — and articulate why the rhythm of execution rather than the clarity of the plan is what catches drift before drift becomes fatal.

On the afternoon of **Thursday, April 23, 2026**, **Renato Campos-Albuquerque** arrived at the firm's Mexico City strategy office at 2:42 p.m., opened the ExecutionGap workbook his strategy-execution director had finalised at 11:05 p.m. the night before, and re-read the sentence he had pinned to his execution-brief wall: *"Strategy is what the fourth department does in March when I am not watching."*

---

## The Protagonist

Campos-Albuquerque, 45, had been Chief Strategy Officer since 2024, had spent thirteen years across São Paulo and Mexico City — four at BCG, five at a Brazilian technology-services firm as Head of Commercial Strategy, and four in a Mexico City digital-transformation consultancy where he had led three enterprise OKR rollouts. In 2024 he authored the firm's internal playbook *El Cuarto Departamento*, which framed execution as a fourth-department test rather than an executive-team test. The CEO had assigned him the April exercise on three considerations: his command of OKR cascades and incentive redesign; his comfort calling the 70/30-to-40/60 reallocation a binding commitment rather than a directional aim; and his willingness to kill initiatives mid-cycle.

---

## The Decision

Campos-Albuquerque had to commit, by the 5:00 p.m. Executive Committee pre-brief, the three-round cascade and the QBR rhythm.

1. **Round 1 — OKR cascade.** Three Objectives × three Key Results per department. Coherence checker runs across Product, Sales, Operations, People. Seven conflicts surfaced first pass. Five resolved by the end of Round 1; two escalated.
2. **Budget reallocation.** Mix moves from 70/30 legacy/strategic to 40/60 over three quarters. Every million moved is name-attached and carries a Round-2 defence.
3. **Round 2 — Stakeholder pushback.** Sales VP, Operations, Product and People each push back on the cascade. Negotiation without strategic concession. The two escalated conflicts resolved.
4. **Incentive redesign.** Sales commission re-weighted 60/40 strategic/legacy KPIs over three years. Bonus pool 70% tied to strategic KRs. Functional KPIs capped at 40% of variable pay.
5. **Round 3 — QBR.** Quarterly rhythm: three KRs on track, two off, one red. Variance explained. Budget reallocated within the 40/60 envelope. One initiative killed. The kill is the signal.

## Industry and Market Context

The 1990–2025 strategy-execution literature produced three findings the exercise had to respect. First, OKR cascades that ran coherence audits across multiple departments produced documented three-year strategy-realisation rates approximately 45–60% higher than sequentially-defined department OKRs on comparable 2015–2024 mid-cap cycles. Second, sales-commission redesign synchronised with strategic Key Results produced documented behaviour-change readings roughly 2.4× those of strategy-communication-only interventions. Third, QBR rhythms with pre-committed variance-explanation templates and budget-reallocation authority produced documented drift-correction rates approximately 50% higher than annual-review rhythms.

The post-2010 Balanced-Scorecard and OKR literature added two findings. Budget reallocation from legacy to strategic buckets was the single strongest mid-cap correlate of three-year strategy survival. And incentive-redesign lag — the time between strategy announcement and commission-plan change — was the single strongest correlate of strategy failure, with a six-month lag producing a 60% failure-rate uplift.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The January 2026 board-approved strategy was communicated to the executive team in January and cascaded to departments in February. The Q1 read-out in April showed four departments each interpreting the strategy differently. The March CEO directive asked for the April 23 execution-cascade exercise.

### What Campos-Albuquerque knew

Sales protects the commission-producing legacy book; Operations reads digital-transformation as software procurement; Product gold-plates; People cannot hire the new capability before Q4 without a broader compensation move; the 70/30 mix cannot deliver the three-pillar strategy; the commission plan pays for the behaviour the strategy is trying to stop.

### What he did not know

Whether the Sales VP would accept the 60/40 commission re-weighting with a three-year phase-in or require a longer phase; whether the ERP-migration-in-flight would absorb the digital-transformation resource demand or collide with it; whether Product would accept the scope-reduction directive; whether the talent-market hiring friction was a binding constraint or a negotiated one; and whether the QBR rhythm would survive the Q3 sales-quota-miss scenario.

### What others were saying

**The CEO**: *"I do not need more strategy. I need the fourth department to do what we said in March."*

**The CFO**: *"The 40/60 mix is binding. The 70/30 mix is the 2025 actual. The path is what the exercise has to produce."*

**The VP Sales**: *"The legacy book pays your salary. Move the commission faster than the customers move and you lose the book and the salesforce in the same quarter."*

**The COO**: *"The ERP migration is the other train on the track. Either the two trains share the rail or one of them derails."*

**The CPO (Chief People Officer)**: *"The talent market cannot deliver platform engineers at the current compensation band. Either the band moves or the hiring timeline slips a quarter."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Campos-Albuquerque had narrowed the space to three commitment postures.

- **Option A — Three-round cascade with 40/60 reallocation, commission redesign and QBR rhythm (Recommended):** OKR coherence, budget commitment, incentive-signal change, quarterly rhythm with reallocation authority and kill-discipline. Recommended.
- **Option B — Strategy re-announcement with minor OKR refresh and compensation deferred to 2027:** rejected — the Q1 read-out is the re-announcement moment; deferring compensation is the single move most correlated with strategy failure in the literature.
- **Option C — Full reorganisation that collapses Sales and Operations into a single commercial operating model:** rejected — the 2026 cycle cannot absorb both the ERP migration and a reorganisation; the reorganisation becomes the story and the strategy becomes the footer.

His private view was that Option A was the right commitment and that the round most correlated with three-year strategy realisation was Round 3 — the QBR rhythm — because the QBR was the round where drift was either caught early or allowed to compound.
