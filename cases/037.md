## Abstract

Atria Goods, a two-person direct-to-consumer sleep-and-recovery accessories brand founded in January 2026, entered Month 1 of its launch window with USD 150,000 in the bank, zero customers, zero revenue, a single hero SKU (a weighted magnesium-infused sleep mask retailing between USD 38 and USD 69 depending on pricing decision), and a twelve-month mandate set by its only two investors — the founder herself and a single angel investor — to reach unit-level profitability before the cash ran out. Four decisions would reset every month: marketing budget, average selling price, funnel split across top-, middle-, and bottom-of-funnel traffic, and fulfillment model. The founder, Lina Saavedra, had spent February and March building the product, the supply chain, and the first version of the website. On April 22, 2026, she would launch. The case opens on April 19 as she drafts the Month-1 decision package for her first board check-in with the angel investor on April 26.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Apply the CAC / LTV / payback-period framework to a concrete D2C launch-month decision.
2. Evaluate the trade-off between top-of-funnel awareness spend and bottom-of-funnel conversion spend against a finite twelve-month runway.
3. Analyze the fulfillment-model decision (self vs 3PL vs drop-ship) as a working-capital-and-margin interaction, not as a pure operations choice.
4. Diagnose the compounding effect of the Month-1 price point on the Month-3 CAC payback and the Month-12 LTV:CAC.
5. Recommend a Month-1 package and articulate the two events in Months 3–6 that would force a re-tune.

On the evening of **Sunday, April 19, 2026**, **Lina Saavedra** sat at her kitchen table in Brooklyn and read, for the sixth time, the one-page launch memo she had written to herself at 2:47 p.m. The memo carried six numbers in bold: *Cash USD 150,000. Runway 12 months. Hero SKU landed cost USD 12.40. First order inventory 1,200 units. Angel check-in April 26. Target CAC ≤ USD 35 by Month 6.* Below those numbers, she had written two sentences in smaller handwriting: *"CAC is a sequence, not a point. Choose the first week as if it decides Month 6."*

## The Protagonist

Lina Saavedra, 34, had founded Atria Goods in January 2026. A former product manager at a large U.S. consumer-subscription company, she had spent six years building and launching three sleep-and-recovery subscription products before leaving to start her own brand. She held an undergraduate degree in industrial design from the Universidad de los Andes in Bogotá and a master's in integrated marketing from NYU. Her angel investor, who had invested USD 80,000 of the USD 150,000 on an uncapped SAFE in February, was a former colleague who had been clear in the term-sheet conversation: *"I want to see your first twelve months of decisions. I am not interested in the product arguments. I am interested in the CAC argument."*

---

## The Decision

Saavedra had to decide, by 9:00 a.m. Friday April 24, the Month-1 package: four decisions the simulation would treat as Round 1 inputs.

1. **Marketing Budget (USD 2K – 20K).** The share of the USD 150,000 cash balance deployed to paid acquisition in April. Below USD 6,000 the brand could not expect to reach statistically meaningful conversion data within 30 days; above USD 12,000 the cash would compress the runway below 10 months.
2. **Average Selling Price (USD 38 – 69).** Five pricing bands were on the table, each with a corresponding landed-cost-to-ASP ratio, competitor-pricing adjacency, and shipping-inclusion decision. The USD 59 band carried the best pre-launch A/B-tested conversion and the cleanest CAC arithmetic; the USD 69 band pushed ASP up but depended on a premium brand story she had not yet told.
3. **Funnel Split (TOFU / MOFU / BOFU, summing to 100%).** Three archetypal splits: *Discovery-Heavy* 70/20/10, *Balanced* 50/30/20, *Conversion-Heavy* 30/30/40. The first built brand equity at the cost of near-term CAC; the last bought customers at acceptable CAC but starved the top of the funnel for Month 3 onwards.
4. **Fulfillment Model (Self / 3PL / Drop-Ship).** Self-fulfillment from Saavedra's Brooklyn apartment (possible up to approximately 400 units/month) preserved margin but bottlenecked Month-4 scale. A 3PL (third-party logistics) partner at USD 3.80 per order added visibility and scale at a 280bp gross-margin cost. Drop-shipping through a contracted manufacturer preserved cash but lengthened delivery to 8–12 days and hurt the first-cohort experience.

The four decisions would reset monthly. The angel investor's April 26 check-in would hear the Month-1 package first, and would price every later conversation against whether the package survived the Month 3 and Month 6 tests.

## Company Background

Atria Goods had been incorporated as a Delaware LLC on January 14, 2026. Saavedra held 82% of the equity; the angel's SAFE was modeled to convert at the next priced round into approximately 12%; the remaining 6% was reserved for future employees. The company had two employees — Saavedra and a part-time creative contractor who split her time across three brands — and a pre-launch e-mail list of 840 warm prospects accumulated through Saavedra's personal network and three product-research posts on a sleep-focused newsletter.

The hero SKU was a weighted magnesium-infused sleep mask, manufactured in a contract-manufacturing facility in Shenzhen at a landed cost of USD 12.40 per unit (including inbound freight and duty). The minimum order quantity had been 1,200 units; the next order would be required by early July. Secondary SKUs — a travel pouch, a complementary pillow spray, and a branded cotton carry bag — were in the product pipeline but not committed.

The target customer, identified through four months of pre-launch research, was the 28–44-year-old high-sleep-intent consumer with household income above USD 85,000, concentrated in U.S. metropolitan areas and — with a secondary beachhead — in Mexico City, Bogotá, and Buenos Aires. The e-mail list was 71% U.S.-based, 22% Latin American, and 7% elsewhere.

## Industry and Market Context

The D2C category entered 2026 under five compounding pressures. First, CAC had inflated across the three-year window to 2023 by approximately 60% on Meta and 120% on Google in the consumer-goods categories, before stabilizing in 2024–2025 at elevated levels. Second, the iOS privacy framework and the subsequent death of third-party cookies had materially degraded attribution and forced most D2C brands to re-instrument their measurement using a combination of Meta Conversions API, post-purchase surveys, and Media-Mix Modeling. Third, the cost-per-mille on Meta had risen an additional 18–22% in Q1 2026 as a cohort of well-funded new entrants had entered. Fourth, the 3PL market in the U.S. had matured, with plug-and-play onboarding and order-level pricing that was now economic for brands at approximately 400 monthly orders. Fifth, micro-influencer organic virality had returned as a meaningful customer-acquisition channel, after the 2023 retreat — with the median viral outcome producing a 2.0–2.5x weekly traffic spike.

Empirically, D2C brands that had, in the 2020–2024 cohort, reached an LTV:CAC ratio of 2.5x within 12 months had a 68% probability of reaching profitability by Month 18; those that had failed to reach 2.0x within 12 months had a 12% probability of reaching profitability before a capital raise.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Saavedra had bootstrapped through January and February on USD 70,000 of her own savings and a USD 80,000 angel check. The product had shipped from Shenzhen on March 12, cleared U.S. customs on April 8, and arrived at her Brooklyn apartment on April 15. The e-mail list of 840 had been warmed for six weeks. The launch was set for Tuesday April 22, 10:00 a.m. Eastern.

### What Saavedra knew

Her pre-launch model had simulated four Month-1 packages. A conservative package (budget USD 6K, price USD 59, funnel 70/20/10, fulfillment Drop-Ship) produced approximately 110 first-month customers, CAC USD 55, LTV:CAC of 0.8x, and runway 11 months. A balanced package (budget USD 8K, price USD 59, funnel 50/30/20, fulfillment Self) produced 180 customers, CAC USD 44, LTV:CAC 1.1x, runway 11.5 months, and a Month-4 fulfillment-capacity risk. An aggressive package (budget USD 14K, price USD 49, funnel 30/30/40, fulfillment 3PL) produced 340 customers, CAC USD 41, LTV:CAC 1.4x, runway 10 months, margin compression 280bp. A premium package (budget USD 8K, price USD 69, funnel 60/25/15, fulfillment Self) produced 120 customers, CAC USD 65, LTV:CAC 1.2x, runway 11 months, brand-premium optionality.

### What she did not know

Whether the micro-influencer partnership she had seeded in Month 0 would convert to a viral Month-3 or Month-4 event; whether Meta's Q2 CPM cycle would come in at the forecast or 20% above it; and whether the angel investor would hear the balanced package as "disciplined" or as "not ambitious enough."

### What others were saying

**Marcus Orellana**, angel investor, had written in an email on April 16: *"I do not want to see a heroic launch. I want to see a CAC arithmetic that survives Month 3."*

**Carolina Duque**, the part-time creative contractor, argued for the **premium package**: "Go USD 69 with a premium narrative. Raise the first cohort on story, not discount. The 3PL and the aggressive funnel can wait."

**A fulfillment advisor** (a former colleague at a large 3PL) had told Saavedra on April 17: *"Self-fulfill until Month 3. 3PL onboarding eats three weeks. Do not waste Month 1 on warehouse operations when you do not yet have a CAC that warrants scale."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Saavedra had narrowed the space to three concrete Month-1 packages.

- **Option A — Balanced Test (the Orellana-advisor midpoint):** Budget USD 8,000, price USD 59, funnel 50/30/20, fulfillment Self. Projected Month-1 CAC USD 44, customers 180, LTV:CAC 1.1x, runway 11.5 months. Month-4 fulfillment hand-off to 3PL pre-built.

- **Option B — Premium Narrative (the Duque plan):** Budget USD 8,000, price USD 69, funnel 60/25/15, fulfillment Self. Projected CAC USD 65, customers 120, LTV:CAC 1.2x, runway 11 months. Brand-premium optionality preserved; conversion risk elevated.

- **Option C — Aggressive Acquisition:** Budget USD 14,000, price USD 49, funnel 30/30/40, fulfillment 3PL (from launch). Projected CAC USD 41, customers 340, LTV:CAC 1.4x, runway 10 months. Margin compression 280bp; 3PL onboarding risk in the first three weeks.

Saavedra's own instinct, not yet shared with Orellana, was that Option A gave her the longest runway for the Month-3 and Month-4 learning events that actually drove 2026 outcomes — the expected viral event, the iOS attribution cycle, and the first realistic read on LTV. She had drafted, in her notebook, a single sentence for the April 26 angel call: *"A D2C brand is not a product. It is a twelve-month sequence of decisions about CAC."*
