# Rheinwerk Komponenten AG: Thirty Days to the Covenant Test

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1903 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1903 — *Risk on the Balance Sheet* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 14 September 2026, Katrin Vollmer laid three documents side by side on the table in her office in Wetzlar and read them as one quarter rather than three. The first was the lead bank's quarterly covenant notice, confirming the next leverage test for 15 October. The second was a treasury memorandum putting the group's twelve-month earnings Value-at-Risk at EUR 24 million — 40% of last year's EBITDA. The third was a receivables summary for Rheinwerk's largest customer, a tier-one powertrain assembler that had moved its payment terms from 45 to 75 days in June.

Vollmer had thirty days. On 15 October the syndicate would measure Rheinwerk Komponenten AG's net debt against its EBITDA and compare the result to a covenant ceiling of 3.75 times. Her board expected a risk and capital plan before that date: what the group would spend to protect itself, what it would do with its plants, and what it would sign with its lenders.

## The Protagonist

Vollmer, 49, had been Group Chief Financial Officer since 2022. She had joined in 2016 to run financial planning, after eleven years in corporate banking in Düsseldorf, most of it lending to family-owned industrials. The EUR 180 million syndicated loan that carried the 3.75x ceiling was hers: she had negotiated it in 2023, accepting the tighter covenant for a lower margin when leverage stood at 2.8x. Group treasury and risk reported to her. The founding family, still the majority holder, had said in July that it would not put in new equity.

## The Decision

Vollmer had to decide how to spend a fixed EUR 12 million that the board had earmarked for risk mitigation rather than growth, which of three restructuring paths to recommend, and what terms to accept from the syndicate — and to present all three as one plan on 15 October.

## The Company and its Setting

Rheinwerk Komponenten AG, founded in Wetzlar in 1971, machined precision aluminium and steel components — housings, brackets and thermal-management parts — for combustion and electric powertrains. It supplied tier-one manufacturers in Germany, France and Central Europe from three plants: Wetzlar; Plzeň, in Czechia; and a small finishing site in Hungary. The group employed 2,400 people and reported 2025 revenue of EUR 540 million with EBITDA of EUR 59.4 million, an 11% margin.

Roughly 30% of revenue was invoiced in currencies other than the euro, and aluminium was bought on contracts indexed to the metal exchange. Some 38% of revenue still came from combustion platforms that Rheinwerk's customers had publicly committed to winding down; internal planning assumed those volumes would fall 8–12% a year from 2027. The Hungary site was sub-scale and had run at negative EBITDA for two years.

## The Situation

Net debt stood at EUR 210 million against EUR 59.4 million of EBITDA; leverage on the covenant definition was 3.50 times, a quarter of a turn below the ceiling (see **Exhibit 1**). A breach did not require a missed payment: it gave the syndicate the right to accelerate the EUR 180 million loan and reprice every other facility.

Cash was the tighter constraint. A EUR 60 million bond tranche matured in nine months. Unrestricted cash was EUR 28 million; the committed revolving facility of EUR 45 million was drawn to EUR 31 million. First-half free cash flow had turned negative at –EUR 4 million, mostly because the terms change had trapped about EUR 22 million in receivables. Treasury reported liquidity headroom — twelve-month sources minus twelve-month uses — at EUR 11 million, against a board floor of EUR 20 million.

The earnings side was equally exposed. The aluminium index had risen 18% and the book was hedged only one month out. The foreign-currency book was unhedged; treasury put the annual translation swing at EUR 7–9 million. Together, the twelve-month Value-at-Risk of EUR 24 million was the loss on EBITDA that treasury expected to be exceeded in one year out of twenty. The board's stated tolerance was EUR 15 million.

Rheinwerk also tracked an internal resilience index — a 0-to-100 measure of the group's capacity to keep funding the electric-vehicle transition through a downturn. It read 30.

At Friday's risk meeting the group treasurer had made one observation that Vollmer had not resolved. The customer that had stretched payment terms was also the largest single source of non-euro invoicing. If that customer's region weakened, Rheinwerk would take the currency loss and the late payment in the same quarter. Whether that changed the EUR 24 million estimate, nobody had yet worked out.

If nothing was tabled by 15 October, the syndicate's stated procedure was to move Rheinwerk to watch status, reprice the revolver, and treat refinancing of the EUR 60 million bond at par as an open question.

## What Vollmer Controlled

Four sets of choices sat in front of her.

The register came first. She could carry the exposures as separate lines, each with its own euro impact; she could record the treasurer's observation in the commentary and leave the EUR 24 million estimate as it stood; or she could re-estimate the figure on the assumption that the currency loss and the late payment arrived together. She had to name one exposure as the most dangerous — the bond maturity, the aluminium position, or the 38% of revenue on combustion platforms — and decide how much of the thirty days to spend building the register at all.

The EUR 12 million came second, and had to be committed in full. An aluminium collar would cap the metal price for a modest premium and leave upside exposure open. Layered currency hedges would cover the non-euro translation exposure in tranches rather than at one rate. A precautionary reserve, held as cash or as a committed standby line, would sit against the bond maturity. Receivables factoring would release part of the EUR 22 million trapped by the new payment terms. Credit insurance on the largest customer would convert that counterparty exposure into an annual fee. Vollmer also had to say which measure the plan protected first if the money ran short: the earnings figure, the cash figure, or both together.

Third was the restructuring path (see **Exhibit 2**). Defend meant refinancing the bond, keeping all three plants and investing EUR 25 million to convert combustion lines to electric-vehicle parts — with a further choice about whether to commit that capital before the October test or stage it after. Reshape meant closing the Hungary site at a one-off cash cost of EUR 8 million to recover EUR 3 million of EBITDA. Deleverage meant a sale-and-leaseback of Plzeň, raising EUR 55 million and taking leverage to 2.6 times, against a plant the engineering team rated as the group's best fit for electric-vehicle work. Separately, she had to state whether the combustion decline belonged in the current plan or in its later years.

Last was the credit committee. The syndicate had signalled it would grant a covenant waiver only against a higher margin and an amortisation schedule; the indicated step-up of 200 basis points on EUR 180 million was worth about EUR 3.6 million a year. Vollmer could accept the terms, negotiate them, or decline the waiver and rely on her own plan to stay inside the covenant. She had to choose what to put at the centre of the presentation — the liquidity bridge through the bond maturity with pro-forma leverage at the next two tests, the electric-vehicle conversion case with the covenant arithmetic annexed, or an assurance that the quarter had been an anomaly — and how hard to press on the margin.

The plan would be judged on four numbers: the earnings Value-at-Risk, the liquidity headroom, pro-forma leverage at the next test, and the resilience index.

## Closing

At 7:15 that evening Vollmer wrote the four numbers on a single sheet — 24, 11, 3.50, 30 — and beside them the numbers the board had asked for: 15, 20, under 3.75, higher. The syndicate would meet on 15 October. She had thirty days, EUR 12 million, and three plants.
