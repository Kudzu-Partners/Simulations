# Rheinmain Graduate School of Business: Quality at Scale

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1907 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1907 — *Quality at Scale — RGSB's Accreditation Crunch* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, March 9, 2026, in her second week as Vice President for Academic Affairs at Rheinmain Graduate School of Business, Marlene Ostwald read two documents that had arrived four days apart and saw that they were about the same eight people. The first was an informal "areas of concern" memo from the AACSB peer-review team that had visited in January. Two of the school's twelve programs had slipped below the qualified-faculty coverage bar; executive teaching days were not being counted against faculty workload; and the executive-education portfolio, now 31% of RGSB's revenue, carried no evidence of learning at all. The second was a countersigned contract for a EUR 6.2 million custom program for a DAX-listed industrial group, to launch in sixteen weeks and deliverable only by eight senior professors, already the most heavily loaded academics in the building.

Ostwald had three weeks to tell the client whether the program would run as sold, and six weeks to table a portfolio-and-quality plan to the Rector and the chair of the Accreditation Steering Committee. The EQUIS mid-cycle review was eight months out. The AACSB Continuous Improvement Review was fourteen.

## The Protagonist

Marlene Ostwald, 49, held a doctorate in operations management, had joined RGSB in 2013, run the part-time Master portfolio for six years and chaired the curriculum committee before the Rectorate appointed her Vice President for Academic Affairs in February 2026. Her appointment letter named the two accreditation visits as her mandate and nothing else.

As a program director she had twice signed workload sheets recording a full load for colleagues who were also delivering executive days that appeared on no sheet. Whatever she took to the Rectorate had to survive those signatures.

## The Decision

Ostwald had to decide who would teach what at RGSB for the coming academic year, what evidence of learning the school would commit to producing and at what cost, whether the EUR 6.2 million program would run as sold, and what she would say about all of it in six weeks.

## The Company and its Setting

RGSB was a private, AACSB-accredited business school in Frankfurt am Main with 2,400 students, 88 core faculty and roughly 140 adjuncts and practitioners on its rosters. Revenue was EUR 96 million and the school ran a 9% operating surplus, about EUR 8.6 million, which funded research time, scholarships and a digital campus (see **Exhibit 1**).

Executive Education was the fastest-growing unit, its contribution margin near 42%, well above the degree portfolio's. The Dean's office had funded four years of growth on that margin by drawing the same senior research faculty into more executive days each year.

Both accrediting bodies asked the same two questions: were the people teaching each program qualified, and could the school show that students learned what the program promised. On the first, RGSB's bar was that at least 90% of a program's teaching be delivered by faculty classed as scholarly academics or practice academics; two of twelve programs sat below it. On the second, the degree programs had learning goals and measures; the executive portfolio had a participant net-promoter score of 72 and nothing else.

## The Situation

The workload arithmetic sat underneath everything. RGSB's 22 most research-active professors averaged 1.34 times the 100% norm once executive days were counted; six sat above 1.5 times. Three had told the Dean of Faculty they would not renew executive commitments for the coming year, and one held an external offer (see **Exhibit 2**).

The 1.34 figure was not official. The published workload model counted only a fraction of executive days; the Head of Quality Assurance had rebuilt it counting all of them, and it was her version Ostwald was reading.

Money pulled the other way. The program carried roughly EUR 2.6 million of contribution on EUR 6.2 million of value. The Director of Finance noted that the surplus paid for research buy-outs and scholarships, and that a regime adding four assessments and two extra faculty days per executive program would take the unit's contribution margin from 42% toward 19%.

The Rectorate had asked Ostwald to report against four measures (see **Exhibit 1**). Three ran on a 0-to-100 scale: accreditation readiness, standing with both bodies as driven by qualified-faculty coverage, at 30; the quality system, a loop from learning goals to direct measures to a documented change in the program, at 15; faculty sustainability, workload held below an agreed ceiling with attrition risk contained, at 35. The fourth was the surplus.

On the current trajectory nothing on that dashboard moved. A conditions outcome from EQUIS was published, read by corporate clients and ranking bodies, and carried into the AACSB review.

The building did not agree on what the week meant. The Dean of Faculty read it as a shortage: post the open senior lines and hire out of it, though a research-active professor took about eleven months to recruit. The Director of Executive Education read it as a growth question — back the highest-margin unit, fund the gaps from what it earned. The Head of Quality Assurance read it as a documentation exercise that would satisfy the review team. The Director of Finance read it as two problems at once, a staffing gap and a governance gap, needing separate remedies. Ostwald had committed to none of the four.

## What Ostwald Controlled

She controlled four things.

In the diagnosis she fixed which of the four readings the school would work from. She named the most dangerous exposure ahead of the two visits: the loss of a marquee corporate client, the qualified-faculty shortfall alone, an unevidenced portfolio producing a published conditions outcome, or nothing that could not wait. And she set what share of executive teaching days, from none to all, the workload model would count.

In the allocation she set how many of the 22 senior professors carried executive teaching, from none to all of them. She committed an adjunct and practitioner budget of EUR 0.5 million, EUR 1.5 million or EUR 3.0 million. She chose how the coverage gaps in the degree programs were closed: by moving core courses to practitioner faculty, the cheapest route and the one that freed seniors for executive work; by posting the open senior lines and waiting out the eleven-month recruitment for permanent research-active staff; or by combining co-teaching, practitioner appointments, a research buy-out and lines started in parallel. And she ring-fenced a share of tenure-track research time, from none to all of it.

On the program she chose to run it at full scope as sold, to decline it, or to re-scope it into phased cohorts with blended delivery, a designated academic director and practitioner faculty alongside. She set the executive portfolio's minimum quality standard at none for now, on the strength of the participant score of 72; at learning goals with one direct measure per program and a closing-the-loop cadence; or at four assessments and two extra faculty days per program, for measurement beyond challenge. And she set a workload ceiling, as a multiple of the norm, anywhere between 1.00 and 2.00.

For the brief she chose the rule the Rectorate would adopt the next time executive demand collided with senior supply: the highest-contribution claim wins, sending scarce senior capacity to where it earns most; the Dean's office settles each case as it arises, judging each on its own facts; or a written policy protects coverage and the ceiling first, staffing executive growth from what remains. She chose how to present it: leading with the client win and the surplus, contesting the memo's findings, or setting out coverage, the loop, the ceiling and the surplus alongside what had been given up. She chose whether to build slack for a departure in month three, to note that risk without building for it, or to plan on the eight staying. And she chose how firmly to state, before the Rector, her position on whether academic quality was a system to be governed or a by-product of the budget.

## Closing

On the Friday Ostwald wrote the eight names on one side of a sheet and the twelve programs on the other, and drew lines between them until she ran out of professors. The client wanted an answer in three weeks; the Rector wanted the plan in six. She had 88 core faculty, EUR 8.6 million of surplus, and two review teams already writing.
