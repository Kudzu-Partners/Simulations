# Helio Verde: Three Rooms in Eleven Days

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1504 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1504 — *CEO Communications: Three Rooms, One Composition* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

The message reached Sofía Carballo, Helio Verde's head of investor relations, on the Wednesday morning a financial wire service ran a story setting the departure of a senior regional executive beside the timing of the company's quarterly results. It came from Christopher Wen, an analyst at Greycap, and it asked for a ruling rather than a comment: *"Will you confirm or deny that the GM-CA-North departure relates to financial irregularities, internal control weakness, or compliance issues that may be a Q3 driver. If not now, on the call."*

Lucía Arenas-Beltrán, eight months into the job as chief executive of Helio Verde S.A., had eleven business days in which to speak in three rooms: the third-quarter earnings call on Tuesday, a town hall with the company's 200 senior leaders on Thursday, and the annual all-hands address to 15,000 employees the Thursday after. She expected each audience to hear, within days, what she had told the other two. She had to decide what she would say in each room and how she would say it. The first room opened at 8:30 a.m. Costa Rica time on Tuesday, with twelve named analysts on the line.

## The Protagonist

Arenas-Beltrán had taken over as CEO eight months earlier. The largest operating decision of her tenure so far was a re-architecture of how Helio Verde's products reached modern retail, paired with a rationalisation of low-margin product lines to make room for new capacity at the company's Cartago plant. It had lifted margins and cost revenue, and both effects were in the quarter's numbers.

The previous Friday she had ratified the dismissal for cause of Carlos Vega Hernández, regional general manager for Central America North. She had not run the investigation. Beatriz Solís, who led human resources, had.

## The Decision

Each room asked four things of her.

On the earnings call she had to settle the opening of her prepared remarks — roughly 200 words that the next morning's analyst notes would quote; her answer to Wen; her answer to Daniel Restrepo of Banco Andino, who was expected to ask for "more colour on the year"; and the voice she would hold across sixty minutes of questions from twelve analysts she could not all anticipate.

At the town hall she had to settle the first ninety seconds; how, or whether, to refer to the man who had left; what to say about the three employees the investigation concerned; and how to move from that subject to the scheduled operating update.

At the all-hands, which the company called *La Conversación*, she had to choose an opening line, the arc of thirty-five minutes, how to treat a flat employee-engagement result, and a closing line.

For each of the twelve choices her team had drafted several approaches (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde S.A. was a consumer-foods company listed on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker HLV, run from Costa Rica. Its 15,000 employees worked in plants at La Sabana, Cartago and Guatemala City, in distribution centres, in field sales and on route-sales teams across Central America. Its leadership campus, the Helio Verde Leadership Center in San Antonio de Belén, housed the Salón de las Banderas, where the town hall would be held.

Its Central America North region was now run on an interim basis by Renata Coralles.

## The Situation

The quarter was a split result. Organic revenue grew 4.2% against a consensus of 7.0%, a miss of 280 basis points, while gross margin rose 90 basis points. Management was cutting the full-year revenue guide to 5.5–6.0% from 7.5–8.5% and raising the full-year margin guide by 50 basis points. By the company's own analysis, about 100 basis points of the miss came from softer demand in Panama and Guatemala, and about 180 came from the channel re-architecture and product rationalisation, which had pulled US$14 million of low-margin volume out ahead of the Cartago capacity ramp (see Exhibit 1). Carballo expected Priya Iyer of Avenida Capital to press on that split, and the buy-side view of the quarter to form around the exchange.

The departure was the second fact. Vega had spent nineteen years at Helio Verde, seven as regional general manager. The written finding was a pattern of harassment against three subordinates over three years, with corroborating evidence and an admission. A two-paragraph internal note had gone out on Monday under Solís's signature. The 200 leaders had learned the rest through messaging groups. Two of the three affected employees had accepted transfers and one was on personal leave; none would be at the town hall. Vega's three former direct reports would be, seated together at table eleven, with Coralles on the dais.

The leadership was divided on what she should say. Half the 200 had spent the week arguing over whether she would name him, and the most senior were waiting to hear whether she would use the word *harassment*. Solís's four-line brief stated that the room needed acknowledgement that something had happened, that the standard had been applied, that it applied to everyone, and that the company was moving forward. Solís asked Arenas-Beltrán to speak as the CEO who had ratified the decision rather than the one who had investigated it. Others on the team held that the usual line on personnel matters — that the company did not comment — protected both the affected employees and the company from a statement that could be used in any later dispute. Some leaders argued that naming him was the only way to show that the standard reached the top and that the company had nothing to hide.

On the call the same fact ran through Wen. A flat denial of the whole question was the shortest answer and closed the subject, but it put a personnel matter on the record as a denial. Confirming a conduct issue would have ended speculation about the accounts, and would have told the market more than the internal note had told employees. The standard reply — that the company did not discuss personnel matters — was one analysts had heard many times and would not be quoted as news, and it left the financial suggestion unanswered. Taking the question apart would have separated what could be said from what could not, and would have required her to draw that line live, with Wen free to push on it.

The third fact was engagement. The September pulse survey had come back at 71% favourability, flat on the year and below a peer-set median of 76%. The item *"I trust senior leadership to do what they say"* was down 6 points; *"I see myself at this company in three years"* was down 4. Most of the all-hands audience would be watching on a screen, 9,200 of them remotely — in field-sales cars, route-sales break rooms and distribution-centre mezzanines — and her communications team expected the opening line to be circulating in messaging groups within ninety seconds.

If Arenas-Beltrán changed nothing, none of the three dates moved. Wen had already said he would ask his question on the call. The 200 leaders already knew what had happened and were waiting to hear how she would talk about it. The pulse would stay at 71, five points under the peer median, until the next survey measured it again.

## What Arenas-Beltrán Controlled

She controlled the twelve choices, four per room, each settled before that room opened.

She would be read four ways as the fortnight unfolded: which of the three rooms she was in; a score out of 100 for how each room went once it had been delivered; a cumulative score across the rooms; and a composition-integrity reading, starting at 100%, meant to show whether a single voice carried from room to room. For each room she would also see a voice count, out of four, across her choices.

## Closing

On Monday afternoon Arenas-Beltrán had three documents on the table: the prepared remarks for the call, Solís's four-line brief for Thursday, and the eighth draft of *La Conversación*. Wen's message was clipped to the first. The call was at 8:30 the next morning.
