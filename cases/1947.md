# Thornbury Industrial Supplies: The Digital Mandate

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1947 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1947 — *Transform the Salesforce* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Friday morning, March 6, 2026, Priya Raval opened a letter three customers had signed together. Thornbury Industrial Supplies' largest, second-largest and fourth-largest accounts — £19 million of annual trade between them, 13% of company revenue — had agreed a common position on how they would buy. From October 1, 2026 they would order only from distributors offering a punch-out catalogue their purchasing systems could call, real-time stock and delivery-date information, self-service reordering, and a working interface between the two companies' software. Thornbury could do none of the four.

Raval had been Commercial Director for four days. The board had approved £4.5 million over eighteen months for exactly this demand; the full build her predecessor scoped — commerce platform, customer-record system, retraining, a low-cost ordering route — was costed at about £7.8 million. She had to decide what the £4.5 million bought, in what order, and what would not be done at all. The October 1 date belonged to the customers.

## The Protagonist

Priya Raval, 44, had spent twenty-two years in industrial distribution and none outside it. She had carried a bag in the North West, run a territory of eleven reps, and managed a builders' merchant's branch network through a consolidation. She started at Thornbury on March 2. Her appointment letter set her variable pay against retained revenue and gross margin, and the board wanted a progress review at four points across the eighteen months. She knew how the letter would sound to the field organisation. She had never run a technology build.

## The Decision

Raval had to tell the board, on one page, what kind of problem the letter represented. Four readings were live inside the company, each with a sponsor at her own table: a purchase — buy the best commerce platform and customer-record system, and build the capability around them; a matter of relationships — protect the field reps and the accounts they held, and hold self-service ordering back; a cost problem — move all 6,400 accounts onto digital ordering and take out the cost of serving them; a single coordinated change — technology, selling capability and the way accounts were covered moving together in one programme.

The same page had to name whose revenue she was defending — the 5,000 smallest transactional accounts, which consumed the most handling per order; all 6,400 equally, since the mandate would spread through the book; or the three signatories plus the roughly £22 million of mid-market customers whose buyers had already asked her area managers what Thornbury intended. And she had to state how firmly October 1 would be held: a date the programme aimed at, or a date nothing else was allowed to move.

Then she had to divide the money across four lines, in steps of £100,000 adding to exactly £4.5 million: the commerce platform and e-procurement integration behind the punch-out catalogue, the interface and the stock and delivery-date feed; the customer-record and sales-analytics system to replace the paper-and-spreadsheet pipeline; the reskilling and change programme for the sales force; and an inside-sales desk and digital reorder route. With the split she had to publish the order of work: platform first with the reskilling wave immediately behind; platform and customer-record system first, reskilling held until the tools were in; or the sales force retrained first and the platform built afterwards, beyond the October window.

## The Company and its Setting

Thornbury Industrial Supplies Ltd., of Coventry, distributed maintenance, repair and operations products — fasteners, abrasives, protective equipment, power tools — to 6,400 active trade accounts across UK manufacturing, construction, facilities management and the public sector. FY2025 revenue was £148 million.

The commercial engine was 95 long-tenured field sales representatives, who held the relationships and negotiated most of the pricing. There was no shared customer-record system: the pipeline lived in reps' notebooks and in spreadsheets that reached head office monthly. The website took 11% of orders and did little more than take them. Several large customers had begun routing part of their consumable spend through online marketplaces and the e-commerce arms of bigger rivals, where a reorder took ninety seconds.

## The Situation

A February capability audit had found 18 of the 95 field reps digitally confident. Forty-one were recorded as actively resistant to any system that, as one told the auditor, "gives away my accounts." The objection had an arithmetic behind it: reorder commission was about 55% of a field rep's variable pay, and a portal reorder was an order no rep had touched.

The exposure ran wider than the signatories. The mandated accounts were worth £19 million, the mid-market customers watching roughly £22 million more: together about £41 million, near 28% of revenue, in play within twelve months (see **Exhibit 1**). The largest single account, £9 million of it, held a clause letting it move business at short notice if service requirements went unmet; its buyer had cited the clause twice in eight weeks.

If Thornbury did nothing, the sequence was not in doubt. On October 1 the three accounts would begin ordering from distributors who could take them electronically, starting with the £9 million account and its clause, and the mid-market customers would draw their conclusions. Nothing would move on its own: the website would keep taking 11% of orders, the reps would keep protecting their reorder commission, and the pipeline would stay in the spreadsheets.

## What Raval Controlled

The board measured the programme on four things (see **Exhibit 2**): retained revenue, how much of the £41 million at risk was still Thornbury's, at £0.0 million; digital adoption, the share of orders customers placed themselves, at 11%; a capability index for the sales force, 0 to 100, at 19; and, running the other way, an index of friction between Thornbury's routes to market and the risk of eroding its own prices, at 55 of 100, lower better. Beside them sat the budget line: £0.0 million committed of £4.5 million.

Three further decisions were hers. Which accounts moved to self-service and the inside-sales desk and which kept a field rep — reorder traffic to the digital route with complex, high-value accounts left field-led; every account including the three signatories moved; or none. What the commission plan paid — reps earning on the account whichever route the order arrived through; the plan left alone, paying reorder commission on telephone and field orders and nothing on portal orders; or reorder commission removed and the money put into the programme. And the marketplace rule — a price floor agreed before any listing opened, so no route undercut the field price list; a listing at list price minus 8% to win volume quickly, with the price floor set once the listing was running; or no listing. She also set how much of the change programme went into redeploying and coaching the 41 resistant reps, from none to all.

Six months in, with the platform live and ordering uneven across the book, she would direct the money still unspent: into enablement and adoption — coaching, portal prompts, account-team support where ordering had not moved; into further platform features; or into a discount push on the marketplace listing aimed at the £9 million account. That account, one missed integration from its clause, she would handle with a field-led task force finishing its punch-out and delivery-date feed at once, with the live portal on the same terms as everyone else, or with the next wave. And she would bring the finance director who signed the £4.5 million one of three things: revenue retained, the change in cost to serve, payback and the route to scale; a record of what had been delivered — platform, records system, reps trained; or where the company was heading and what the programme opened up next. She had to say too how firmly she would stand behind a single payback figure she could evidence.

## Closing

On the evening of March 6 Raval wrote the four investment lines down the left of a sheet of paper and the four measures across the top. The £4.5 million had to reach the bottom exactly. Two hundred and eight days remained until October 1; the first board review was in three weeks.
