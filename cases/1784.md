# Méridien Audio: Embracing the Unknown

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1784 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1784 — *Embrace the Unknown* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Thursday, 7 May 2026, Élodie Chevalier read the two items on her desk and understood that neither, alone, was the real problem. The first described the Berlin trade show two days earlier, where a venture-backed entrant had launched an AI audio platform that licensed its personalisation software to any hardware maker that wanted it — sidestepping Méridien Audio's premium-device strategy. The second was a one-line question a French retail partner had forwarded that morning: would Méridien's next headphones support open AI audio profiles, or was the partner locked in? Méridien's sell-through had not moved; nothing was visibly breaking. Chevalier's task, with nine months left before the board's FY2027 strategy review and a EUR 6 million discretionary budget to spend or preserve, was to decide whether that stillness meant the threat was overstated — or not yet arrived.

## The Protagonist

Élodie Chevalier, 43, had led Méridien Audio's innovation unit for four years, reporting to the chief executive. The unit was small by design — eighteen engineers with a standing discretionary mandate — created because Méridien's hardware organisation, where Chevalier had spent twelve years bringing two headphone generations to market, worked on a cadence measured in years. That cadence had built Méridien's reputation; whether it could survive a market where rivals shipped new capability overnight was untested.

## The Decision

Chevalier's task did not resolve into one choice; it ran as a sequence. She first had to name the kind of situation Méridien faced, and choose the opportunity framing to carry forward. She then had to decide how much money and time to spend testing that framing, before committing to a full response's cost. Once evidence returned — with no guarantee it would arrive clean — she had to decide what it actually meant. Only then, with less runway left, did commitment itself become the question.

## The Company and its Setting

Méridien Audio SAS, founded in 2011 and headquartered in Lyon, designed premium wireless headphones, earbuds and home speakers, sold through European retail and a direct-to-consumer channel. The company employed 620 people and generated EUR 240 million in revenue at a 12% operating margin (Exhibit 1) — built on the idea that customers paid for a well-engineered device and kept it for years. The Berlin entrant's proposition ran the other way: if personalisation could be delivered and continually improved through software licensed to any manufacturer, the device itself stopped being where the value lived.

## The Situation

Nothing in Méridien's own numbers yet confirmed the shift. Sell-through was flat, not falling (Exhibit 1) — no collapsing order book, only an unresolved question about where the category was heading. Two of Chevalier's eighteen engineers had been approached by the entrant's recruiters, and a second, similarly funded competitor was preparing its own move within the quarter. Chevalier held EUR 6 million of discretionary budget and nine months before the board's FY2027 product commitments locked (Exhibit 1); a full pivot to an AI-personalised software platform like the entrant's was estimated at EUR 40 million over three years — a bet no board approved on one trade show and one email. If Méridien continued as it was, the nine months would still pass and the board would still convene, but with a rival that had spent the same months signing partners and shipping software.

## What Chevalier Controlled

The first task was naming what she faced: measurable risk, to be handled with a business case built on a probability assigned to the software market; a passing fad, with flat sell-through itself proof there was nothing yet to decide; or genuine uncertainty, a shift whose shape could not be reduced to odds at all. Having named it, she had to choose an opening framing: that the entrant was a threat to be contained while Méridien defended its existing device business; that Méridien should become the trusted hardware inside an open AI-audio ecosystem; that the shift was a niche, to be met by continuing the multi-year hardware cadence unchanged; or that the software and personalisation layer was itself an opportunity worth entering directly. Whichever she chose, she had to name the one assumption it depended on — that brand loyalty alone would hold regardless of competitors, that the entrant's software was too difficult to copy, that customers would pay for AI-personalised audio as an ongoing service rather than a bundled feature, or that no single assumption mattered because the framing was self-evidently right — and state, as a percentage, how willing her team was to treat it as a hypothesis rather than a belief to defend.

With a framing chosen, Chevalier could spend part of her EUR 6 million to test it (Exhibit 2): a fake-door landing page testing direct-to-consumer demand, at EUR 0.05 million over three weeks; an open-profile trial with one retail partner, at EUR 0.20 million over six weeks; a software pilot on existing hardware, at EUR 0.40 million over ten weeks; an acqui-hire to bring the technology in-house, at EUR 1.50 million over fourteen weeks; or a six-month build of a full platform, at EUR 1.20 million over twenty-six weeks — combinable, so long as the total fit her remaining budget. Before any of them ran, she had to set her discipline: run the moves and judge results only once they arrived, design them to confirm the framing already chosen, or pre-commit a threshold below which a disappointing result meant walking away.

Whatever she chose to run, the probes would report back — stronger engagement than expected on the pilot but little sign customers would pay, and an enthusiastic but small retail trial — and she would have to decide how to read the result: converting the ambiguity into a single number — a 65% probability the software market was real; holding the encouraging and the discouraging signal at once rather than choosing between them; leading with the engagement figure on the assumption willingness to pay would follow at scale; or treating the weak pricing signal as fatal to the whole framing, discounting the stronger engagement read. She had to state, honestly, how much of the uncertainty remained, and narrow her path: toward shelving the initiative and returning to the existing roadmap; toward the full EUR 40 million immediately, on the evidence she already had; toward a staged commitment with explicit triggers preserving her ability to abort; or toward one further, sharper probe on the pricing question.

The last decision would arrive as runway shortened and a second competitor closed its funding round: whether to wait — not yet, holding off two further quarters for clearer market data before deciding; not at all, returning the option to the board; immediately and in full, the entire EUR 40 million, before the window closed; or to commit now, at a size scaled to the evidence with named triggers to scale up or pull back. If she committed, she had to size the opening stage — the full EUR 40 million at once; no capital, the evidence still too thin to risk; roughly EUR 8 million as a small first step; or roughly EUR 18 million as a more material stage — and name the condition that would tell her whether to accelerate or abandon the bet: a judgment made when it felt right, a measured willingness-to-pay milestone paired with a rival's deadline, or waiting until the board next asked. Whatever she chose, she would close by telling the board chair, in percentage terms, how firmly she stood behind a recommendation built on evidence that would never fully resolve.

Across the sequence, four measures would follow her: how much of the fog her probes had cleared, how much of her option to act later remained unforfeited, how evidence-based, honest and well-timed her judgement had been, and how many months of runway she had left at the end.

## Closing

At her desk that Thursday morning, Chevalier opened a blank page and wrote down the two things she knew: a flat sales chart and a partner's one-line question. She had nine months, a budget of EUR 6 million against a possible bet of EUR 40 million, and no instrument yet for telling whether the fog ahead was a storm, a mirage, or the outline of her company's next decade.
