# The Talon Shop: One Semester Behind the Counter

**Case code:** OPS-2026-2248 · **Associated simulation:** Express 2248 — *The School Store* · **v1.0 · October 2026**
**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At a quarter past seven on Monday, 11 January 2027, Jada Marchbanks unlocked the door of The Talon Shop, the student store of Cedar Ridge High School in Lawrenceville, Georgia, and found last year's binder on the counter. It held no price list and no sales record by item. The stockroom behind it was full of medium hoodies with last year's logo. Marchbanks had to decide that same week what the store would charge and what spirit wear it would buy for the spring, because the assistant principal wanted a written plan on his desk by Friday.

There would be four sets of decisions in the semester: in January the prices and the spirit-wear order; on 8 February the snack assortment and the way the cash was counted; on 8 March the answer to the first full inventory count and a spring reorder; and on 12 April the last four weeks and the end-of-semester report (see Exhibit 2).

## The Protagonist

Jada Marchbanks, 17, was a junior and the store's student manager for the semester. She had worked shifts at the counter as a sophomore volunteer, but had never set a price, placed an order or closed the books.

## The Decision

The store was the student business club's school-based enterprise, and its advisor, Denise Kimbrough, who taught marketing, had handed Marchbanks the keys that morning. Dr. Calvin Strickland, the assistant principal, approved the store's purchases; his condition for keeping it open was a plan with prices and the spring order. The store's last day would be Friday, 7 May, and Ms. Kimbrough needed the end-of-semester report by Friday, 14 May. Each decision took effect from the day it was made and held for the rest of the semester.

## The Company and its Setting

The Talon Shop occupied a converted classroom by the cafeteria and opened before school and through both lunches. Twelve club volunteers staffed it in shifts of two. Its money ran through the school's student-activity account, kept by the bookkeeper, Lorraine Quarles, and what it earned paid for the chapter's competition travel.

It sold two things. Snacks and drinks came from Hollins Snack Supply at about $0.90 a unit; at last year's prices about 260 sold on a school day. Spirit wear — T-shirts, hoodies and caps — came from Ridgeback Apparel Co. at $11 a piece, and the chapter's own estimate was that students would buy about 70, 80, 90 and 120 pieces in the four periods of the semester if the store had their sizes and designs. About 70% of sales were in cash; a card reader took the rest. The point-of-sale subscription, bags and receipt paper cost $110 a period (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The store's usual way was simple: prices matched to the vending machines and the gas station across the street, $1.50 a snack and $25 a shirt; last year's spirit-wear order of 400 pieces repeated, although about 30% of it was in sizes and old designs nobody bought; the same 24 snacks; the cash box counted on Fridays; and shrink — the gap between what the records said should be on the shelves and what was there — known to be about 4% of sales and left alone.

If nothing changed, a semester like that would sell $26,742 and earn $8,534. The store would serve about 134 students a day, write down 120 unsold pieces at the end, and finish with its controls-and-trust index at 37 out of 100.

Troy Mattingly, Ridgeback's sales rep, had already called with an offer of 600 pieces at $9.50. A volunteer had his own view: the store was "a captive market" and should charge $2.25 a snack and $30 a shirt.

## What Marchbanks Controlled

On 11 January Marchbanks would choose the price list: match the vending machines and the gas station, $1.50 a snack and $25 a shirt, as now; charge what a captive market would bear, $2.25 a snack and $30 a shirt; set a markup students would still pay, 40% over cost on snacks rounded to the quarter and twice the cost on apparel, $1.25 and $22; or price at cost plus 10% to keep things cheap for students, $1.00 and $12. And the spring spirit-wear buy: no apparel this semester, snacks only; repeat last year's order of 400 pieces at $11, as now; take Ridgeback's bundle of 600 pieces at $9.50; or buy from the sales record by size and design, a first run of 240 pieces with a reorder planned.

On 8 February the first period's numbers were in. Carla Thibodeaux, the district's school-nutrition director, had sent every school a reminder about the federal nutrition standards for food sold to students during the school day, and the volunteers said students kept asking for energy drinks and candy bars at lunch. The cash box had been $31 short the Friday before, and nobody knew which day. Marchbanks would choose the snack assortment: keep the same 24 snacks, as now; widen to 60 items so there was something for everyone; stock the 14 best sellers from the sales record, all meeting the federal standards; or add the full-sugar energy drinks and candy bars students asked for at lunch. And the cash: count the box once a week, on Friday, as now; count it herself when the box got full; make it balance by writing the register's total on the count sheet and keeping any difference in an envelope; or close every day with two volunteers counting the drawer against the point-of-sale report, logging the over or short and taking the deposit to Ms. Quarles the same day.

On 8 March the first full count of the semester, taken the Friday before, showed the shelves short of the records by about 4% of sales: snacks walking out at the lunch rush, free items for friends, damaged stock never logged. The small and large sizes were sold out, and spring sports and prom season were coming. Marchbanks would choose the answer to the count: write the gap off as samples; lock everything behind the counter and install a $180 camera; set controls — weekly counts of the 20 top items, two volunteers on every shift, a written no-freebies rule every volunteer signed and a receipt for every sale; or ignore it and count again in May, as now. And the spring reorder: take Ridgeback's consignment offer of 150 pieces at $14, with unsold pieces returned; repeat the January order; place no reorder, as now; or reorder the sizes that had sold out, 150 pieces.

On 12 April four weeks remained. The seniors wanted a class shirt, and the stockroom still held the pieces nobody had bought. Marchbanks would choose the last four weeks: open during class periods with volunteers pulled from class; do nothing special, as now; take senior class-shirt pre-orders paid in advance and set out a clearance table at cost; or take 50% off everything. And the end-of-semester report: report total sales as the store's profit; take a full physical inventory, reconcile the cash to the deposits and prepare a profit-and-loss by product line with a recommendation for the fall buy; count the unsold stock at its retail price; or hand the cash box and the keys to Ms. Kimbrough without a count, as now.

For each period Ms. Kimbrough would see the sales, with the total to date; the profit, with the total to date; the gross margin, sales less the cost of goods as a share of sales; the shrink and cash losses as a share of sales; the students served a day; the apparel sold, in pieces, pre-orders and clearance included; and the controls-and-trust index on a scale from 0 to 100. Marchbanks would receive a round score out of 100 for each period.

## Closing

At twenty to eight the first bell was ten minutes away and two volunteers were waiting outside the door with the cash box. Marchbanks looked at the hoodies in the stockroom, at Mattingly's offer on her phone and at the blank page where the price list should have been.
