# Red de Salud Privada: A Tariff for La Esperanza

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-496 · **Companion simulation:** Express 496 — *La Clínica* · **v1.0 · November 2026** · **Time required: 75 minutes**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 3 March 2026, Lucía Paz Mejía laid two documents side by side on her desk at the Tegucigalpa head office of Red de Salud Privada (RSP). The first was the chief executive's proposal for a 20-bed clinic in La Esperanza, a farming community of 12,000 people with no private healthcare within 45 kilometres. The second was the costing her own finance team had attached to it: US$180,000 to build and equip, US$15,000 a month to run, and US$25 of medicines and supplies for every patient visit.

The board would meet on Thursday, 12 March. It wanted a visit tariff for La Esperanza from her and a reading of the demand that tariff would bring. If the project went ahead, she would carry the rural clinic's results through the year that followed.

## The Protagonist

Lucía Paz Mejía, 44, had been RSP's Finance Director since 2019. She had trained as an accountant in San Pedro Sula, spent nine years in the finance department of a regional health insurer, and joined RSP when it still had three clinics. She had negotiated most of the network's contracts with private insurers and corporate health plans, and she knew to the dollar what an urban visit cost and earned. Her bonus, like those of the other directors, moved with the network's consolidated operating profit. In January the board had made her accountable for the rural project's profit and loss in addition to her existing role.

## The Decision

The proposal came from Dr. Arturo Flores, RSP's founder and chief executive, who had argued for two years that a company calling itself a health network could not serve only insured city dwellers. His draft set the tariff at US$30 a visit. He reasoned that a price within reach of farming families would fill the clinic, and that a full clinic would spread its fixed costs across enough visits to pay its way.

Paz's first decision was the tariff itself. Every dollar she added would raise the margin on each visit and shrink the number of families able to pay it. Household income in La Esperanza ran between US$150 and US$250 a month.

Her decisions would not end on 12 March. The board had approved a sequence. After the tariff and the market reading, she would choose the operating format and the tariff it would open with. After six months of pilot operation she would bring back actual demand figures and a recommendation for the clinic's next stage. In the final stage she would propose the long-term financing structure under which the clinic would run, and the tariff patients would pay under it.

## The Company and its Setting

RSP operated four urban clinics in Tegucigalpa, San Pedro Sula and La Ceiba, with 160 beds between them. In 2025 the network earned US$8.3 million of revenue at a 20% operating margin, or about US$1.66 million of annual operating profit (see **Exhibit 1**). Its patients were overwhelmingly urban and insured, through private policies or employer plans, with a smaller group paying directly. That profit paid for equipment renewal, the network's debt service and every new initiative the board approved.

La Esperanza looked nothing like that patient base. Most residents farmed or worked informally. Public provision in the area was thin, and international health charities covered parts of the region but not all of it. The nearest private clinic was 45 kilometres away.

The board was not of one mind. Flores argued that a network with rural reach would be valued by its urban patients, by the employers who bought its corporate plans, and above all by its own doctors, several of whom had asked to work in the pilot. Ramón Castellanos, a retired banker who chaired the audit committee, answered that the 20% margin was not a surplus but the means by which RSP stayed solvent and modern, and he asked what loss the board was being asked to accept, and for how long.

## The Situation

Paz's team had mapped the community at 12,000 residents who used a health service, on average, 1.8 times a year: about 21,600 visits a year, or roughly 1,800 a month if every one of them came to RSP. A lower tariff would bring in more of the community's visits; a higher one would earn more on each visit that came.

Her team had also costed three alternatives to the building Flores had proposed (see **Exhibit 2**). One kept the same building and staff with a narrower list of services, and came out at the same cost figures. A second was a level II health post: a full-time nurse, a general physician shared with one of the urban clinics part of the week, and no surgery, at US$8,000 a month fixed and US$12 a visit. A third paired a small fixed post with a mobile unit visiting the surrounding villages, at US$5,000 a month fixed and US$12 a visit, but with a ceiling of 800 visits a month. Both smaller formats needed less capital than the US$180,000 building.

If the board approved the proposal as drafted, RSP would commit US$180,000 of capital and US$15,000 a month of fixed cost to La Esperanza. If the board declined, RSP would keep earning its US$1.66 million from the cities, and La Esperanza would remain 45 kilometres from private care.

## What Paz Controlled

In the first stage Paz set the proposed tariff, anywhere from US$10 to US$40 a visit, and read the demand it implied against the visits needed to break even. In the second she chose among the four formats in Exhibit 2 and set the opening tariff, again between US$10 and US$40.

For the six-month review the board had asked her to price four courses in advance. She could close the clinic and stop its operating costs. She could fund an additional outreach and marketing campaign of between US$10,000 and US$50,000. She could cut the tariff to US$15 and run the clinic as a mission-led service, with the network covering any gap. Or she could bring an international health charity in as operating partner, sharing staff, supplies and decisions with it.

For the final stage, four structures were on the table. Residents could pay monthly premiums into a community health insurance pool, with the aim of enrolling four in five households. A public health fund could pay US$15 a visit while patients paid a copayment. Government could fund the infrastructure in a public–private partnership while RSP ran operations. Or the urban clinics could transfer 5% of their annual profit to the rural clinic, openly and on the books. With whichever she chose, she would set the tariff patients paid, between US$5 and US$30.

At every stage the board would see the same figures: the rural clinic's monthly revenue, its monthly costs, the monthly profit or loss between them, the patients it saw each month, and the total capital committed to the rural project so far. Beside them it would watch the urban network's annual profit, the source of any subsidy.

## Closing

At 9:15 Paz opened a blank spreadsheet and typed three tariffs into the first column: US$30, US$20 and US$15. Beside each she began to estimate how many of La Esperanza's 12,000 residents would walk through the door. The board met in nine days.
