# Meridian Iberia Consulting: The First Six Days

On the morning of 2 June 2026, Álvaro Mendes read the firmwide email confirming what half of Meridian Iberia Consulting already suspected: he would take over as Head of the Digital Transformation practice — 38 consultants, €21,000,000 in annual fees — effective immediately. The role had been widely expected to go to Núria Camps, a senior manager with nine years at the firm who had built the practice's two largest client relationships. Instead, Núria would now report to him. The reassignment posted to the firm's internal engagement-marketplace dashboard within the hour, where every consultant, partner and client lead could see it. Álvaro had six working days to decide how he would use the authority he had just been given, starting with who would lead the firm's most visible new mandate.

## The Protagonist

Álvaro had spent five years inside the Digital Transformation practice, most of them one level below Núria. He had never run a department before. The research on power transitions that Meridian required new managers to read was blunt about what tended to happen next: people who had just gained power tended to overclaim credit, to consult less than they believed they did, and to trust their own judgment more than the evidence supported. He had read it that morning, before the promotion email even arrived.

## The Decision

Before touching any staffing question, Álvaro owed himself an honest diagnosis. His pulse-survey trust index — measured across all 38 reports — stood at 62 out of 100 the day he took over, and he expected it to move with almost everything he did over the next six days. Separately, Meridian's partners tracked a second number for every new department head: how much of his authority they actually backed in practice, as distinct from what his new title alone gave him. That figure — his influence retained — opened at 60 out of 100. He had to decide which relationship to secure first: the high-performing consultant likely to become his own right hand, the partnership itself, the people who had just promoted him, the early backers who had pushed hardest for his promotion, or Núria, who now held three of his five largest accounts. He also had to name, for himself, which specific trap the promotion was most likely to spring — insisting on everyone's sign-off before making any move at all, moving fast and alone, deciding without asking anyone first, under-using the authority the promotion had just given him, or handing off every hard call rather than making one himself. And he had to rate, as honestly as he could, how strong his own urge was to prove, this week, that he was now in charge.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Iberia Consulting, S.L. was a Barcelona-headquartered management and technology consultancy founded in 2009, with 640 consultants across Barcelona, Madrid and Lisbon and €92,000,000 in 2025 fee revenue at a 19% operating margin (see Exhibit 1). The firm had doubled in size over four years, which meant promotions like Álvaro's were routine and closely watched: Meridian's distinguishing habit was to run its staffing decisions through a public "engagement marketplace," a dashboard on which every flagship assignment, and the forecast its leader committed to, was visible firmwide. A power transition handled well or badly was not private information at Meridian. It was Tuesday-morning conversation across three offices.

## The Situation

The most immediate test was the Grupo Camino mandate: a €4,200,000 transformation programme for the firm's third-largest client, which had to be staffed within the six-day window (see Exhibit 2) and which the client had asked be led by "a senior, credible lead." Núria had built the Camino relationship over four years. Álvaro had to choose among assigning Núria herself, the person who had built that relationship; his own rising direct report, with Núria in a defined advisory role; himself, taking direct charge of the practice's most visible mandate; or a neutral senior consultant brought in from another Meridian office. Separately, he had to decide what happened before the public post went up — posting the staffing to the dashboard before speaking with her at all, having HR deliver the news to Núria while he stayed out of it, or speaking to Núria privately before any public post went up — and how the post attributed credit: naming Núria explicitly as the architect of the Camino relationship, crediting "the practice" as a whole without naming anyone, or framing the account as his own new direction for it.

Underneath the staffing question sat a harder number. Núria held three of the practice's five largest client relationships, worth an estimated €9,500,000 a year in fees, and two competing Barcelona firms were known to be hiring senior digital-transformation leads. Meridian's client-service team kept a running account-risk reading, on the same 0–100 scale, of how much of that revenue was actually in danger of walking out the door with her; it opened at 30. Five private messages had already reached the HR business partner questioning how the promotion had been handled. If nothing in how Álvaro managed the week changed that trajectory, the practice's forecast would rest on the goodwill of the one person he had just been promoted over, at the exact moment that goodwill was most in question.

By day three, the partnership required Álvaro to commit a public Q3 revenue forecast for the practice. The pipeline's base case was €5,100,000, with an 80% confidence band running from €4,400,000 to €5,900,000. Whatever he committed would be tracked against that base case as an overconfidence gap — the committed figure minus the calibrated expected value, in euros — which stood at €0.0 million until the moment he named a number. He had to set the committed number itself, decide whether to publish it together with an explicit confidence band, commit a single flat figure with no caveats attached, or publish a wide "could go anywhere" caveat with no real number at all, and decide whether to consult Núria and the team before committing to a number or to own the number alone and inform them afterward. A confident figure would read well on the dashboard the day he posted it; whether it would still read well once the quarter actually closed was a separate question, and the same dashboard would answer it for everyone to see.

## What Álvaro Controlled

Two days after the forecast went public, a Slack thread in which several team members debated how Álvaro had handled his first week leaked beyond its original channel, and Núria took an exploratory call from one of the competing firms. Álvaro still controlled a retention offer for her: a redefined role — ownership of the top-five accounts plus a visible deputy mandate, a pay raise that left her role and authority exactly as they were, a bigger title that carried no additional real authority with it, or nothing beyond what any loyal employee of the firm already had. He controlled one visible act of power-sharing that week — publicly crediting Núria for the Camino win on the dashboard, having her co-present the practice strategy to the partners, or making no such gesture at all this week — and, separately, one boundary he chose to hold: keeping the final forecast call and practice direction for himself, giving Núria an effective veto over his decisions to keep the peace, or holding no boundary at all and conceding whatever she asked. He also had to say, honestly, how deliberately — under pressure, with the thread still circulating — he had actually managed the trust he had left to spend.

## Closing

At his desk on the morning of 2 June, Álvaro opened the dashboard, still blank under his name, and started with the diagnosis he owed himself before anything else. Six working days, a €4,200,000 mandate, and a trust index he did not yet know how to spend.
