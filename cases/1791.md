# Atlântico Learning Partners: The Banco Verde Bid

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1791 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1791 — *Design the Program* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 September 2026, Mariana Costa read Banco Verde's closed-bid RFP twice before reacting to the number at the bottom: ten working days to submit a design, a measurement plan, and a fixed price for 600 branch and area managers who needed to stop supervising and start coaching. Banco Verde, a EUR 9-billion Iberian retail bank with 4,800 employees, had just merged two regional networks, and its board wanted three things moved within nine months: advisory-product cross-sell up 6 percentage points per branch, front-line manager attrition down 15%, and manager sentiment — tracked as a net-promoter figure — at +30 or better. The prior vendor's EUR 1.1 million programme had produced glowing completion certificates and no movement on any of the three; Banco Verde had fired that vendor in the spring, and two rival design firms were now bidding alongside Atlântico for the replacement contract.

## The Protagonist

Costa, 41, had been Programme Director at Atlântico Learning Partners for six years, arriving from an in-house learning role at a Porto insurer whose leadership academy she had watched close without anyone able to say what it had changed. Inside the firm she was known for asking, of every proposed workshop day, what a manager would do differently the Monday after it — before asking what the workshop would cover. The Banco Verde account was hers to win or lose: she had led the pitch that put Atlântico on the shortlist, and her name would be on the design going back to the bank's CLO and CFO in ten days.

## The Decision

Costa had to decide, in stages, what Atlântico would propose. First, which capability gap the programme would close, and how much of the ten days to spend diagnosing it — naming the target behaviour, the conditions enabling or blocking it, and a baseline to measure against — before committing a euro to content. Second, how to divide a fixed EUR 1,200,000 across the channels that would deliver the programme, and whether to build one experience for all 600 managers or two. Third, how far up the ladder of proof her measurement plan would climb, and what she would tell the board about the return to expect. Fourth, what she would promise in writing, and defend in person, once the CFO asked her to back it up.

## The Company and its Setting

Atlântico Learning Partners was a Lisbon-based executive-education design studio, founded in 2014 and run from the LX Factory creative campus with a smaller hub in Porto. It designed and delivered custom leadership, digital-transformation and commercial-excellence programmes for large Iberian and pan-European clients, drawing on a pool of 60 associate faculty — academics, practitioners, executive coaches — backed by 28 permanent staff. In 2025 it booked EUR 6.8 million in revenue across 34 engagements, delivered 11,200 learner-days, and closed the year at a 16% blended margin.

What set Atlântico apart was a promise few competitors matched: a contractual guarantee, on flagship programmes, to measure and report the business impact actually produced. That promise had built the firm's pipeline. It was also why a bad Banco Verde outcome would cost Atlântico more than one contract.

## The Situation

The RFP's numbers were not negotiable (Exhibit 1). The ceiling was EUR 1,200,000 for the first cohort of 600 managers — EUR 2,000 per learner — with no co-funding from the bank. In-person time off the branch floor was capped at four days total, and the bank's busiest season, November and December, was blocked out entirely. If Atlântico won the bid and then missed the board's targets, the impact-guarantee clause the firm was known for would trigger a clawback of 30%, or EUR 360,000, and cost it a EUR 3.4 million three-cohort pipeline resting on the first cohort landing clean. If it lost the bid outright, the same EUR 3.4 million walked away regardless.

Three voices had already told Costa what they wanted, and they did not agree. The CLO wanted whatever would actually move the board's numbers this time, and was explicit that satisfaction surveys were not it. The Head of Retail, the loudest voice on every call, wanted the 600 managers taught the bank's new advisory product range. A focus group of branch managers mostly wanted to be left alone. If Costa built what the fired vendor had built — content shaped to whoever asked loudest, progress reported through end-of-course scores — the RFP's own history suggested exactly what would follow: full completion, warm feedback, and nine months later the same flat line on cross-sell, attrition and manager sentiment that had gotten the last firm fired.

## What Costa Controlled

Costa's first choice was what the programme would be designed to fix: the advisory-coaching behaviour the board's own metrics pointed to, the product knowledge the Head of Retail wanted, the leadership skills the RFP's own wording literally requested, or a blended curriculum touching all three. Tied to that was a dial, not a switch: how much of the ten days to spend on structured diagnosis — naming the behaviour, the on-the-job context, and the baseline — before any content got built, rather than moving straight to design.

Once the target was named, the EUR 1.2 million had to be split across four channels, each with its own economics and its own strength at changing what a manager actually did on the branch floor (Exhibit 2): in-person intensives, priced at roughly EUR 1,900 per learner-day and the most expensive way to buy the four capped days; virtual-live cohorts, priced at roughly EUR 450 per learner-day, able to run without touching the day cap; self-paced digital content, priced at roughly EUR 120 per learner and the cheapest way to reach all 600; and a sustainment phase of coaching circles and manager-led practice running through months four to nine, funded the same way but earning nothing until after the formal sessions ended. Layered on the format mix were two more calls: whether to staff the programme with senior practitioner-coaches, a blended team of senior coaches and junior associates, or a lower-cost faculty of junior academics; and whether to segment the 600 into a senior-area-manager track and a first-time-branch-lead track, or build one identical experience for all 600 managers.

Costa also had to decide how far her measurement plan would reach: a design carried through to a projected financial return, anchored by a baseline and a control group; one stopping short of that, relying on direct observation of on-the-job behaviour; one stopping further back, at testing what participants had learned; or one measuring only how participants rated the sessions and whether they finished them. In the final round, ahead of the pitch itself, she had to fix the price within the ceiling and choose what promise to attach to it: a guarantee claiming only what the baseline and control group could prove, a guarantee built around a headline return figure, a conditional guarantee tied to a ninety-day recovery plan, or no guarantee at all, priced lower with nothing pledged beyond delivery.

She would be judged, in the end, on whether the design changed what a manager did on a Monday morning, whether it served the metrics the board tracked, whether her measurement plan would survive a skeptical CFO's questions, and whether the whole package fit inside EUR 1.2 million and four days without breaking either ceiling.

## Closing

Costa cleared her desk, laid the RFP, the three stakeholder notes and the prior vendor's post-mortem side by side, and opened a blank page. Ten working days remained, and two rivals were already drafting bids of their own.
