# Helio Verde: Two Clocks and a Ceiling

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1405 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1405 — *Antitrust War-Room — CADE & EU Phase II on Helio Verde / Cárnicos del Pacífico* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At 13:40 on Wednesday, September 16, 2026, Lucía Arenas-Beltrán, chief executive of Helio Verde, closed the door of the Sala Pacífico, the board annex at the group's headquarters in Cartago, Costaria, and laid two documents on the table. One was a fourteen-page dispatch from Brazil's Administrative Council for Economic Defence (CADE). The other was the European Commission's 38-page decision opening an in-depth Phase II review. Both concerned the same transaction: Helio Verde's acquisition of Cárnicos del Pacífico, a Mexican premium-beef exporter, signed twelve weeks earlier at an enterprise value of US$285 million.

She had ninety minutes to agree a remedies package that both regulators could accept and her board could approve. CADE expected a remedies proposal within ten working days; the Commission's formal window for remedies would open in week 16. At 11:50 that morning the chair, Manuel Beltrán-Rojas, had telephoned her with one sentence: no remedy north of US$6 million of annual EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation) give-up.

## The Protagonist

Arenas-Beltrán, 47, had been chief executive since 2023. She had negotiated the deal herself and presented its synergy case to the board. She would not lightly accept a package that broke that case, nor take to the board a deal that a regulator went on to prohibit.

Around the table sat Daniel Olmos-Sandoval, group general counsel, who ran the regulatory file; Renata Coralles, chief operating officer, whose division held the Brazilian business most likely to be sold; and Esther Bracamonte-Núñez, interim head of finance, who owned the EBITDA bridge and would not sign a synergy number she could not defend to the auditors. Tomás Ulloa, chief of staff, kept the clock. Outside counsel joined by video: Hélène Vacher-Lamoureux of Brevaine in Brussels and Otávio Caldas-Bittencourt of Fontelar in São Paulo.

## The Decision

The meeting ran in three blocks. In the first, 28 minutes, the room had to settle how to describe the markets and the competitive harm before discussing remedies. In the second, 32 minutes, it had to build the package, one part for each regulator. In the third, 24 minutes, it had to defend that package twice: first to Beltrán-Rojas, then to its own outside counsel. Arenas-Beltrán intended to close at minute 84.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde was a family-controlled Costarian group with six divisions, revenue of about US$3.1 billion and EBITDA of about US$412 million in 2025 (see Exhibit 1). One division, Helio Brasil Cárnicos, imported and wholesaled beef in Brazil through three distribution centres. Another, Helio Foods Iberia, sold in Spain and Portugal.

Cárnicos del Pacífico earned about US$78 million of EBITDA on roughly US$420 million of revenue. About 38% of its sales went to the United States, 22% to institutional foodservice buyers in the European Union, 14% to Brazil through three independent distributors, and 26% stayed in Mexico. The founding Mendoza-Galindo family kept 12% of the equity and an earn-out, and had to be told of any remedy touching the export book.

Beltrán-Rojas, 71, regarded merger review as a tax on doing business. He had also lived through one that failed: in 2019 the Costarian competition authority had blocked Helio Verde's purchase of Lácteos Andinos after twenty months.

## The Situation

The regulators had opened in-depth reviews on different theories. CADE's dispatch, signed by its case handler, Camila Bevilacqua-Moraes, was vertical. Helio Brasil Cárnicos held 14.0% of Brazil's premium-cuts beef-import market and the Cárnicos del Pacífico export book another 5.1% (see Exhibit 2). The three independent distributors bought about 17% of their volume from Cárnicos del Pacífico; after the deal they would be buying it from a company that competed with them downstream. CADE's question was whether Helio Verde would then have the ability and the incentive to restrict or reprice that supply.

The Commission's concern was horizontal. Across the EU, the parties' combined share of premium beef sold to institutional foodservice was between 6% and 8%. The opening decision, however, leaned towards a market confined to Spain and Portugal, where Helio Foods Iberia and the export book overlapped more heavily. Whether a small but lasting price rise in Iberia would be defeated by buyers turning to suppliers in France or the Benelux was, in Vacher-Lamoureux's words, the question the EU file turned on.

The clocks did not line up. CADE's review stood at day 105 of 240; the Commission's at working day 22 of 90. Helio Verde would have to commit in Brazil before knowing what Brussels would formally ask for.

Counsel did not agree on how much to give. Caldas-Bittencourt, who had spent three years advising the CADE Tribunal, wanted a clean structural fix on the Brazilian side. Vacher-Lamoureux, a former Commission case handler, thought the EU geographic argument was exposed and favoured offering something structural early. Beltrán-Rojas wanted as little as possible.

Ulloa had put seven numbers on the screen at the end of the table (see Exhibit 1). Counsel put the likelihood of clearance at 50% for CADE and 60% for the Commission. The EBITDA give-up, annual EBITDA lost to divestitures plus the running cost of commitments, was zero. Synergy retention, the share of the board-approved synergy case still intact, was 100%. The deal's internal rate of return (IRR) stood at the approved 18.0%; the group hurdle was 14%. The room put the chair's confidence at 70 of 100 and outside counsel's trust in the client's strategy at 60. After each block Ulloa logged the two clearances, the give-up, synergy retention, the chair's confidence and the IRR.

If the room did nothing, the CADE deadline would pass with the foreclosure theory unanswered, the Commission's window would open with the narrow frame on the table, and both reviews would run towards a decision on a deal that had offered nothing. A prohibition would end the transaction, with a US$22 million break fee owed to the Mendoza-Galindo family on top of about US$14 million already spent on advisers and integration.

## What Arenas-Beltrán Controlled

In the first block three questions were open. The **product market**: accept premium-cuts boneless beef as a market of its own, the frame both regulators had used, or argue for all beef cuts, which would dilute the combined shares. The **EU geographic market**: argue for an EU-wide market, which counsel said sat furthest inside the safe harbour of the EU's 2004 horizontal merger guidelines; argue for a multi-state market of Iberia, France and the Benelux; or concede the narrow Iberian frame. And the **stance on CADE's vertical theory**: contest it end to end with an empirical study, accept its logic while disputing its magnitude, or concede it.

In the second, three more. The **CADE remedy**, from Fontelar's menu: a full divestiture of the Brazilian wholesale arm, which Bracamonte-Núñez's bridge put at about US$14 million of annual EBITDA; a partial divestiture with a most-favoured-nation (MFN) non-discrimination overlay, at about US$9 million; or behavioural commitments only, an MFN and no divestiture. The **EU remedy**, from Brevaine's menu: a structural carve-out of the Iberian export contracts; a three-year restriction on coordinating Iberian prices between Helio Foods Iberia and the export book; or no EU commitment, defending the market frame alone. And a separate **overlay for Brazil's independent distributors**: a five-year MFN with a monitoring trustee, a three-year MFN with light monitoring, or none.

In the third, three more. The **register for Beltrán-Rojas**: present the give-up as portfolio rationalisation; lead with the numbers, an enterprise-value and IRR bridge; or lead with legal risk and the prohibition scenario. The **posture towards outside counsel**: hold the EU geographic line and pre-mandate a search for a buyer of the Brazilian assets; accept Vacher-Lamoureux's advice and add an EU structural carve-out now; or defer the buyer search and let the CADE Tribunal trigger a sale if needed. And the **coordination between the two reviews**: staff-to-staff calls between the agencies along the lines the International Competition Network recommends, with a confidentiality waiver; aligned filing dates and no staff contact; or contact between the two law firms only.

## Closing

At 13:41 Ulloa started the clock. Arenas-Beltrán looked at the line she had written at the top of her pad after the chair's call, *no remedy north of six*, and put the first question to the room: what market were they in?
