# Racine: Growing on Purpose

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1996 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1996 — *Growing on Purpose* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 2 March 2026, Camille Roy read the trade-press alert twice before she opened the bank statement. Terravise, the rival Racine had outsold on four contracts in two years, had closed a €12 million Series A round and published a plan to hire eighteen people. The bank statement said Racine held €855,000 in cash and was consuming €95,000 a month net of revenue. Roy set the two documents side by side on her desk in Nantes and did the division in her head: nine months, and a competitor with twelve million euros to spend inside them.

Racine's board had asked for a growth plan by the end of the quarter. In that time Roy had to decide how the company would be financed, what she would agree to in return, and what she would refuse to trade — with a term sheet in front of her that carried a clause she had now read six times.

## The Protagonist

Camille Roy, 41, had spent nine years as an agronomist advising cooperatives in the Loire valley before founding Racine in 2022 with a technical co-founder she met on a waste-audit project. She raised €900,000 of seed capital that year and nothing since; everything after it had come from revenue. Twice she had declined paying customers whose practices she judged inconsistent with what Racine measured. Both decisions she took alone, announced to the whole company, and was thanked for by engineers who had joined at below-market salaries. She held the largest single block of shares and chaired the weekly commercial review. She had never negotiated a term sheet.

## The Decision

Roy had to choose which of three sources of capital would fund Racine's next eighteen months, what she would concede to obtain it, and at what pace she would then grow — and to put that plan in front of her board inside the quarter.

## The Company and its Setting

Racine SAS, founded in Nantes in 2022, sold subscription software that let mid-sized food and agriculture companies measure and reduce waste in their supply chains. By March 2026 it served 38 customers with a team of 21, and its annual recurring revenue — the value of its live subscriptions on an annualised basis, hereafter ARR — stood at €1.4 million (see **Exhibit 1**). ARR had been flat for three quarters.

Around the company sat a set of relationships Roy had built and not drawn on. An accelerator cohort of eleven founders met quarterly. Two anchor customers took her calls and had more than once advised her before she advised them. A community of investors in sustainability had watched Racine for two years. The cohort had offered co-marketing and the loan of a senior engineer. The two advisers had each said, unprompted, that they could open an enterprise pilot, and one founder in the cohort had offered to introduce a third. Roy had thanked all of them and had not taken any of them up.

Terravise sold into the same market with a broader and less selective product. Its €12 million meant it could now outspend Racine on the same enterprise accounts.

## The Situation

Racine's pipeline held qualified enterprise opportunities that had not moved in three quarters; the company had never employed an enterprise salesperson. Roy's head of growth read the stall as a matter of capacity — nobody senior enough, or free enough, to work accounts of that size — and with the finance lead had costed a candidate at €180,000 a year fully loaded; both assumed that hire would close two enterprise contracts in the second half of 2026. A board member had written the month before that the product might simply have reached the limit of the market it served. Every version of the plan Roy had drafted assumed the hire.

Three sources of capital were live (see **Exhibit 2**). A generalist venture fund offered €6 million at a €24 million valuation, expected the company to triple, and attached a liquidation preference of two times its investment together with a clause committing Racine to pursue any paying customer. A revenue-based lender offered €1.5 million, a quarter of the fund's figure and an eighth of what Terravise had just raised, repaid as a fixed share of monthly receipts — a claim on the same cash the company ran on, rising as revenue rose — with no equity and no change to the board. An agricultural corporate offered €2 million plus access to its distribution, in exchange for exclusivity that would close Racine's retail channel — about 30% of the pipeline — and a seat on the board.

Roy tracked four measures weekly. Runway was cash divided by net monthly burn, expressed in months; below six months, in her view, a founder negotiated from need rather than from strength. ARR stood at €1.4 million against the €3 million she took to be the level at which a further raise would be routine. An internal index the team called identity alignment, scored from 0 to 100 and built from customer-trust interviews and staff mission-fit surveys, stood at 82. A fourth line, network leverage, recorded in euros the value Racine had actually converted out of Roy's relationships. It read zero.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was not in dispute. At €95,000 a month, €855,000 was gone by December 2026. ARR stayed near €1.4 million. And the enterprise accounts that were Racine's growth plan would be contested by a rival with €12 million and eighteen new employees.

## What Roy Controlled

Roy controlled four things in sequence, and each had to be settled before the next.

She had to name the cause of the stall, choosing among a product that had reached its market ceiling, sales capacity that had never been resourced, a mission that capped the addressable market, or a poor quarter that would correct itself. She had to state the runway: more than a year, about nine months, or a finance matter that would resolve once growth resumed. She had to say which attributes of Racine were not available to trade — the customer selectivity and staff mission-fit that had shaped her hiring, the growth rate and valuation that would mark Racine as a serious company, or none of them, on the argument that survival preceded character. And she had to set how much of her own quarter she would reserve for the decision itself.

She then had to decide what to do with the relationships: convert none, one, or all three of the offered enterprise pilots; decline the cohort's offer, take the co-marketing alone, or take the co-marketing and the shared engineer; and fix the share of her working time that went to those conversations rather than to investors.

She had to choose one of the three sources of capital, and separately decide whether to accept its conditions as written, negotiate those touching customer selection, channel or board, or take only capital that carried no conditions. Mid-quarter the venture fund brought its deadline forward and the corporate hardened its exclusivity demand. Roy could sign inside the new deadline, hold her position and let a deal slip, or move to whichever source could close fastest and read its conditions afterwards.

Finally she had to commit or defer the €180,000 hire; set the plan's headline target — the tripling the fund's model assumed, €3 million of ARR over eighteen months, or flat spending and a deferred milestone; choose what to lead the board presentation with; and state how firmly she held her own view.

## Closing

At 9:15 that morning Roy wrote three numbers on the whiteboard in her office: 855, 95, 9. Under them she wrote three names — the fund, the lender, the corporate — and beside those, the eleven names of her accelerator cohort. The board met in twelve weeks. The term sheet expired before that.
