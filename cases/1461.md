# Helio Verde Renovables: A Defensible Ranking

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1461 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1461 — *Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis — Site Selection (Helio Verde Renovables)* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Friday, May 8, 2026, at 2:00 p.m., Inés Carvalho, chief executive of Helio Verde Renovables, closed the boardroom door in San Mateo on four function heads and four different rankings of the same four sites. Each had arrived with a one-page note. No two notes put the same site first, and none of them said how its author had weighed one consideration against another.

The Investment Committee would meet on Tuesday, May 12, at 2:00 p.m. By then Carvalho had to name one site for Proyecto Mariposa, a 250-megawatt (MW) solar-and-storage project and the largest asset the company had ever built. She also had to show the committee the weights that had produced the choice. "I do not want to leave with a ranking," she told the room. "I want to leave with a defensible ranking. There is a difference."

## The Protagonist

Carvalho was forty-seven and had run Helio Verde Renovables since the group spun it out in 2022. Before that she had led the parent group's strategy office. Earlier still she had spent eight years on the Latin-America development desk of a large European utility, sitting in site-selection meetings for its wind programme. She had never studied formal decision methods. What she knew about scoring sites she had learned from her former employer's scoring sheets, and from watching one of its wind projects, Brezal Tierras Altas, abandoned in 2017 after the surrounding communities turned against it.

## The Decision

Carvalho had to recommend one of four shortlisted sites, together with an explicit weight vector: eleven criteria, with weights summing to 100, that the committee could interrogate. The committee, not she, would decide. Her job was the recommendation and its defence.

Between Friday afternoon and Tuesday morning the vector would be tested six times. On Friday the team would set its own developer vector. Over the weekend and on Monday, three function heads would each argue for their own emphasis. Diego Restrepo, the chief financial officer, wanted more weight on cost. Mariana Quesada, head of development, wanted more on the community and regulatory license. Pablo Linares, head of engineering, wanted more on solar resource and the grid. The two development-finance lenders expected to anchor the debt, Fondo Atlántico de Desarrollo and Banco Interoceánico de Inversión, would then apply their own view, built around protection against the downside. On Tuesday at 11:00 a.m., with Roberto Solana, chief executive of the parent group, on the speakerphone from Madrid, the team would commit to the vector and the site it would carry into the room.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde Renovables, S.A., was the renewable-energy developer of the Helio Verde group, headquartered in San Mateo, the capital of Costaria. It operated about 480 MW across Costaria, Panama and the Dominican Republic: 180 MW of run-of-river hydro, 220 MW of solar and 80 MW of wind. Proyecto Mariposa combined 200 MW of photovoltaic panels with a battery of 50 MW and 200 megawatt-hours (MWh). Its capital cost was estimated at USD 280 million to USD 340 million, depending on the site.

Three dates boxed the decision in. Costaria's Ministry of Energy and Mines had set December 31, 2028, as the deadline for commercial operation of the auction tranche in which Helio Verde held its 250 MW award. Missing it meant losing the award and a USD 14 million performance bond posted in 2024. With a build cycle of 24 to 32 months, the site, the permits, the construction contract and financial close all had to be settled by late in the third quarter of 2026. Separately, the state-owned utility had told Ana Solana, head of commercial, that the 15-year power purchase agreement (PPA) she had negotiated, at USD 64.50 per MWh fixed in real terms, had to be signed before June 30, 2026. The same PPA would apply at any of the four sites.

## The Situation

The four sites had survived a 28-site screen earlier in the year (see Exhibit 2). Mirador, on a desert plateau in the northwest, had the strongest sun and the lowest modelled cost of energy, but it sat 60 km from a 230-kilovolt substation and had little water. Llanos, cattle pasture in the central plain, was a private freehold 12 km from an existing line, with three villages within 8 km whose view of the project nobody had yet tested. Costa Sur, near the southern port, could connect to the port substation 4 km away and had the best water and labour, but it bordered mangroves under partial protection. Valle Norte, a semi-arid valley in the northeast, had the strongest community position of the four: a lease already signed with the Indigenous Council. The lease ran 15 years against a 25-year asset life, and the grid connection would need a substation upgrade.

The Friday notes split accordingly. Quesada ranked Valle Norte first and Mirador last. Linares ranked Mirador first and Costa Sur last. Restrepo ranked Llanos first and Valle Norte last. Solana ranked Llanos first and Costa Sur last. Quesada carried two precedents into every meeting: Brezal Tierras Altas, and Río Sombrío, a 2018 hydro project that had come within ninety days of losing its concession after a misstep with the local community.

By late afternoon the team had pooled the criteria behind the four notes into eleven, each with an owner, and had agreed a developer vector as its starting point (see Exhibit 1). If nothing changed, that vector would reach the committee untested, attached to whichever site had been argued for longest. Solana had already told Carvalho how the committee worked. It would ask each challenger in turn whether the analysis had been run through his or her lens, and whether the recommendation still stood. Meanwhile the PPA window and the commercial-operation deadline kept running.

## What Carvalho Controlled

At each of the six passes Carvalho set two things. The first was the weight vector: how much the recommendation would rest on the levelized cost of energy (LCOE); on the capital cost of connecting to the grid; on the community license; on biodiversity; on solar irradiance; on the security of land tenure; on water for panel cleaning; on the local workforce; on the regulatory horizon; on the quality of the off-take counterparty; and on the range of financing structures open to the project. The eleven weights had to sum to exactly 100. The second was the site she would name.

The team's working sheet re-ranked the four sites by weighted score whenever a weight moved, with a one-line profile beside each site, and it flagged any vector that barely separated them. After each pass the team logged the site Carvalho had picked, the site her vector ranked first, and whether the two were aligned. It recorded three readings. Defensibility asked whether the pick stood where her own weights put it. Robustness asked whether the pick held when the weights shifted modestly. Mandate compliance asked how far the vector honoured the emphasis that the pass's challenger had asked for. The three readings resolved into a round score, and the round scores accumulated over the six passes.

## Closing

At 2:40 p.m. Carvalho wrote the eleven criteria on the whiteboard, with the developer weights beside them. Restrepo was already recalculating on his laptop. Quesada had opened her file on Río Sombrío. Linares had drawn a line on the wall map from Mirador to the nearest substation. The committee would sit in four days, and the numbers on the whiteboard would have to survive every one of them.
