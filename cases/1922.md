# Maple & Meadow Foods: The Third Campaign

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1922 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1922 — *Make the SHIFT* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 1 June 2026, Nadia Beaulieu set two documents side by side on a table in Maple & Meadow Foods' Vancouver office. The first was a retailer sustainability scorecard that ranked the company 11th of 14 national food brands on helping consumers reduce waste. The second was the closing report on Maple & Meadow's last two consumer campaigns, which had spent CA$6.5 million between them and moved measured household behaviour by less than one per cent.

Beaulieu had thirty days to put a campaign in front of the chief marketing officer and the board, and CA$4.0 million to build it with. The budget was fixed: no top-ups, and whatever she committed was committed. Approval had to come in time to launch before the autumn category review, at which the grocery chain carrying most of Maple & Meadow's volume had warned it might de-list slow-moving lines and give the shelf space to higher-scoring competitors.

## The Protagonist

Nadia Beaulieu, 41, had led sustainability marketing at Maple & Meadow since 2023, after nine years in category management at a larger grocery supplier. She had commissioned neither of the campaigns in the closing report; she had inherited their measurement, and the research she had built since arriving was why the CA$4.0 million sat with her. Grant Whelan, the chief marketing officer, would take her plan to the board; Rosalind Ferreira, who had voted down both previous campaigns, would be at the table.

## The Decision

Beaulieu had to settle four things in thirty days, in the order the board would ask.

## The Company and its Setting

Maple & Meadow Foods Co., founded in Vancouver in 1998, sold refrigerated plant-forward meals, dairy and pantry staples through the national grocery chains. It turned CA$480 million of revenue at a 9 per cent net margin, employed 1,250 people, spent CA$38 million a year on marketing, and reached roughly 6 million Canadian households.

The company had committed publicly to a 2030 emissions target. About 70 per cent of its footprint sat outside its own plants and trucks — in what households bought, how much they threw out, and whether the package went in the right bin. Nothing it did to its own operations would close that share.

## The Situation

The flagship pack had been recyclable for four years and was recycled 28 per cent of the time. The two campaigns had between them reached most of the country, raised stated concern in the tracking study, and left the behaviours where they found them (see **Exhibit 1**).

Ferreira had said at the last review that she would not fund a third campaign that counted people who had seen something. Whelan wanted a plan that would survive the chain's audit too: the retailer had begun checking on-pack environmental claims against their evidence, and an unsupported claim would attach to the brand.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was plain. The rank would stand into the autumn review, the chain would reallocate the shelf space it had warned about, and the 2030 target would stay out of reach while 70 per cent of the footprint sat in behaviour the company did not influence. The four measures the board had set would end where they started: the share of exposed households taking up the behaviour, at 1 per cent; the share still doing it ninety days later, at nothing; a 0–100 reading of how far each claim could be substantiated, at 50; and the Canadian dollars per behaviour actually changed, not yet computable because none had been counted.

## What Beaulieu Controlled

The first of the four was how to describe the problem she wanted funded. She could put it to the board as a gap between what households intended and what they did: Canadians told the tracking study that recycling mattered and did not do it 72 per cent of the time. She could put it as a gap in what households knew, on the evidence that recall of the company's sustainability messages sat below the category average. Or as a gap in the product, on the evidence that the pack was sorted correctly only by households who read the panel.

She then had to name a priority. Recycling the package was a small action repeated weekly, performed at the curb where no neighbour saw it. Reducing food waste at home was also repeated and also private, and gave a household no moment at which it could see what its own restraint added up to. Switching to the plant-forward range was a single decision taken in front of a shelf, bound up with how a shopper thought of themselves. She could make one of the three the priority, or carry all three on one identical message, which was what a national media buy could actually cover.

She had to say which barrier the campaign would remove — that the easy path did not lead to the behaviour, that households did not know, or that households did not care enough — and how much of her team's remaining time went into measuring a baseline before launch, from none of it to all of it, against the time left for the work itself.

The second decision was what each behaviour would be met with. For recycling: printed sorting cues and step prompts on the pack itself; a message telling households how many of their neighbours put the pack in the right bin; or a short film following the pack from the kerb to whatever became of it. For food waste: a monthly statement of what the household had saved in dollars and meals; a message reporting that most households wasted less than they did; or a reminder, timed to the weekly shop, that food thrown out was food paid for. For the switch: a campaign showing a visible and growing number of households making it; automatic re-order prompts in the loyalty app once a shopper had tried the range; or a message tying the choice to what the shopper already believed about themselves. Any message reporting what most other households did could report the wrong thing, and she had to decide how much of the schedule went into pre-testing for that — from none of it to all of it.

The third decision was how to split the CA$4.0 million across five routes to a household, totalling exactly that (see **Exhibit 2**). On-pack cues printed the message on the package. In-store activation put staff and shelf-edge material at the point of choice. The loyalty app carried personalised prompts to its 740,000 enrolled households. Broad digital bought national paid social, online video and display. The retailer partnership co-funded shelf space and opened the chain's purchase data.

She had to choose how the campaign would prove what it claimed: a control group set against loyalty-card records and returned packaging; a post-campaign survey asking households whether they had changed; or impressions and advertising recall reported by the media agency against the plan. And she had to say how much of the money — up to CA$2.0 million — went into recurring prompts and defaults that ran on after the launch spend stopped.

The fourth was how to take it into the room. She could build the pitch on the behaviour, the mechanisms and the measured result; on the five million households the campaign would reach; or on the planet story the creative team had storyboarded. The on-pack line could state a specific benefit she could evidence in an audit, could say that recycling the pack helped save the planet, or could use the broad language of eco-friendly, green and natural. She had to state what evidence would let the pilot scale — ninety-day retention and attributable uptake measured against the control, the uptake spike in launch week, or no gate at all and a national rollout committed immediately — and decide how hard she would hold that line when the board pushed back.

## Closing

At 8:15 that morning Beaulieu wrote the three behaviours down the left of a sheet and ruled two columns beside them: what the household would be asked to do, and what would make it happen twice. The category review was set for the autumn. She had thirty days, CA$4.0 million, and a board that had already paid CA$6.5 million for the two campaigns before hers.
