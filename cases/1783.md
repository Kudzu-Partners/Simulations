# Lumière Diagnostics: Becoming a Leader
**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1783 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1783 — *Becoming a Leader* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 April 2026, Dr. Camille Roche presented the year's roadmap for Lumière Diagnostics' new Solutions division to the executive committee in Paris. Halfway through, the CEO cut her off. "This reads like an R&D wish-list, not a business plan," he said. "I expected a leader, not a scientist with slides." The meeting ended early, and within hours the exchange had travelled through all 240 people in the division Roche had led for three weeks. A pulse survey the next morning put their confidence in the new leadership at 38 percent, against a company norm of 71. Two of six team leads, both passed over for the job Roche now held, asked to transfer out within 48 hours. The executive committee wanted a revised roadmap and a credible leadership stance in ten working days, with no extension.

## The Protagonist

Roche was a scientist by training who had spent her career on Lumière's research bench before the reorganisation that merged the company's R&D and Commercial functions into the new Solutions division, built to shorten the distance between a laboratory discovery and a product a hospital could buy. Her appointment as its first director, three weeks earlier, had skipped two layers of management. She had never run a team larger than a handful of scientists, let alone one this size. The division had no precedent and no playbook, and its first director had just been told, in front of her peers, that she did not yet look like one.

## The Decision

Over the ten days the committee had given her, Roche and the small circle she chose to trust had to work through four linked questions, one per round of the crisis (see Exhibit 2): what kind of problem she actually faced, who would be allowed to see her uncertainty, how she would present herself to the people watching her most closely, and what she would say she stood for once the crisis had passed. Each answer would be visible to the 240 people deciding whether to trust her.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumière Diagnostics SAS was a Paris-based medical-diagnostics company designing and distributing point-of-care testing devices to hospitals and laboratory networks, with EUR 310 million in annual revenue, a 17 percent EBITDA margin, and 1,150 employees across France, Belgium and Spain. It promoted from within almost exclusively, and its most capable scientists were routinely handed bigger teams rather than training in how to run them. The Solutions division — 240 people drawn from separate R&D and Commercial functions — was the company's first attempt to put research and go-to-market work under one roof, and Roche's promotion to run it was itself an unspoken test of whether that model of promotion still worked.

## The Situation

Team confidence in the new leadership stood at 38 percent against the company's usual 71. Two of six team leads had already asked to transfer, a move that would unsettle roughly 50 of the division's 240 people. Three further bench-to-leadership promotions planned for next quarter were on hold. If nothing changed, confidence would stay near 38, the two departures would go through as filed, the three promotions would stay frozen, and the only public word on the new division would remain the CEO's line about a scientist with slides.

## What Roche Controlled

The first question was what had actually just happened. Roche's circle had to settle on what the interruption had really attacked: her own technical competence and whether she still knew the science deeply enough to be trusted; the roadmap itself, too heavy on research and not yet a business plan; whether the two passed-over leads were working to unsettle her position; or her still-provisional sense of herself as a leader. She then had to choose her first move within 48 hours — say little publicly, return to the technical detail she knew best, and let the reaction settle before facing the two departing leads; acknowledge that the roadmap had missed the mark and signal a credible rebuild; argue the roadmap was sound and the CEO had misread it; or attribute the gap to the chaos of the merger and the short timeline. She also had to set, as a percentage, how openly her circle would name the work ahead as being about who she was becoming, not slide quality.

The second question, over the two days that followed, was who would be allowed to see her uncertainty. Four people were available, and she could open up fully to no more than one or two: a trusted peer, honest but not senior enough to lend legitimacy; her former manager, powerful but tied to the identity she was leaving behind; a direct report, closest to the division's daily reality but a subordinate; and an executive coach, neutral but slow and outside the company. Whichever she chose, she still had to decide what she was asking of them: concrete advice on the roadmap and the committee; reassurance that she still belonged, not proof but comfort; direct intervention with the CEO and the departing leads on her behalf; or a place to feel the anxiety without acting on it and decide clearly afterward.

The third question, with five of the ten days gone, was how she would present herself to the two departing leads and the wider division. She had to place herself on a line running from projected certainty to full disclosure of her doubt, and choose an opening line for the two leads: that she did not know if she was the right person for the job; that everything was under control and she expected their full commitment; that the roadmap had missed the mark and she needed their help; or an offer to back their next promotion if they stayed six months and kept it quiet. She also had to decide how much doubt to show — a measured, owned acknowledgement paired with clear direction; none at all; or the full extent of it.

The fourth question, due on day ten in a three-minute meeting with the CEO, was what she would say she stood for. She had to write a leadership purpose — built around hitting the committee's numbers and keeping her job; built around turning laboratory breakthroughs into care patients could reach, by leading scientists she had once been one of; built around being the smartest person in the room, prized for mastery over trust; or built around being an inspiring, innovative leader who drove excellence — and decide how to reframe the roadmap: as the same research-heavy plan with better slides and a summary on top, as a purely commercial plan with the science ambition dropped, or as a business plan linking each research bet to a market outcome and a path back to confidence. She had to sequence a 90-day recovery plan — visible early wins and named commitments followed by steady follow-through, a single grand relaunch event, or silence while results spoke for themselves — and put a number on how firmly she would defend that a held, honest identity mattered more than performed certainty.

Progress showed up in four numbers tracked after every round (see Exhibit 1 for the starting position): team confidence, the pulse-survey figure starting at 38 against the 71 norm; identity integration, a 0-to-100 read on how solidly Roche was holding a leader's sense of herself rather than retreating into the scientist she had been, starting at 30; credibility, a 0-to-100 read on her standing with the executives, the leads and the division, starting at 40; and sustainability, a 0-to-100 read on whether her stance would hold the next time something went wrong, starting at 20.

## Closing

That afternoon, Roche closed her office door for the first time since the meeting and wrote four questions on a single sheet of paper: what had been attacked, who would be allowed to see her doubt, what she would say to the two leads already packing, and what she would tell the CEO in ten days that would still hold ninety days later. Beside the sheet sat the pulse-survey printout at 38 percent and the two transfer requests (see Exhibit 1). She had ten days and no draft of any answer.
