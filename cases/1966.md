# Nordveld Industrial Supplies: The Cost of Serving

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1966 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1966 — *Control the Numbers* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday 19 January 2026, Marieke Doornbos sat in a third-floor room at Nordveld Industrial Supplies' Rotterdam head office while the company's new owner read the quarter out loud. Revenue had closed the year at a record €410 million; operating margin had slipped to 6.5%, or €26.6 million, and free cash flow was weak. The operating partner set the two side by side and asked which one Nordveld's managers were paid to produce.

Doornbos had been Group Controller for eleven days. She left the room with a mandate and a clock: lift operating margin from 6.5% to 9% within two years, and put a credible redesign of how Nordveld measured performance and paid for it on the board's table within one quarter. Failing that, the owner would impose an across-the-board cost cut and a revenue-only target on the sales force.

## The Protagonist

Marieke Doornbos, 44, had come from a Dutch food-ingredients group, where she spent six years as divisional finance director and rebuilt how that division charged shared costs to its plants. Before that, eight years in audit. She was Nordveld's first Group Controller: the role had been created by the incoming owner, and she had been hired by the owner rather than by the chief executive. Her own package moved with the margin number she was being asked to move.

## The Decision

Doornbos had to decide what Nordveld would measure, what it would pay its people for, and what it would do with the customers the new measurements showed were losing money — and to present the three as one system to the same board at the end of the quarter.

## The Company and its Setting

Nordveld Industrial Supplies B.V. distributed bearings, seals, fasteners and fluid-power parts to 3,400 business customers across the Benelux and western Germany. It employed 720 people, including 95 field representatives, ran three distribution centres and its own last-mile fleet, and had closed the year on €410 million of revenue at a 6.5% operating margin (see **Exhibit 1**).

Margin was managed at the gross level, product line by product line, and overhead was spread across accounts in proportion to revenue. The books closed eleven working days after month-end, and above forty performance indicators circulated across the functions, most counting volume; no manager Doornbos had spoken to could name the three that moved margin. Field sales earned 80% of variable pay on revenue and 20% on gross margin, and what it cost to serve an account appeared in no figure a representative saw (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Situation

A pilot commissioned before Doornbos arrived had loaded those costs onto Nordveld's 200 largest accounts. Ranked by operating profit instead of revenue, the bottom fifth lost money: an estimated €6.5 million of profit destroyed each year, invisible in gross-margin reporting. A band of mid-sized accounts earned far more than assumed. The most operating-unprofitable account belonged to the highest-earning representative on the field force.

The owner's team had fixed the four readings Doornbos would be judged on. **Operating margin**, 6.5% against the 9% mandate, was the headline. **The margin leak** was the €6.5 million the loss-making tail destroyed each year, which the redesign was meant to close. **Retained-customer lifetime value** — what the accounts Nordveld kept and developed would earn over the rest of their relationships, discounted to today — stood at €64 million, so the leak could not be closed by shedding customers. The fourth was a **goal-congruence score**, a 0-to-100 reading of whether what Nordveld measured and paid for pulled behaviour toward firm-level margin; it stood at 28. She also had a **change budget of €1.2 million** for retraining, a transition arrangement for the sales force and the pricing system.

Ruud Balder, the commercial director, had told her that re-basing commission on profitability would cost Nordveld several of its best representatives and a quarter's revenue with them, and that the tail's volume absorbed fixed cost across three distribution centres and a fleet. Femke Aalders, who ran operations, had proposed a bonus indicator based on orders shipped: objective, collected daily, a direct read on warehouse effort.

If nothing changed, margin stayed at 6.5%, the €6.5 million kept leaking, the representatives kept being paid for revenue and kept producing it, and the quarter ended with the cost cut and the revenue-only target.

## What Doornbos Controlled

**The diagnosis.** She had to settle what the analysis ranked customers by: revenue, which the commercial organisation ran on; gross margin, which the monthly pack carried by product line; or operating profit after loading each account with what it cost to serve, which nobody at Nordveld had produced. Loading meant choosing costs: the handful the pilot suggested carried most of the difference — order frequency, expedited freight, returns; field selling cost, the largest single line, spread evenly across accounts; or none, gross margin standing in for customer profitability. She had to state what the diagnosis implied about commission: that it worked as designed and revenue pay kept the force hungry; that it directed effort toward the accounts destroying margin; or that pay was a compensation question, separate from the costing one. And she had to fix what share of the 200 largest accounts to load before acting on any of them.

**The measurement system.** How many of the forty-odd indicators survived was hers: all of them, each with an owner and a purpose; a core of three financial numbers; or six to eight spanning financial, customer, process and capability measures, each with a target and a link to margin. One would sit at the centre: orders shipped, already collected daily; total revenue, which the whole company understood; or customer operating margin with cost to serve per order. Aalders' proposal needed its own answer: adopt it, let it lapse without one, or decline it with the reasoning set out and any volume measure paired with average order size and cost per order. Underneath sat the plumbing: leave the eleven-day close and revenue-based allocation alone, shorten the close only, or shorten the close and re-allocate overhead by the activities that drove it. She had also to decide how much design time to spend testing each measure against the ways a capable manager could satisfy it without helping margin.

**Pay and the portfolio.** Variable pay could stay on revenue; move wholly onto customer profitability at once; or be re-weighted toward customer profitability and contribution with a component for developing strategic accounts. Each basis arrived with a transition: immediate effect; an immediate reduction for representatives carrying loss-making accounts; or a phased change with a guarantee period, retraining and conversations with the highest earners. The tail needed an action: exit the whole bottom fifth; leave it in place; or sort it by lifetime value and act differently on each part. One repriced mid-sized account was threatening to leave: withdraw the surcharge, let it go, or hold the surcharge where lifetime value was thin while keeping accounts whose lifetime value was high. The €1.2 million was hers to commit: nothing; €0.4 million on the pricing system alone; or €1.0 million across retraining, the transition arrangement and the pricing system.

**The board review.** One to two quarters on she would take the result back and choose what to lead with: the margin number itself; the fact that revenue had held and little had needed changing; or the margin alongside where representatives now sold, the lifetime value retained and the speed of the close. The operating partner would press on whether the gain was durable or price rises that would reverse at the next tender: assert durability, keep to the quarter's margin, or produce the reading of where the sales force had shifted its mix. She would name one measure or incentive to change next — restore a volume measure, change nothing, or refine the weakest measure and tighten its guard — and say how firmly she held that aligned pay and a sorted customer book beat a single across-the-board cut.

## Closing

At half past seven the next morning, Doornbos laid the pilot's ranking of the 200 largest accounts beside the commission schedule. The quarter had started the previous day. The €6.5 million was on the first page; the name at the top of the commission schedule was on the last.
