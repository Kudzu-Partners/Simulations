# Maya's Ice-Cream Stall: Five Days on the Beach

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-752 · **Companion simulation:** Express 752 — *Maya's Ice-Cream Stall: Demand & Supply* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On the evening of Sunday, August 23, 2026, Maya Hartley sat on an upturned milk crate behind the ice-cream stall on Brighton Beach and looked at a price board with no price on it. For twenty-two summers her aunt had chalked the day's price on that board before the first customer arrived. From the next morning, the job was Maya's.

She had five trading days, from Monday to the Friday that opened the August bank-holiday weekend. Each morning before 9:00 she had to decide two things: the price of one cone for the whole day, and how many cones to make. Anything she had not sold by closing time melted and went in the bin. On Monday morning the freezer would be waiting for a number.

## The Protagonist

Maya Hartley was eighteen. She had finished school in June and would start a college course in September. She had worked at the stall on Saturdays for three summers, serving customers, wiping the counter and counting change, but she had never set a price or decided how much to make.

Her aunt, Rosa Hartley, had run the stall for twenty-two summers until she retired at the end of July 2026. Rosa now lived inland and had promised to phone every evening. Maya's cousin Jamie, fifteen, would work the counter beside her.

## The Decision

Each morning Maya had to fix the day's price and the number of cones to make by 9:00, when the machine started. The stall opened at 10:00. The price stayed the same all day. Rosa's rule was one price on the board, with no changes once the first cone was sold. There was no second batch either. The machine filled the freezer once, in the morning, and that was the day's stock.

Two of Rosa's sayings pulled in different directions. "A melted cone is seventy pence down the drain," she liked to say. She also said: "A customer who walks away from an empty freezer takes the money with them." Maya would have to weigh those two kinds of loss five times, and the forecast did not show the same weather two days running (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

The stall was a small wooden kiosk on the lower promenade of Brighton Beach, between the pier and a row of beach huts. It sold one product: a soft vanilla cone, made on site. Its customers were day-trippers, families, students and people walking their dogs. Most of them saw the board as they walked past and decided in a few seconds whether to stop.

Trade depended on the weather. On a hot day the promenade filled by mid-morning. On a cool day it stayed half empty. Rosa's notebooks from twenty-two summers said the same two things in different handwriting. When the price on the board went up, fewer people stopped. When the sun came out, more people stopped at any price. The notebooks did not say by how much.

For years there had been no other ice-cream seller in sight of the stall. In August, the empty kiosk next door had been painted blue and white. A sign in its window read *Seaside Scoops — Opening Soon*. It gave no date, and nobody on the promenade had met the owner.

## The Situation

Each cone cost Maya £0.70 to make: milk, cream, sugar, the wafer cone and a napkin. She paid that cost for every cone she made, whether it sold or not. The freezer held at most 300 cones, so she could not make more than 300 in a day. The price board had slots from £1.00 to £4.00, in steps of 5p, and Rosa had never priced outside it (see Exhibit 1).

The stall started the week with nothing in the till: £0.00. Every evening Maya would count the day's takings, subtract the cost of every cone she had made, and write down the day's profit. Because the till began empty, the money in it at any moment was also the week's profit so far.

Rosa added one more measure. Each evening she would give the day between zero and three stars for how well Maya had read the beach: how close the price and the number of cones had come to what that day's market called for. Fifteen stars made a perfect week. On Friday evening the stars would be turned into a mark out of 100.

What a bad day looked like was clear enough. If Maya made no cones, the stall earned nothing. If she made cones that nobody wanted at her price, each one cost 70p and melted. If she made too few, the freezer emptied early and the queue walked away with its money. Jamie had a clicker to count the people who asked for a cone after the freezer was empty.

## What Maya Controlled

Every morning Maya controlled two numbers. The first was the **price per cone** on the board, anywhere from £1.00 to £4.00. The second was the **number of cones to make**, from none up to what the freezer could hold that day. Everything else followed from those two choices and from the people on the beach.

Each evening her notebook recorded six things for the day: the **price** she had charged, the cones she had **made**, the cones she had **sold**, the cones that had **melted**, the day's **profit** and Rosa's **stars**. Beside them she wrote down the day's **takings** and the **cost** of the cones she had made, how many people had wanted a cone at her price, and how many of them had gone without because the freezer was empty. On her phone she kept four running figures: the **money in the till**, the **week's profit**, the **stars earned** so far, and the **last day's profit**.

She could not control the weather, the price of milk, the number of people on the promenade or what anyone else on the seafront did. She could not change the price halfway through the day or make a second batch. Each morning the local radio would bring the forecast and the news, and she would hear it at the same time as everyone else.

## Closing

At 8:40 on Monday, August 24, Maya chalked the date on the board and left a space beside it. The radio said sunny and mild. Jamie was unstacking wafer cones. The machine was warm, the freezer was empty, and Rosa's phone went to voicemail. Maya picked up the chalk.
