# Helio Verde Beverages: The Forecast After the Break

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1371 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1371 — *Time Series for Business: Stationarity, ARIMA, Forecasting* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, January 12, 2026, at 08:05, Matías Arrieta-Solano opened a memo that had reached the strategy office by mistake. Inés Garrido-Lobos, chief financial officer (CFO) of Helio Verde Beverages, had written it to the executive committee the previous Friday, once the December books had closed. Its second paragraph carried a number the quantitative desk already knew. The rolling 12-month forecast for regular Helio Cola, built in a spreadsheet from a moving average and a fixed seasonal index, had missed actual sales by an average of 18% a month over the last 24 months. Its third paragraph carried a complaint the desk had not heard before: the forecast reached the board as a single line, with nothing to say how far the real number might land from it.

By 11:00 the memo had become an assignment. Garrido-Lobos wanted a proper forecasting framework, built on the 96 months of regular Helio Cola sales from January 2018 to December 2025 and tested against 2025 as a hold-out year. It had to be ready for the executive committee on Thursday, February 5, 2026, where she would present the 2026 forecast. Arrieta-Solano was to make the build's eight methodological calls in order, from the first look at the raw series to the slide the board would see. She would mark each call as it reached her.

## The Protagonist

Arrieta-Solano, twenty-six, had joined the strategy office's quantitative desk fourteen months earlier, after a master's degree in applied econometrics. He had estimated seasonal models in coursework and had since built the desk's price-response dashboards. He had never put a forecast of his own in front of a board. He had never had to defend one against the method it would replace.

## The Decision

Arrieta-Solano had to decide, one stage at a time, how the series would be diagnosed, transformed, modelled, repaired, compared and presented. Each call would be marked before the next one opened (see Exhibit 2). With each he could attach a single line of rationale, written for the CFO's slide.

Two colleagues had already given him their views. Rosa Elgueta-Marín, head of demand planning, had built the spreadsheet method in 2019. She pointed out that the board had planned production on its single line for six years without once asking for a range. Federico Lagos-Urrutia, vice-president of commercial, had launched Helio Cola Zero. He held that the regular line would win back most of its lost volume once the novelty of the new product wore off.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde Beverages, S.A., the soft-drinks division of a diversified consumer-goods group, reported revenue of about US$410 million in 2025 and employed 1,600 people across two bottling plants (see Exhibit 1). Its flagship was Helio Cola. The division measured volume in units of one thousand litres. Its home market lay in the southern hemisphere, so cola demand peaked in December, at the height of summer, and bottomed out in June.

In April 2024 the division added Helio Cola Zero. The commercial team's tracking put the volume Zero had drawn from regular Helio Cola at about 8%. The spreadsheet method had not been re-estimated since the launch. Its seasonal index still averaged months from before and after it.

## The Situation

The series sat on Arrieta-Solano's screen as a single line of 96 points. Annual volume had risen from 317,074 units in 2018 to 357,083 in 2023, then fallen to 345,189 in 2024 and held at 345,850 in 2025 (see Exhibit 1). The lowest month in the record was June 2019, at 20,699 units; the highest was December 2023, at 36,809.

The desk tracked four figures and logged them after every call. The first was the forecast's mean absolute percentage error (MAPE): the average size of a monthly miss as a share of actual sales. The spreadsheet's stood at 18.0%. The second was the mean absolute scaled error (MASE), which compared the forecast's misses with those of a rule that simply repeated the same month of the previous year. A value below 1.0 meant beating that rule; the spreadsheet stood at 1.62.

The third was the coverage of the 95% prediction interval: the share of months in the 2025 hold-out year whose actual sales fell inside the band. A well-calibrated band captured about 95% of them. A higher figure meant a band too wide to plan against; a lower one meant a band too narrow to trust. The spreadsheet produced no band, so the figure had never been measured.

The fourth was Garrido-Lobos's methodology mark: two points for a call she judged rigorous, one for a partial answer, none otherwise, sixteen in all, starting at zero. A decision log kept each call, its points, and the error and coverage recorded after it. A chart showed the 96 months with the 2026 forecast and its 95% band.

If the desk changed nothing, the 2026 forecast would go to the committee as the spreadsheet produced it. It would carry twelve monthly points, an error that had averaged 18% for two years, a seasonal index blind to the launch, and no range around any month.

## What Arrieta-Solano Controlled

The **first call** was what the raw plot showed. The series might be close enough to stable to model as it stood. It might show an upward drift together with an annual cycle and a shift in level around April 2024. It might show drift without seasonality, or seasonality without drift.

The **second** was the transformation fed to the model, made with the unit-root tests on the desk. He could keep the raw series; take first differences alone, if they passed; take seasonal differences alone, to remove the annual cycle; or take logarithms and then both first and seasonal differences.

The **third** was the model order read from the autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation plots. The candidates were a seasonal autoregressive integrated moving-average (ARIMA) model of order (1,1,1)(1,1,1) on a twelve-month season; a twelfth-order autoregression, matching the spike at lag 12; a non-seasonal ARIMA(1,1,1); or a larger (2,1,2)(2,1,2), fitted to be safe.

The **fourth** was the rule for choosing among fitted candidates. He could take the highest in-sample R-squared, or rely on the Akaike criterion. He could prefer the Schwarz criterion, which penalised extra parameters harder. Or he could validate each candidate on rolling windows of data it had not been fitted on.

The **fifth** was how to treat the bias that the residuals showed after April 2024. He could set it aside as small, or run a second test for leftover autocorrelation. He could run a formal test for a break at the known date, or re-test the residuals for a unit root.

The **sixth** was the repair, if any. He could add an intervention term marking every month from April 2024 onward. He could refit only on data from April 2024, nine months at first and growing. He could leave the model unchanged and let the shift wash through, or switch to a long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network.

The **seventh** was the recommendation, once the 2025 hold-out errors of seven methods were in. The options were the LSTM; an equal-weighted average of Holt-Winters exponential smoothing, the seasonal ARIMA and the Prophet model; the seasonal ARIMA alone; or Prophet, with its automatic detection of change points.

The **eighth** was what the board slide said about uncertainty. It could show twelve point forecasts and nothing else, or each point with a 95% interval. It could add 80% and 95% intervals with the 2025 coverage record and a quarterly re-fit schedule. Or it could show both intervals with a footnote that bands widen with the horizon.

## Closing

At 11:20 Arrieta-Solano opened the 96 months in one window and an empty model log in the other. Elgueta-Marín's note about the board sat in his inbox, with Lagos-Urrutia's forecast of a recovery just above it. The first call would reach Garrido-Lobos before lunch. The committee met in twenty-four days.
