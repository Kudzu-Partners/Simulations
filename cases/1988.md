# Tulsi Naturals: Containing a Marketplace Rumor

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1988 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1988 — *Word on the Street* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, June 16, 2026, Meera Kulkarni opened the war-room dashboard in Tulsi Naturals' Pune office and read four numbers that had each moved the wrong way over a single weekend. Net sentiment on the company's hero product — its cold-pressed coconut oil — had fallen from +71 to +12. Rumor reach stood at 2.4 million impressions, roughly doubling every twenty-four hours since Saturday. Conversion on the marketplace listing had dropped from 6.8% to 4.1%. At that rate the hero product was losing ₹1.7 crore a month.

The cause was a three-minute video posted on Saturday, June 13, by a consumer with 120,000 followers, claiming an independent laboratory test had found mineral oil in Tulsi's cold-pressed coconut oil. The laboratory was not named and no report was produced. By Tuesday a consumer-affairs journalist had asked for comment inside a seventy-two-hour publishing window, and two marketplace category managers wanted a documented response within five business days or a precautionary banner would go on the listing. Kulkarni had to decide what Tulsi would say, to whom, how fast, and how to spend the ₹1.65 crore left in June's digital budget.

## The Protagonist

Meera Kulkarni, 34, had run digital marketing at Tulsi Naturals for three years, after five years buying performance media in Mumbai. Her largest contribution had been the verified-buyer review program, which invited customers who had actually purchased to write about the product. The 41,000 reviews at 4.6 stars were the number she quoted in every budget conversation, and the argument she had won with it was that Tulsi's word of mouth beat any reach it could buy.

## The Decision

Over two weeks Kulkarni had to commit, in sequence, to a written reading of what the wave of negative reviews was; a response posture and the messages carrying it; an allocation of the month's ₹1.65 crore across five competing lines; and adjustments once the second week's numbers arrived. The journalist's window closed within seventy-two hours, the category managers' deadline ran to five business days, and the month's budget could not be carried forward.

## The Company and its Setting

Tulsi Naturals Pvt. Ltd. sold cold-pressed oils, herbal supplements and ayurvedic skincare from Pune, and had booked ₹220 crore of revenue in FY25. Most of that flowed through large online marketplaces. Tulsi had served 1.9 million customers, and one product — the cold-pressed coconut oil — accounted for 31% of revenue on its own.

The company had been built with very little paid media: its growth came from customers telling other customers, and from the review page that recorded them doing it. That page also fed something Tulsi did not control: the marketplaces ranked listings partly on ratings and review activity, so a listing whose rating fell could lose search position without anyone deciding it should.

## The Situation

Within seventy-two hours of the post, 380 new one- and two-star reviews had landed on the hero product's page, and the claim had spread across Instagram, WhatsApp forwards and the marketplace's own question-and-answer thread (see **Exhibit 1**). Some of the new reviews were near-identical in wording. Others named batch codes, described a change in smell, and asked what the company was going to do.

The community team's coding of the 380 put roughly 40% with buyers who appeared genuinely worried and roughly 60% with a coordinated pile-on. The community lead disputed her own team's coding, arguing the copy-paste patterns ran deeper than the sample caught. The quality head took the opposite view: if even 40% were real customers, the allegation deserved to be treated as a possible product failure until Tulsi proved otherwise. Tulsi held a current third-party laboratory certificate for the batch under attack and batch-level traceability records, and had published neither.

The listing's fall in search position had begun on Sunday, before most of the review damage registered internally. Nobody could say where the reach curve would stop; it had doubled three times.

If Tulsi did nothing, the arithmetic was not in dispute: reach would keep compounding while the video remained the only account on the record, the journalist would publish with or without a comment, the category managers would reach their deadline, and the hero product would keep losing ₹1.7 crore a month against a ₹1.65 crore budget.

## What Kulkarni Controlled

She was measured on four numbers: **net sentiment**, an index from −100 to +100 scoring the tone of the hero product's review page; **rumor reach**, impressions of the claim across the three channels; **hero-SKU conversion**, the share of listing visitors who bought; and **campaign ROI**, the revenue the response protected against the budget it consumed — the one the chief executive asked for by name.

**Reading the wave.** Kulkarni had to record what kind of event this was: a coordinated smear, with the 380 reviews treated as inauthentic and dismissed as a block; a confirmed quality failure, with the allegation accepted and the product recalled; or an unresolved mix customers could not settle for themselves. She had to state where the spread came from: two channels — the video and WhatsApp forwards — credited by one estimate with roughly 80% of traffic; reposts scattered too widely for any channel to be worth targeting; or a spread whose location mattered less than waiting for the peak and the facts. She had to name what compounded the loss beyond the rumor: the ranking system's reaction to the rating drop; the rumor and nothing else; or a price problem, answered by cutting the hero product's price. And she had to fix how early on the doubling curve Tulsi's first substantive response landed, from within half a day of the post to five days out.

**Saying something.** Four postures sat on the table: silence, on the reasoning that answering feeds a rumor; a factual rebuttal publishing the laboratory certificate; a reply to customer questions publishing the certificate and batch traceability alongside it; or a correction rejecting the allegation and demanding a takedown. For the review page: a paid five-star drive putting roughly 600 fresh reviews up within forty-eight hours; a verified-buyer drive with reposted customer content and paid micro-advocates; or nothing, leaving the statement to carry the load. For the journalist: an answer inside her window, no comment, or a line calling the claim a rival's smear. And a line fixed in advance on amplification: no manufactured or incentivized reviews at any point; a few seeded reviews left undisclosed; or no line at all.

**Spending the money.** Five budget lines competed for the month's total (see **Exhibit 2**): paid search and marketplace ads, influencer and micro-advocate fees, community and rapid-response staffing, public relations and a laboratory fact sheet, and a contingency reserve. She also had to settle the order of operations — spend on ads at once to force the demoted listing back up the rankings, spend nothing on ads at all, or repair the page first and scale paid media once it converted the traffic it got.

**Week two.** When the next numbers arrived she would answer the category managers: supply the documented response inside their deadline, decline to document and state the wave was inauthentic, or answer on every platform the claim had reached. She would re-cut the budget — cutting what had not moved its metric and scaling what had, freezing the allocation as set, or raising paid media regardless of conversion. She would decide what Tulsi kept: review-velocity monitoring, a permanent advocate program and a twelve-hour rapid-response protocol; more always-on advertising; or nothing. For the chief executive, she would lead with the impression counts, with the platform algorithm and the accuser, or with all four numbers and the revenue saved. And she had to state, as a percentage she would be held to, how firmly she believed supplying evidence and genuine customer voices would beat out-shouting the rumor.

## Closing

At 9:20 a.m. Kulkarni copied the four numbers onto the whiteboard and drew a line under them. The journalist's window closed in seventy-two hours, the category managers' deadline five business days out, and the ₹1.65 crore had to be committed before the month ran down.
