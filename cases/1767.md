# Halden & Rowe: Friction or Fuel

On the morning of 4 March 2026, Priya Anand's phone showed the message she had spent three weeks chasing: the transformation steering committee at a national retail bank had approved Halden & Rowe's £12 million, 14-month digital-transformation programme — the largest contract in the firm's history — on one explicit condition. The delivery team had to "reflect the diversity of our customer base." Anand, the programme's Project Lead, had five working days to compose an eight-person pod from a bench of fourteen candidates. The thesis that had won the contract was one she had pitched herself: diverse teams outperform homogeneous ones, but only if someone manages the friction they generate.

The deadline was only the first of several. Her operating plan for the engagement called for a formal read of team friction partway through discovery, a fixed budget of attention to spend fixing whatever it found, and a Day-45 checkpoint at which she would stand in front of the client sponsor and defend whether the diverse team she built had actually outperformed the safer one she had turned down. Her own compensation, like every principal's at the firm, moved with the contract's realised margin and with whether the client renewed.

## The Protagonist

Priya Anand, 41, had led Halden & Rowe's operations-transformation practice since 2021, having joined the firm in 2013 from a Big Four consultancy's banking-operations group. She had built the Inclusion Dividend argument the firm now marketed to clients — that cognitively diverse delivery teams outperform homogeneous ones, provided the disagreement they produce is actively managed — and had used it personally to win this bid. This was the first time she would have to prove the thesis on a contract of this size, in front of the client who had bought it.

## The Decision

Anand's first decision was who sat in the eight seats. The bench of fourteen candidates split, roughly, along two axes: how senior and delivery-tested a person was, and how much they added to the pod's mix of professional backgrounds, thinking styles, and visible difference from one another. The highest-credential candidates on the bench clustered into a near-homogeneous pod that had shipped similar programmes before; the most cognitively and demographically diverse candidates clustered at the other end, with less seniority behind them. No combination of eight was both maximally diverse and maximally senior (see Exhibit 2); every seat filled from one cluster was a seat not filled from the other. Alongside the eight names, she had to name which kind of conflict she expected to have to manage inside the pod she built: a disagreement about the substance of the work itself, a dispute about roles and who actually held decision rights, or friction between the people themselves that had nothing to do with the work.

## The Company and its Setting

Halden & Rowe was a London-headquartered management and technology consultancy, founded in 2004, with 1,180 people across offices in London, Manchester and Lisbon. Its 2025 fee income was £196 million, at a 14% operating margin — roughly £27.4 million. The firm billed by staffing projects; a single flagship engagement that slipped could erase a quarter's margin on the account. The bank contract carried a 30% margin, well above the firm average, priced on the strength of the Inclusion Dividend claim, with an option for the bank to renew at £30 million if the programme delivered (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

By the time Anand opened the bench list, the programme's four scoreboards all read zero, because nothing had happened yet. Cognitive Diversity would track how much the eight people she chose differed from one another in background and thinking style. Decision Quality would track how good the pod's calls turned out to be as the work proceeded. On-time Delivery would track the probability the programme hit its schedule. Return on Inclusion — the number the bank's sponsor would actually ask about at the Day-45 checkpoint — would track whether the diversity premium she had sold had shown up in the results, and not only in the org chart.

The discovery phase ran six weeks; the first client checkpoint fell on day 45. Whichever way Anand built the pod, the Day-45 checkpoint would ask for more than the roster itself — decision quality, delivery, and whether the diversity premium the bank had paid for had actually shown up in the results.

## What the Protagonist Controlled

Once the pod was staffed, her plan called for a formal read of team friction partway through discovery: classifying whatever disputes had emerged as a disagreement about the work itself, a dispute about process and decision rights, or a clash between people, and rating how serious each had become. With Day 45 following soon after, she would have a fixed budget of six attention points to spread across up to three interventions — never enough to fully resource everything at once — choosing for each dispute from the same menu: put a mediator directly between the two individuals; keep the disagreement structured and let the argument continue; separate the people involved from each other entirely; clarify, formally, who held the actual decision rights; or leave the dispute to continue for now, unaddressed.

If, later, any of her most diverse hires signalled through People & Culture that they were considering leaving, she would have to choose how to respond — offer a retention bonus and change nothing else about how the role worked; name the issue directly, equalize airtime, and reshape their role around their strength; let them go and backfill the seat from elsewhere on the bench; or focus on delivering the programme and assume they would simply stay. At the Day-45 checkpoint itself, she would choose how to frame the result to the bank: emphasize how well the team was getting along without detailing whatever disputes had occurred; present the decision-quality and on-time numbers and name, specifically, which friction she had chosen to keep; or point to the diversity of the org chart itself as proof the dividend had been delivered. Alongside the pitch, she would state, on a five-point scale, how confident she was that the diverse pod had actually beaten a homogeneous one.

## Closing

At her desk, Anand had the bench list open in one window and the five-working-day deadline in her calendar in the other. Whatever she chose in the next few days, the eight names she picked would be the only team she had for the length of the engagement.
