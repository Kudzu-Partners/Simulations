# Brackenfold Foods: Twelve Desks and One Signature

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-791 · **Companion simulation:** Express 791 — *The Org Chart Game: Structure, Span & Delegation* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, February 2, 2026, Nadia Okonjo-Price began her fifth week as chief operating officer of Brackenfold Foods by counting the approval requests in the founder's tray. There were forty-one; the oldest had been waiting nine working days. Graham Pell, who had started the company in a rented unit in Wakefield in 1998, still signed every price change, every new label, every hire and every purchase order above £5,000. The board's non-executive chair, Margaret Lowe, had asked Okonjo-Price for a first redesign at the board meeting of Thursday, February 26.

It would be the first of five. The board had set aside one meeting in each of February, April, June, September and November 2026, and each meeting would settle one question about how the company was organised. The November meeting would receive a final restructure memo that put the whole chart on one page.

## The Protagonist

Okonjo-Price was forty-four. She had spent fifteen years at a larger food group, running a 600-person plant and then a supply-chain function across three countries. She had redrawn organisation charts before. She had never done it with the founder still in the building, still signing, and still the largest shareholder.

## The Decision

At each board meeting Okonjo-Price had to put a specific proposal to the directors: how much of Pell's authority to hand down and to how many people; how many layers of management the company should carry; how it should be organised around its product lines; which decisions its EU business could take for itself; and, finally, the shape of the whole chart (see Exhibit 2). Each meeting would take the company as it then stood. Nothing approved in February could be quietly reversed in April without the board noticing.

## The Company and its Setting

Brackenfold Foods Ltd was a family-owned manufacturer with 200 employees and revenue of about £26 million in 2025. It made three product lines, snacks, chilled ready meals and drinks, at its Wakefield plant and at a smaller site near Venlo in the Netherlands, which supplied its customers in the European Union. About three-quarters of sales went to UK retailers and food-service customers; the rest went to the EU.

The company had grown by adding products faster than it added management. Until 2019 it had made only snacks. Meals and drinks had arrived in quick succession, each run by a product manager who reported to Pell, and each sharing the same production manager, the same marketing team and the same finance office as the snacks business.

## The Situation

Twelve people reported to Pell directly: the heads of production, quality, sales, marketing, finance, human resources, procurement and logistics; the manager of the Venlo site; and the three product managers. Each of them brought decisions to him, and most waited. In November 2025 a national supermarket chain had offered Brackenfold a private-label drinks contract worth £1.2 million. Recipe, packaging, price, production slot and label each needed Pell's signature, the approvals took twenty-three working days, and the retailer's window had been fifteen. The contract went to a competitor.

The board tracked three figures on its dashboard (see Exhibit 1). A **decision-speed index**, built from approval times and the staff pulse survey, stood at 50 out of 100. A **communication index**, measuring how fast and how accurately information moved between the floor and the top, also stood at 50. **Management cost**, the salaries and on-costs of everyone whose job was to manage others, ran at 12% of revenue, about £3.1 million a year.

Three pieces of advice sat on Okonjo-Price's desk, and they did not agree. A consultant Pell had hired in the autumn had drawn a new chart with three management layers between Pell and the shop floor; the first appointments were due in March. The finance director, Tom Adeyemi-Clarke, had written in the margin of that chart that every layer was a salary line and that the company's margin could not carry many more. And Pieter Aalders, who ran the Venlo site, had sent her a two-page note arguing that EU customers wanted different flavours and pack sizes and that he could not keep asking Wakefield for permission to sell them. The head of marketing had replied to the same note that a brand run from two places soon became two brands.

Pell's own view was shorter. He had signed everything for twenty-eight years, he told her, and the company was still there.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic of the tray was not in dispute. Pell would keep twelve direct reports, the company would keep adding products and customers, and each new product and customer would add to the queue in front of one desk. The consultant's layers would arrive in March whatever the board decided about authority.

## What Okonjo-Price Controlled

For February she had to decide what to do about Pell's role. The options were to change nothing; to delegate authority to his direct reports without changing the chart; to add a layer of management between Pell and those reports without changing who decided what; or to delegate and add a layer together. With that she had to set how many people should report to Pell directly, anywhere from one to the twelve who did now.

For April, with the consultant's layers in place, she had to decide how many layers of management the company should carry, from one to six. The consultant's chart had three.

For June she had to propose the basic structure for three product lines selling in two markets. It could be functional, organised by department as it was; divisional, with snacks, meals and drinks each holding its own production, sales and quality; a matrix, in which people reported both to a function and to a product line; or a hybrid, with functional departments at headquarters and product divisions beneath them.

For September she had to allocate five decision areas between headquarters in Wakefield and the EU business: pricing, product design, hiring, marketing and finance. Each could be held centrally or handed to the region, one by one.

For November she had to write the memo. It would set the final number of management layers, from one to six; the typical number of people each manager would lead, from two to fourteen; the primary structure, from the same four types; and an overall delegation pattern: low and centralised, medium and mixed, or high and decentralised.

After each meeting the board would record the decision-speed and communication indices, management cost as a share of revenue, the number of layers and the span of the chart it had approved. It would also score her proposal out of 100, the five meetings adding up to a total out of 500, and at the end of the year Lowe would rate the programme as a whole.

## Closing

At 17:30 Okonjo-Price took the tray to Pell's office herself. He was on the phone to a supplier. She set it down, waited until he had finished, and asked whether he could spare her an hour before the twenty-sixth. He looked at the tray, then at her, and said he could spare her thirty minutes on Thursday, after he had signed these.
