## Abstract

The Head of Analytics Engineering at the case-universe Mexico City-headquartered mid-cap e-commerce marketplace had been asked, in the March 2026 VP-Data mandate, to deliver a Q2 2026 query-review rubric for the analytics-engineering team in response to a Q1 2026 internal audit that found 42% of business-facing analytical reports were either answering the wrong question or answering the right question against the wrong table. The audit had pulled six representative cases from six different business stakeholders — a sales-ops request for "top customers by revenue" that the analytics engineer had returned against gross bookings rather than net revenue; a category-manager request for "products sold in every region" that had been answered with a simple inner join and missed 34% of qualifying products; a finance request for "month-over-month by category" that had been solved with a self-join rather than a window function and ran for 47 minutes; a CRM request for "cohort retention at 30/60/90 days" that had rounded the cohort boundaries and over-reported retention by seven percentage points; an ops request for "anomalous order activity" that had been answered with a simple threshold rather than a rolling standard deviation and missed two out of three real anomalies; and a CFO request for "customer lifetime value with survival weighting" that had been answered without NULL handling on unfinished cohorts and over-reported CLV by 22%. The March 2026 mandate asked the Head of Analytics Engineering to solve all six cases cleanly, document the translation of the question into the SQL, and commit to a query-review rubric that would govern the team's 2026 output. The case opens at 7:36 a.m. on Wednesday, April 22, 2026, at the marketplace's Mexico City head office as **Yolanda Bezuidenhout-Ramos**, a 41-year-old Head of Analytics Engineering, prepares the six-case commitment.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the Case-1 warm-up (top five customers by revenue last quarter; GROUP BY customer, SUM(revenue), ORDER BY DESC, LIMIT 5) and articulate why the apparently-trivial query hides a question-framing decision — gross vs. net revenue, order-date vs. ship-date, invoice-currency vs. reporting-currency — and why the SQL only looks simple.
2. Analyse the Case-2 relational-division pattern (products sold in every region; COUNT(DISTINCT region) per product compared against total regions) and articulate why the naive inner join returns the set of products sold in *any* region and misses the logic of "in every region" — and why the business-English "in every" is a relational-division signature.
3. Diagnose the Case-3 window-function pattern (month-over-month by category; LAG(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY month)) and the Case-4 cohort-retention pattern (CTE with first-order dates, JOIN back to orders, COUNT DISTINCT in a 30/60/90-day window) and articulate why window functions and CTEs are the operational levers that turn otherwise-nested subqueries into readable, reviewable SQL.
4. Respond to the Case-5 rolling-anomaly pattern (STDDEV in a window; flag observations more than three standard deviations from the rolling mean) and the Case-6 survival-weighted CLV pattern (three chained CTEs, weighted sum of future revenue by survival probability, NULL handling on unfinished cohorts) — articulating why detecting the anomaly is the easy step and explaining it is the hard step, and why NULL handling on unfinished cohorts is the single most common CLV mis-specification.
5. Design the query-review rubric that prices question-translation at 60% of the review weight, SQL correctness at 25%, query performance at 10% and documentation at 5% — articulating why the craft of analytics engineering is the translation of the business question into a precise retrieval, and why the SQL is the transcription.

## The Decision

Bezuidenhout-Ramos had to commit, by the 4:30 p.m. VP-Data pre-brief, the six-case commitment and the query-review rubric.

1. **Case 1 — Top 5 customers by revenue last quarter.** GROUP BY customer, SUM(revenue), ORDER BY DESC, LIMIT 5. Solved in 18 seconds on the operational warehouse. The framing-question is *which revenue* — gross bookings, net revenue, or invoice amount — and the rubric requires the analytics engineer to document that choice in the pull request.
2. **Case 2 — Products sold in every region.** Relational division. `COUNT(DISTINCT region_id) = (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT region_id) FROM regions)` per product. The naive inner join returns products sold in *any* region; the correct query recognises the "in every" signature.
3. **Case 3 — Month-over-month revenue change by category.** Window function. `LAG(revenue) OVER (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY month)`. Solved in 8 seconds on the warehouse; the previous self-join version ran 47 minutes. Window functions are the operational lever.
4. **Case 4 — Cohort retention at 30, 60, 90 days.** CTE with first-order-date per customer, JOIN back to orders, COUNT DISTINCT in each window. CTE saves three levels of subquery; the rubric requires the CTE to be named for what it represents rather than for the order of execution.
5. **Case 5 — Rolling-window anomaly detection.** STDDEV in a window; flag observations more than three sigma from the rolling mean. Detecting the anomaly is easy; explaining it is hard — the rubric requires the analytics engineer to annotate at least one hypothesised cause for each flag.
6. **Case 6 — Survival-weighted CLV.** Three chained CTEs — a first CTE for historical revenue, a second CTE for the survival curve, and a third CTE for the weighted future revenue. NULL handling on unfinished cohorts is mandatory; the rubric requires an explicit WHERE or COALESCE clause for every unfinished cohort.

On Bezuidenhout-Ramos's working rubric, the six solved cases anchored a query-review scorecard with four categories: question-translation (60%), SQL correctness (25%), query performance (10%) and documentation (5%). The rubric replaced the prior scorecard that had priced SQL correctness at 70% and question-translation at 15%.

## Industry and Market Context

The 2015–2025 analytics-engineering practitioner literature produced three findings the rubric had to respect. ## The Situation

### How we got here

The March 2026 mandate asked for the six-case commitment by Q2. The Q3 2025 memo, the Q4 2025 audit, and the drafting of the query-review rubric had all landed by April 16. The VP-Data had asked Bezuidenhout-Ramos to bring the commitment to the April 22 pre-brief.

### What Bezuidenhout-Ramos knew

The six cases covered the full analytics-engineering stack from trivial aggregation to survival-weighted CLV; window functions and CTEs were the operational levers; question-translation dominated SQL correctness as a predictor of rework; and the NULL-handling-on-unfinished-cohorts failure mode was industry-wide.

### What she did not know

Whether the rubric shift from 15% to 60% question-translation would be read by the engineering team as "downgrading the technical craft" or absorbed cleanly as "re-weighting the operational lever"; whether the rework-rate trajectory would respond within two quarters or lag by a full cycle; whether the six representative cases were a valid sample of the team's actual workload or skewed toward the audit-pulled incidents; and whether the VP-Data would accept a 60% weighting on question-translation or push for a compromise at 45–50%.

### What others were saying

**The Senior Data-Quality Engineer**: *"The NULL-handling clause on Case 6 is the single most important line in the rubric. Do not soften it."*

**The VP-Data**: *"I will defend sixty on question-translation. The team's pushback is absorbable. The board's rework number is not."*

## Options Under Consideration

Bezuidenhout-Ramos had narrowed the space to three rubric postures.

- **Option A — 60% translation / 25% SQL / 10% performance / 5% documentation:** the recommended rubric. Pays for the operational lever; requires the pre-work question-framing exchange; passes the six-case audit. Recommended.
- **Option B — 45% translation / 40% SQL / 10% performance / 5% documentation:** the compromise rubric. Rejected — does not reweight the operational lever enough to change the rework-rate trajectory.
- **Option C — Retain the prior rubric and address the rework rate through headcount growth:** rejected outright — the audit finding specifically rejected the headcount-growth hypothesis.

Her private view was that Option A was the right rubric and that the case most correlated with the rework-rate trajectory was Case 6 — survival-weighted CLV — because the NULL-handling failure mode was the one the business stakeholders and the analytics engineers both had to understand jointly.
