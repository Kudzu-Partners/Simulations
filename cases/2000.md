# Nordlys Forsikring: The Only Promotion

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-2000 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2000 — *Fair Enough* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 10 March 2026, Marte Lindeland read two documents that reached her desk within an hour of each other. The first was an open internal complaint from a mid-career claims assessor about a promotion made eighteen months earlier and settled, it said, behind a closed door for reasons nobody had written down. The second was a screenshot of a message posted in the department's team channel the previous evening: everybody, it said, already knew who was getting the job. By the time Lindeland read it, most of the department had read it too.

The Senior Team Lead of Claims Excellence retired on 1 May. Nordlys Forsikring had budget for exactly one promotion into the role — 140 000 kr a year and a place on the leadership track — and two internal candidates had applied. Lindeland had two weeks to run whatever process she intended to run and announce the outcome. She had been head of the department for six weeks.

## The Protagonist

Marte Lindeland, 41, had spent eleven years at Nordlys, the last four running retail claims out of the Ålesund office, and had moved to Bergen at the end of January to take over Claims Excellence. A qualified marine adjuster, she had built a reputation in Ålesund for settling contested files quickly and telling people plainly what she had decided and why. She had never run a team this large, this technical, or this closely watched. She had inherited the department's numbers, its open complaint and its retirement date on the same Monday, and had met perhaps half of her twenty-eight people.

## The Decision

Lindeland had to allocate one indivisible reward between two people who both had a defensible claim to it, in a department where the previous such allocation had produced a complaint still live.

The candidates were not close substitutes (see **Exhibit 2**). Astrid, 58, had been at Nordlys twenty-two years: the deepest technical underwriter in the building, mentor to roughly half the team, appraisals consistently strong and never spectacular, passed over twice. Several senior assessors had told Lindeland, in different words, that the role was owed. Henrik, 29, had been there four years. He had led the claims-automation pilot that cut average settlement time by 22%, which made him visible to the executive committee as nobody else was, and he had led no one. Several younger assessors had told Lindeland, also in different words, that if the job went by years served their work did not count.

Both claims could not be honoured. What Lindeland could still decide was how the choice was reached, who was heard first, what she wrote down, and what she said to the person who lost.

## The Company and its Setting

Nordlys Forsikring AS, founded in 1986 and headquartered in Bergen, wrote property, marine and commercial liability cover for retail and small-business customers across western Norway: 6.4 billion kr (NOK) of gross written premiums, 720 employees. Claims Excellence was its commercial-claims engine — twenty-eight people settling roughly 14 000 claims a year worth about 1.9 billion kr, whose judgement moved both the loss ratio and what customers said afterwards.

The department was multigenerational by design: eight employees over 55 carrying underwriting judgement built over decades, twelve mid-career professionals, and eight under 30, several hired to push the automation rollout. Nordlys advertised *rettferdighet* — fairness — and flat Nordic leadership as what distinguished it from larger competitors, and had just been named a finalist for a regional Great Place to Work award. The result was announced in eight weeks.

## The Situation

The department's own measures had moved in one direction for two years (see **Exhibit 1**). The perceived-fairness score from the company engagement survey stood at 58 out of 100, down from 71 two years earlier; Nordlys treated 50 as the line at which an open complaint became a formal process involving the verneombud, the elected employee representative. Team trust, measured across the department from nought to ten, stood at 5.4. A grievance-resolution rating tracking how live complaints were handled stood at 42 out of 100, the assessor's complaint unresolved. In the survey's free text, the most cited negative was that promotion decisions were not transparent.

HR's exit-risk modelling named five people. Three were high performers under 30, Henrik among them, whose modelled probability of leaving rose if the promotion went on years served. Two were senior technical experts whose modelled response to a decision made on the automation result was disengagement or early retirement. HR costed each replacement at 600 000 to 900 000 kr in recruitment, onboarding and lost productivity — and for the senior two, the judgement did not come back at any price. On 10 March all five were still in the building.

The screenshot changed the shape of the problem. Nordlys's communications advisor rated the department's reputational exposure high: with the award result eight weeks out and an internal message already travelling beyond the team channel, what had been a personnel matter on Monday was, by Tuesday, something the company could be asked about.

If Lindeland changed nothing — announced a name inside a week on her own judgement, as her predecessor appeared to have done — the arithmetic was not mysterious. The score had fallen thirteen points in two years on that pattern; the complaint went formal at 50; the losing cohort would read the result as confirmation of what it already suspected; and HR's five would decide what to do about it eight weeks before an award panel published its verdict on the company's culture.

## What Lindeland Controlled

The two weeks broke into four pieces, and she held every lever in each.

**The diagnosis.** What had actually gone wrong: the wrong people promoted, a process nobody could see, or results announced without a decent explanation — and which was doing most damage now. The open complaint taken as the evidence that told her, parked until after the announcement, or pushed back on. And anywhere from none to all twenty-eight of her people consulted before she designed anything.

**The process.** Criteria and weights declared in advance — leadership potential, technical mastery, strategic contribution, years of service, in whatever proportion — or none set, and judgement applied when she saw the candidates. Voice for both candidates and the team before a panel, informal soundings from people she trusted, or the decision taken alone and quietly. A rationale bias-checked by HR and the verneombud on an auditable, correctable record; a light sign-off at the end; or nothing written down. And how much of the fortnight went into process rather than into reaching the announcement.

**The allocation.** Astrid, Henrik, or a third answer — a parallel technical-expert track alongside a staged leadership move, explained in full. Whether the choice followed the published weights, whether she overrode them openly and left them standing, or whether she quietly restated them so the choice fitted. Whether she named out loud whose idea of fairness the weighting privileged, or left it unsaid. And a budget for the cohort she disappointed: nothing, 150 000 kr for a development conversation and a gesture to the runner-up, or 350 000 kr for a real growth path and stay-conversations across the group.

**The telling.** An announcement that opened with the process and closed with the name, one that opened with the name, or a single line on the intranet. For the candidate who lost: the specific reason tied to the published criteria with concrete next steps, warm reassurance that she was valued and other opportunities would come, or the decision without the conversation. The screenshot and the award clock addressed openly, left alone as internal matters, or met with a request to delete the message. And a sixty-day plan to rebuild the fairness score, at whatever firmness she would be held to.

Four numbers would tell her whether it worked: the fairness score, team trust, the grievance-resolution rating, and how many of the five stayed.

## Closing

At 08:40 Lindeland wrote the two names on her office whiteboard and drew a line between them. Below she added a third heading — *hvordan*, how — and left the space under it empty. The role opened on 1 May. The award panel reported in eight weeks. She had two weeks, twenty-eight people and one promotion.
