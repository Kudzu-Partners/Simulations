# Helvar Werkzeuge: Built for Fifteen, Sold Every Five

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1782 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1782 — *Pattern the Future* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 23 November 2026, Sabine Herrmann arrived at Helvar Werkzeuge GmbH's Stuttgart headquarters to find two documents attached to the same note from the owners: a legal summary of new European rules requiring manufacturers to prove their products could be repaired rather than quietly replaced, and a client list showing two trade accounts, worth EUR 14 million between them, that had just moved their tool purchases to a venture-backed subscription rival called ToolFlow. Both documents made the same point from two directions — the sell-a-new-tool model Helvar had run on for 55 years was running out of road.

Herrmann, Helvar's newly appointed Sustainability Lead, had eighteen months to answer it: redesign the business model itself to produce measurable ecological and social value without letting EBIT margin, which funded everything else, fall below 10 percent. She had EUR 24 million for the redesign and four stages to plan, build and defend it — to the owners first, then to an outside investor. She had not chosen a single new approach yet, and had not even finished naming what was wrong with the old one.

## The Protagonist

Sabine Herrmann, 45, had spent nine years running Helvar's product-durability testing lab before the owners created the Sustainability Lead role for her that year. Her career had been built on proving that Helvar's drills, saws and hedge trimmers survived treatment that competitors' tools did not; she had never had to explain why that same durability was a problem the sales model had to keep outrunning. The role reported directly to the owners and had no precedent inside the company. It also had no obvious internal ally: the commercial organization was paid on units shipped, and the factory was tooled for volume, not for taking tools apart and building them again.

## The Decision

The mandate ran in four stages, against one fixed budget and one non-negotiable floor.

Before choosing any new approach, Herrmann's team had to diagnose the old one: which of four practices destroyed the most ecological and social value — the five-to-seven-year replacement habit, packaging and shipping on the direct channel, the Stuttgart factory's energy use, or the marketing spend that pushed new-unit sales; and which of four elements the regulation and the rival had rendered obsolete — selling on the promise of a future replacement, the two-year warranty terms that repair law outpaced, the retailer relationships built for one-time sales, or the premium, over-engineered brand promise ToolFlow undercut. Herrmann also had to set a number from zero to a hundred — a working draft read sixty — for how candidly her team would tell the owners that the business itself, not one feature of it, needed to change.

With the diagnosis filed, the team had to shortlist three approaches to rebuild the model around, from six then circulating inside the project (Exhibit 2), and then characterize the shortlist itself: leaning mostly toward keeping products working longer, leaning mostly toward opening tools to customers who lacked access before, striking a balance across keeping products working, opening access and spreading benefit more widely, or not sorted by that logic because the three had been chosen for how well they'd resonate with buyers.

The next stage carried the most consequence: how the redesigned business would make money — outright sale with a repair leaflet added, outright sale alongside a refurbished-resale line, leasing tool-use instead of the tool, metered pay-per-use billed by hours of operation, or a hybrid of sale, leasing and resale — and how to split the EUR 24 million across product redesign, reverse logistics to collect and sort returns, refurbishment and repair capacity, and a digital platform for subscriptions and access. A working draft already put EUR 6 million against redesign, EUR 5 million against logistics, EUR 6 million against refurbishment and EUR 5 million against the platform: EUR 22 million of the 24, two still uncommitted.

In the final stage Herrmann would present the design to the owners and to a sustainability-focused investor, phasing it — protecting the margin floor early and letting impact build as refurbished-unit revenue grew, switching to the new model at once and accepting a temporary margin dip, or moving slowly to stay near the current 12 percent. To an owner worried about margin erosion she could offer a phased bridge showing EBIT holding at or above the floor, ask him to trust the sustainability case on faith, or retreat toward selling as before with a stated environmental claim to protect the current margin. To an investor doubting the impact was real she could offer quantified avoided-waste and repair-jobs figures with a plan to keep measuring both, a narrative account with no numbers, or waste and carbon figures alone. Last, she had to set a final number, zero to a hundred — seventy, in the draft — for how firmly she would argue that a business could not be sustainable unless it was also economically viable.

## The Company and its Setting

Helvar Werkzeuge GmbH, founded in Stuttgart 55 years earlier, made professional power tools and garden equipment for tradespeople and serious hobbyists across German-speaking Europe. It employed 1,300 people and had booked EUR 280 million in revenue the previous year at a 12 percent EBIT margin, selling mostly through retailers alongside a smaller, growing direct channel. Helvar over-engineered its products on purpose: a drill or hedge trimmer carrying its badge was built to run for fifteen years or more. The commercial model underneath that engineering had never had anything to do with the engineering itself — Helvar made money when a customer bought a new tool, and the company's own planning assumed that happened every five to seven years, long before any given tool actually gave out.

## The Situation

Two developments had forced the owners' hand in the same week: new European rules on product design and on the right to repair what customers already owned were about to require Helvar to prove its tools could be fixed and kept running, rather than retired for a new sale, inside an eighteen-month window, and ToolFlow, the venture-backed rival, had begun renting professional-grade tools by subscription instead of selling them, persuading EUR 14 million of trade accounts to stop buying Helvar tools outright. The owners' response, delivered on 23 November 2026, was a single mandate: redesign the model itself within eighteen months, spending up to EUR 24 million, in a way that produced measurable ecological and social value without letting EBIT margin drop below 10 percent — two points under where it stood that morning (Exhibit 1). On the scorecard the owners set up to track the redesign, Helvar's ecological value stood at 12 out of 100, its social value at 10 out of 100, and the coherence of its still-nonexistent new approach at zero. If Herrmann changed nothing, none of those numbers would move on their own: the regulation would take effect on schedule regardless, ToolFlow would go on renting tools to whichever customers Helvar's model no longer served, and in eighteen months the owners would be asking why a scorecard reading 12, 10 and 0 still read the same.

## What Herrmann Controlled

Across the four stages, Herrmann's team worked through one diagnosis, one shortlist, one redesigned business model and one pitch, against the same EUR 24 million and the same 10 percent floor throughout. She was measured on four things: how much ecological value the design created, counted as avoided waste, extended product life and closed material loops; how much social value it created, counted as repair jobs and access for smaller trades; whether EBIT margin held at or above the 10 percent floor from a starting point of 12 percent; and how coherently the approaches she chose fit together rather than working against one another.

## Closing

On the morning of 23 November, Herrmann opened a blank file to begin the diagnosis the owners had asked for. Eighteen months and twenty-four million euros sat at the top of the page. Nothing about the shortlist, the revenue model or the board pitch could be decided until she answered the question in front of her first: where, exactly, was Helvar's own model already losing the value it claimed to protect?

**Exhibit 2. Six candidate approaches considered for the shortlist stage**

| Candidate | What it would mean in practice |
|---|---|
| Leasing / pay-for-use | Customers would pay to use a tool for a period; Helvar would keep ownership of it. |
| Take-back management | Helvar would set up a system to recover tools from customers once they were finished with them. |
| Remanufacturing / refurbishment | Helvar would rebuild returned tools to as-new condition and sell them again. |
| Repair and maintenance network | Helvar would expand its capacity to repair tools already in customers' hands. |
| Modular, long-life design | Helvar would redesign tools so more of each one could be replaced or upgraded in place instead of the whole tool being discarded. |
| Unit sales alongside any new approach | Helvar would keep selling new tools at its established pace, adding a new approach on top of that rather than in place of it. |
