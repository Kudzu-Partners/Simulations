# Lutèce Mobility: The Silicon-Carbide Bet

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-1787 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1787 — *Decide Under Risk* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 May 2026, Élodie Vasseur, Chief Executive Officer of Lutèce Mobility SA, sat down with her head of research and heard that the company's silicon-carbide power-module programme — eighteen months old and €22 million into its budget — had hit a technical wall it might not clear in time. That same week, the German rail manufacturer that bought 31% of Lutèce's output issued a request for proposals for its next-generation inverter, with a binding supplier decision due on 30 June. Six weeks remained. Vasseur chaired the investment committee that controlled the firm's €90 million discretionary capital envelope for the year, and whatever the committee chose to do with it, she would have to account for the choice to the board chair once the deadline had passed.

Three ways of spending that money were already on the table when the committee sat down, each trading capital, time and uncertainty differently, and the engineer who had spent eighteen months building the in-house option made no secret of which one he wanted the room to choose.

## The Protagonist

Vasseur, 51, had joined Lutèce Mobility in 2009 as a plant controller at the Wrocław facility, moved into group finance, and became Chief Financial Officer in 2016. The board named her Chief Executive Officer in 2021 when the company's founder retired, with an explicit mandate: lift the operating margin from single digits toward 12% within three years, a target the board had tied to a planned secondary listing. She had built her career on capital discipline — questioning budgets rather than inheriting them — and the silicon-carbide programme was the largest bet on the books that she had not personally proposed. She trusted her head of research's technical judgment. She was less sure the room could separate that judgment from eighteen months of effort already spent.

## The Decision

Vasseur had to decide, on behalf of the committee, how to commit the year's capital envelope among mutually exclusive paths before the OEM's deadline, and then keep deciding as new information arrived. Before ranking any option, the committee first had to agree on a reference point: the result below which it would call the year a failure, and where to anchor that line — to the board's own margin mandate, to simple break-even on the capital at risk, to whatever the strongest-looking path delivered on average, or to a standard of avoiding any negative outcome at all. Only once that line was drawn could the committee ask which of the three paths actually looked best on average.

Averages were not the whole exercise. The committee also had to identify which of the three paths would leave the least anticipated regret if it turned out wrong, and which single path carried the largest regret of all if things went badly, then weigh that picture — as a matter of degree, not a flat either/or choice — against the path that simply maximised the average outcome. It also had to identify which path, in its worst case, would push the company's leverage past the lender's 3.0x net-debt-to-EBITDA limit.

## The Company and its Setting

Lutèce Mobility SA, founded in Lyon in 1991, designed and manufactured power-electronics modules — inverters and on-board chargers — for European electric-vehicle and rail customers, employing 2,300 people across plants in Lyon and Wrocław. The company generated €620 million in annual revenue at a 9% EBIT margin (€55.8 million), carried €140 million of net debt against €380 million of equity, and depended on a single German rail manufacturer for 31% of that revenue (see Exhibit 1). That customer had grown uneasy about Lutèce's pace on silicon-carbide technology, the component generation expected to define the next decade of power electronics, and had put its business out to tender.

## The Situation

The €90 million envelope could fund a continuation of the in-house silicon-carbide programme, adding €60 million to the €22 million already spent; Lutèce's engineers judged the technology's chances of qualifying in time as better than even, with a smaller chance of a partial delay that still produced a worthwhile return, and a smaller chance still of an outright failure that would force a full write-off. Alternatively, the committee could license an already-qualified design from an American specialist for €45 million plus ongoing royalties, an option judged highly likely to qualify on schedule but carrying a modest chance the OEM would decline a module Lutèce did not own outright. A third option spent nothing new and continued selling the existing silicon line, guaranteeing a small gain for the year while, Lutèce expected, prompting the OEM to begin sourcing the part elsewhere within a few years. A fourth combined licensing with continued in-house work as a hedge, at €75 million combined against the €90 million envelope — leaving little of the year's capital for anything else; the committee judged the combined path better than even to land a qualified module through one leg or the other, with a smaller chance that the in-house leg alone fell short while the licensed fallback still carried the OEM award, and a smaller chance still that both legs disappointed at once, consuming most of the envelope for a result no better than licensing alone would have delivered. Left unchanged, the outcome was not in doubt: the deadline would pass without a qualified module, the rail account would dual-source, and the board's 12% margin target would move out of reach.

Two complications sat inside the numbers. First, the €22 million already invested in-house was, strictly, irrelevant to the forward decision, but the head of research had already told colleagues that abandoning the programme now would mean "throwing away eighteen months of work." Second, the in-house path's worst case would by itself push net-debt-to-EBITDA past the 3.0x covenant limit the company's lenders enforced; the other paths did not carry that exposure alone, though combining them could reintroduce it (see Exhibit 2). The odds Lutèce's engineers had assigned were early estimates from a testing programme still running, and could shift as further results arrived before the deadline.

## What Vasseur Controlled

Once the deadline went live, the committee had to commit fully to one path or take the combined hedge, and state plainly whether that commitment rested on a threshold fixed in advance, on whatever the newest information happened to be, or on a decision to disregard new information and hold the original plan. It also had to decide whether to treat the OEM's 30 June date as fixed or as something that could be pushed back if asked. Weeks later, whatever path had been chosen would produce a partial result, and the committee would face a further choice: commit further capital, stop, or continue exactly as designed — and state explicitly whether the capital already spent on the in-house effort changed that forward calculation. Accounting for the year's decisions to the board chair, it could rest its case on the documented chain of reasoning built round by round, on the fact that things had turned out well, or on the research head's continued confidence in his team, and rate, as a percentage, how confidently it could tell its own process apart from the role chance had played.

Four measures tracked the committee through the year: a decision-quality score, starting at a neutral fifty on a zero-to-hundred scale, meant to capture the consistency of its process rather than its luck; an expected-value figure, the probability-weighted return implied by whichever path it favoured; an anticipated-regret figure, the gap between that path's outcome and the best result achievable in the same circumstances; and a covenant-headroom figure, standing at exactly the lender's 3.0x limit before any capital was committed and moving with whatever the committee chose.

## Closing

At nine that morning, Vasseur asked her assistant to print the paths onto a single page, with a blank line at the top for the number the committee would name before it read anything else. The clock over the boardroom door read six weeks to 30 June. Her head of research was already in his seat.
