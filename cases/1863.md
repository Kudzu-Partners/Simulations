# Engage to Innovate: Rescuing Brevia Sense

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1863 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1863 — *Engage to Innovate — Rescuing Brevia Sense* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Tuesday, 12 May 2026, Elena Ricci sat through the quarterly steering review for Brevia Sense and heard the sponsor call the project's status a flatline. Fourteen months after launch, with EUR 4.1 million of a EUR 6.4 million budget already spent, the wireless, app-connected smart caliper that was supposed to be Brevia Strumenti's first connected product was tracking to miss its own launch fair by five weeks. The board had eighteen weeks left before that fair opened, and it had already told Ricci what missing it would cost.

## The Protagonist

Ricci, 38, had led the eighteen-person Brevia Sense squad — drawn from mechanical engineering, firmware, app development and quality — since the program's launch. She had built her career inside Brevia Strumenti's mechanical-precision businesses, where a locked specification and a fixed schedule had always been enough to ship on time. Brevia Sense was the company's first attempt at a connected product after four decades of competing purely on mechanical precision, and it was the first program Ricci had run where the plan itself, not just the execution of it, seemed to be part of the problem.

## The Decision

Ricci's recovery would unfold as four linked choices. First, she had to name what was actually wrong: a collapse in the squad's engagement pulse, with people who felt unheard having quietly stopped delivering; a schedule that had slipped and needed more hours and a rebuilt plan; a frozen specification — forty-one requirements locked fourteen months earlier, with no mechanism since to absorb anything new; or none of those alone, with delivery, scope and morale as three separate problems to fix side by side. Alongside that diagnosis she had to name the most underused lever — putting more people and hours directly onto the work already behind, making visible idea-adoption and shared autonomy the lever on the theory it was the cheapest one still unused, or loosening the plan so the customer's new request had somewhere to land — and set, on a percentage scale, how rigorously she would separate the three threads before acting.

Second, she had to set how much of the frozen scope stayed frozen, and choose her recovery moves. On flexibility: keep a credible core frozen and let the rest reset every sprint; freeze all forty-one requirements until after the pilot; convert part of the scope into a backlog reset monthly; or open the whole backlog to renegotiation every sprint. Independently, she could combine any of: shipping two squad ideas every sprint, at no direct cost but some cost in scope; a co-located war-room week for the whole squad, at EUR 60,000; bringing the customer into the sprint reviews, at no direct cost but with gaps exposed earlier; backfilling the two departing firmware engineers, at EUR 45,000 plus ramp-up time; or a recognition-and-autonomy redesign giving each sub-team ownership of its own backlog, at no direct cost. Whatever she picked, she had to name the one number — the pulse, or the count of shipped ideas — she expected it to move, or admit she was not tying it to either.

Third, across the eighteen weeks ahead she had to split the squad's capacity across four workstreams summing to the full hundred percent: the firmware-app integration that was the critical path and still lacked a working demo; the edge-filtering the customer had never specified and now wanted; technical-debt cleanup, which paid off later but not at the fair; and hardening the demo itself. Partway through, a key firmware engineer would be poached, costing roughly fifteen percent of whatever capacity sat on integration, forcing a re-split. She also had to define the pilot: integration and the edge filter only, faking or deferring the rest; integration, the edge filter and a solid demo, deferring the deeper technical debt; or every item cleared, technical debt included, fully hardened before the fair.

Fourth, she had to decide how to present the recovery and what to keep. She could present clean dashboards and leave the engagement number and deferred backlog out of the room; report all four numbers against where they had started and defend every trade-off in the open; or report delivered value and slippage while keeping the engagement and idea-adoption story inside the squad. Her pilot recommendation could be to go ahead with what she could defend, go ahead while flagging the risks still carried, or pull the pilot and ask the board for more time. And she had to decide what survived: keeping the ritual, the flexibility cadence and shared sub-team ownership together as one permanent model; letting the interventions lapse and reverting to the old model; or keeping only the idea-shipping ritual and letting the rest lapse — stating, on a 0–100 scale, how firmly she held that engagement and flexibility, not schedule pressure alone, had driven the outcome.

## The Company and its Setting

Brevia Strumenti S.p.A. was a EUR 96 million precision-instruments maker based in Lecco, in the Lombardy region of northern Italy, that had competed on mechanical precision for four decades and had never shipped a connected product before Brevia Sense. The smart caliper streamed measurements wirelessly through a companion app, and the board had publicly committed a working pilot to a German Tier-1 automotive customer worth EUR 11 million of annual sales, timed to the EMO Hannover trade fair.

## The Situation

The numbers on the table at the steering review were not in dispute (see **Exhibit 1**). Of the last six sprint milestones, four had been missed, and the integrated firmware-and-app demo was still not built. At the current pace the pilot would land five weeks after the fair closed. The squad's internal engagement pulse, tracked on a 0–100 scale, had fallen from a peak of 78 to 41, and two of the program's four firmware engineers had asked to transfer off it. The squad had surfaced nine improvement ideas over the past quarter — a simpler pairing flow, a cheaper sensor, a modular firmware architecture among them — and none had been adopted; all had been pushed to "after launch." The frozen forty-one-requirement specification had no slot for the edge-filtering the German customer was now asking for. EUR 2.3 million of the original budget remained, against a burn of EUR 290,000 a month. If none of that changed, the arithmetic was simple: the pilot would still land after the fair closed, the engagement number would have no reason to recover, and the customer had already signalled what a missed fair would mean for the relationship.

## What Ricci Controlled

Ricci was going to be judged on four things across the coming rounds of decisions: the share of the nine ideas that actually shipped, the engagement pulse itself, how many weeks late the pilot landed against the fair, and the euro-value of whatever features actually worked and could be shown at the stand (see **Exhibit 1**). Every choice she made — the diagnosis, the flexibility setting, the recovery menu, the capacity split, the pilot scope, and how she reported it — fed into those same four numbers, and none of the four moved on its own.

## Closing

At 8:15 the following morning Ricci opened a blank page and wrote the four numbers across the top: idea adoption, engagement, slippage, delivered value. Eighteen weeks stood between her and the fair. Two-point-three million euros of runway stood between her and running out of road before she got there. The steering committee wanted her recovery plan by the end of the week.
