# Veridian Systems: The Data-Layer Race

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1815 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2026 — *Capturing the Value* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 9 March 2026, Élodie Vasseur read the product sheet a German competitor had handed out overnight at the Hannover trade fair and recognised almost every line of it. The rival's new offering, PlantSense AI, bundled a low-cost sensor with a cloud analytics subscription priced at 90 euros per machine per month, with no hardware margin. It was close to the platform her own research centre had spent fourteen months designing and never brought to market.

The board would meet in 120 days, wanting three things from her: a strategy for capturing more of the value its sensors already created, a committed date to bring something to market, and a one-sentence answer to a question its chair had begun asking by phone — what stopped PlantSense from taking the business away.

## The Protagonist

Élodie Vasseur, 44, had been Chief Strategy Officer of Veridian Systems SAS since 2023, promoted from the corporate-development role she had held since joining eight years earlier from a Lyon strategy consultancy. Before consulting she had spent two years on an automotive supplier's factory floor — the one line on her résumé, she said, the engineers in Veridian's 110-person research centre fully trusted. She had sponsored the platform's original business case eighteen months earlier; the board deferred it twice while a plant expansion absorbed the capital budget. The prototype sitting unlaunched down the hall was, in every way that counted inside the building, hers.

## The Decision

Vasseur's task split into four linked decisions, and each one narrowed the next. First, she had to locate, somewhere along the chain from sensor to data to analytics to guaranteed uptime, the point where Veridian kept the value it created, and separately, the point most exposed to a fast copy. Second, once she had named where the innovation effort would concentrate, she had to decide how Veridian would acquire the analytics capability, inside a fixed two-year spending envelope. Third, she had to choose the pricing model the company would operate under and the mechanism meant to stop a rival from reproducing the result. Fourth, she had to walk into the boardroom with a launch date, a return figure, and a defensible answer to the chair's question, and defend all three under questioning.

## The Company and its Setting

Veridian Systems SAS, headquartered in Lyon, had been founded in 2009 by two engineers who left a large French automation group to build precision sensors for factory machinery. Sixteen years later it employed 940 people, 110 in the research centre behind its reputation for reliability, serving automotive, food-processing and pharmaceutical plants across Europe. Revenue in 2025 had reached 280 million euros at a 12% operating margin, or roughly 33.6 million euros. About 70% of that revenue still came from the way Veridian had always sold: a customer bought a sensor once, installed it, and called only when it broke. The sensors themselves streamed continuous vibration, temperature and wear data from the roughly 25,000 machines Veridian had installed across the continent — data it had never turned into a product of its own.

## The Situation

At Hannover, the rival had claimed a nine-to-twelve-month head start and was outspending Veridian on marketing roughly six to one (see Exhibit 1). Two customers running pilot installations paused their next reorder to evaluate the new subscription. Veridian's finance function forecast the 41% gross margin it earned on hardware falling toward 22% within two years, as connected sensors turned into a price-driven category. Meanwhile, the uncaptured value was substantial: the installed base of 25,000 machines represented about 45 million euros a year in analytics subscription revenue. Veridian held three granted patents, all on the hardware; the analytics models beneath the prototype carried none, and Vasseur's team believed a competitor could reproduce them within six to nine months. The risk was concrete: a plan that took eighteen months and shipped nothing in between left the rival free to sign four of Veridian's ten largest accounts — 28% of revenue — to exclusive, multi-year agreements before Veridian's platform went live. On the board's scorecard, value-capture stood at 18 against a target of 70, defensibility at 12, innovation return at 20, and time-to-market unset.

## What Vasseur Controlled

On where in that chain Veridian actually kept the value it created, four views competed: the sensor hardware, which Veridian had spent sixteen years learning to build best in Europe; the connectivity layer, the gateways and pipes that carried a machine's data to the cloud and back; the data and analytics layer, recurring revenue on an installed base that was hard to copy at scale; or value that was spread evenly across the whole chain, with no single link more decisive than the rest.

A parallel question asked which part a copyist could most easily reach: the hardware, on the reasoning that a physical sensor could be reverse-engineered faster than a data-driven platform could be replicated; the analytics itself, unpatented and reproducible, Veridian's own engineers believed, within six to nine months; the brand, since a marketing budget six times Veridian's own could reshape customer opinion faster than any feature could; or the judgment that none of it yet amounted to real exposure, one product launch and not an industry shift. A further figure recorded how sharply the diagnosis separated what Veridian created from what it kept.

Once the effort had a target, three paths existed for acquiring the platform, compared in Exhibit 2: building it inside Veridian's own research centre, buying a small Paris analytics company, or partnering with a cloud provider's machine-learning platform under a white-label deal, each trading speed against control differently. Alongside it sat where the budget would concentrate — a next-generation sensor meant to out-engineer PlantSense's free hardware, the data and analytics platform built on the installed base, or an even split of the budget across both — and a euro figure, up to eight million, for an interim move to hold the two paused pilot customers while the fuller platform was still being built.

The pricing model could be a straightforward subscription priced per machine per month, structured much like PlantSense's own; the existing one-time hardware sale left in place, with a subscription added only where a customer asked for one; or outcome-based, a fee tied to guaranteed uptime so revenue tracked the result the data delivered. The mechanism to keep it from being copied could be switching costs, built by wiring the platform deep into a customer's daily operations and workflows; patents, filed on the underlying algorithms and enforced through litigation against any imitator; brand, competing on Veridian's sixteen-year reputation for reliability and the reach of its marketing; or data network effects, where more connected machines made the models better and harder to leave. Two further figures followed: euros, up to fourteen million, to fund whichever mechanism was chosen, and a percentage for how firmly sales incentives would move off the one-time quota they had operated under for years.

The launch date itself could be a single consolidated launch roughly nine months out, with no interim step along the way; a staged rollout: the interim move live at once, the full platform live within months; or an eighteen-month build with no interim move, delivered only once the whole platform was complete. The return figure could be presented at its full forty-five-million-euro face value, as though first-mover advantage were guaranteed; left as a narrative about platform leadership with no hard figure attached; or discounted for the probability and speed of being copied, arriving at an imitation-adjusted number. The one-sentence answer to the chair could rest on the patents Veridian already held, on the reasoning that imitators would lag well behind; on speed itself, on the reasoning that outrunning the rival needed no additional structural lock-in; on a compounding data moat paired with switching costs on an installed base rivals could not reach; or on the company's reliability reputation, on the reasoning that customers loyal for years would stay that way. Last came a percentage for how firmly she would argue, under questioning, that durable capture was worth more than a fast giveaway.

## Closing

At her desk, Vasseur closed the product sheet and opened a blank slide for the board deck. She had a hundred and twenty days, a forty-million-euro envelope, four decisions that each narrowed the next, and a chair already asking, again, what stopped PlantSense from eating them alive.
