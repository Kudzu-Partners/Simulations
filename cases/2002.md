# Bank Pertiwi Berhad: The League Table

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-2002 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2002 — *Culture vs. Compliance* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 16 March 2026, Zarina Yusof read a four-page internal audit memo twice before she telephoned anyone. It described eighteen branches of Bank Pertiwi Berhad — every one of them in the top band of the bank's published monthly sales league table — where dormant accounts had turned active in clusters, where roughly 2,400 customers appeared to have been signed up for credit-protection insurance they had not asked for, and where same-day product cancellations had tripled over nine months. Against a bank holding RM 92 billion (Malaysian ringgit) in assets, the numbers were small. They were also the shape of the first page of every mis-selling scandal she had ever read.

In two weeks Yusof would sit in front of Bank Pertiwi's board and account for what the bank intended to do. Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM), the central bank that supervised Pertiwi's prudential and conduct obligations, had not been told. She had fourteen days to settle what the anomaly actually was, what to do about it, when and whether to notify the regulator, and what she would say out loud in the boardroom.

## The Protagonist

Zarina Yusof, 49, had been Bank Pertiwi's Chief Governance Officer since July 2023, with compliance, conduct and the whistleblowing line reporting to her. She had joined the bank in 2016 as Head of Compliance after nine years in BNM's supervision department, where she had spent most of her time reading other institutions' remediation plans.

She had also signed the governance clearance on the retail incentive scheme in January 2023. That review had asked whether the targets were achievable and the payout curve affordable. It had recorded no objection.

## The Decision

Yusof had to produce, inside a fortnight, a diagnosis of what had happened, a response, a disclosure position and a board brief — and each of the four had to be consistent with the other three.

The immediate question was whether eighteen branches were eighteen branches. One reading was that a handful of sellers had behaved badly and could be identified and removed. A second was that the approval and monitoring checks had failed and needed tightening. A third was that the bank's own reward design had produced the behaviour — in which case the eighteen were a sample, and the other 192 branches ran the same scheme.

The Head of Retail Banking, Lim Chee Kong, who had built the scheme, argued that a design fault would not stop at eighteen branches. What audit had found, he told Yusof that afternoon, was "a few bad apples in a network of eight thousand six hundred people," and the league table was "just healthy competition." Killing it would cost eight to ten points of fee-income growth next year.

## The Company and its Setting

Bank Pertiwi Berhad, founded in 1982 and headquartered in Kuala Lumpur, was a mid-tier Malaysian commercial bank listed on Bursa Malaysia: RM 92 billion in total assets, 210 branches, 3.4 million customers, 8,600 employees, a return on equity of 11.4% and a cost-to-income ratio of 46% in 2025. Its franchise was retail and SME.

The bank changed shape in January 2023, when it launched an aggressive retail-growth strategy built on steep individual sales incentives: personal cross-sell targets, product-bundling bonuses, and a league table published monthly across the network naming the top and bottom ten per cent of sellers. Fee income climbed, analysts began calling Pertiwi the best-run franchise of its size, and the 2026 plan underwrote 28% growth.

BNM's published expectations of licensed banks included the fair treatment of financial consumers and the prompt self-reporting of conduct issues. Where a supervisor found a pattern independently rather than being told, comparable Malaysian cases had escalated from remediation into an enforcement action, a fine and a mandated independent review; Pertiwi's general counsel put the benchmark near RM 30 million.

## The Situation

The audit findings covered a nine-month window at eighteen branches, not the network (see **Exhibit 1**). Refund and remediation exposure for the estimated 2,400 customers ran to RM 14–22 million, before any reputational damage.

Two further datasets sat in Yusof's file, neither from audit. The quarterly staff survey showed 41% of branch sellers agreeing that "I feel pressure to hit targets even when a product isn't right for the customer," against 19% before the scheme launched, and 8% saying they would report a colleague's mis-selling. Her whistleblowing line had taken three reports in two years.

Yusof tracked the bank's conduct position on four indices, each scored 0 to 100 and reported to the board audit committee (see **Exhibit 2**). The misconduct-risk index, on which lower was safer, stood at 78: target pressure, incentive concentration, cancellation and complaint patterns, and the strength of the checks around a sale. Compliance standing — the bank's own estimate of the credit it held with its regulator — stood at 52. Board confidence, the audit committee's read on management's grip and candour, stood at 50. Culture health, built from the speak-up rate, the pressure score and how far reward followed customer outcomes rather than volume, stood at 34, the lowest of the four. Beside them ran a fee-income growth outlook of +28% and open regulatory exposure of RM 14–22 million.

If nothing moved in the fortnight, the position was plain. The league table would publish again at the end of March across all 210 branches. The 2,400 customers would remain un-refunded and the RM 14–22 million would stay a footnote rather than a provision. BNM would learn of the pattern from its own thematic work, from a customer or from a member of staff. And the board would meet on 30 March with a four-page audit memo and no diagnosis attached to it.

## What Yusof Controlled

She would first sign a written diagnosis — one of the three readings above — and state which evidence she was leading with: the small absolute numbers, the correlation between the flagged branches and the top of the league table, or the cancellation spike. The same document had to record how she read an 8% speak-up rate against 41% reported pressure, and a number for how many of the 192 un-flagged branches carried the same exposure.

She then controlled how the fortnight's effort split between two workstreams and in what order they ran. Rules-tightening meant mandatory consent verification, a second signature on every insurance sale, enhanced transaction monitoring, suspension of implicated staff and a tightened product-approval gate — fast, visible and worked around. Culture work meant redesigning the reward system, rebuilding the speak-up channel, holding managers to account for how their numbers were produced, and changing what senior leaders said in public — slower, and stopping nothing meanwhile.

Four decisions then had to be signed. The incentive scheme could be kept, abolished overnight at the stated cost to fee growth, or rebuilt around balanced customer-outcome measures. BNM could be notified now with early findings and a remediation plan, notified once Pertiwi's own investigation closed, or not notified yet. Consequences could fall on the seven sellers at the top of the league table, on nobody until the investigation finished, or on the managers and scheme designers as well as the frontline. And the provision for affected customers could be nothing, RM 8 million for the clearest cases, or RM 18 million for the full estimated population.

Board day carried the rest. Whatever the fortnight produced — a journalist asking questions, a BNM thematic-review request covering retail sales practices, an email to the board chair from a member of staff describing target pressure across the network — Yusof would meet it by denying and containing, by postponing the meeting, or by briefing the regulator and the chair before anyone else did. She would choose the single sentence her brief opened with, and the measures she would commit the bank to publishing thereafter: sales volume and cross-sell ratios, a count of mis-selling cases caught, or a conduct dashboard carrying the speak-up rate, the target-pressure index, customer-outcome quality and senior accountability.

## Closing

Late on 16 March, Yusof drew two columns on the whiteboard in her office. She headed the left one *stop the bleeding* and the right one *stop it happening again*, and she wrote the eighteen branch codes underneath both. Down the corridor, retail was closing the March league table for publication. The board met in fourteen days. BNM had not been told.
