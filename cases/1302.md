# Aurelian Holdings: Five Mandates, Nine Formats

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1302 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1302 — *Auction Design Lab* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

---

On Monday, April 13, 2026, Clara Mendel came out of the Aurelian Holdings partners' meeting with five sellers to advise and one question to answer for each of them: how should the thing be sold? A foundation was parting with a Picasso study, a telecom regulator was licensing spectrum, a Treasury desk was placing bonds, a city was letting a cleaning contract and a search platform was selling advertising space. Every one of them wanted the most money its sale could raise, and every one had asked Aurelian which auction format would raise it.

Mendel had two weeks. At the partners' meeting on Monday, April 27, she was to present one recommended format per client and defend each in front of people who had run auctions for thirty years. Before then, under a house rule older than her job, she had to sit the firm's bidding lab.

## The Protagonist

Clara Mendel, 39, had run Aurelian's auction advisory desk for eighteen months. She had joined the firm from the pricing team of a commodities exchange, where she had watched the same contract clear at different prices under different trading rules, and she had been hired partly to bring that habit of mind to a desk that had always been run on relationships. She was respected for her preparation and suspected, by some of the older partners, of trusting a spreadsheet over a saleroom. Her bonus was tied to the desk's fee income, and fee income was tied to whether clients came back.

## The Decision

The five mandates arrived at the same time and looked nothing alike (see **Exhibit 1**). Mendel had nine formats to choose from for each (see **Exhibit 2**), and nothing obliged her to use the same one twice.

The desk had already split into three positions. Two analysts wanted one sealed format applied across the board, on the grounds that a single, well-understood process was easier to explain and to defend. A senior partner wanted open bidding wherever the client could tolerate it, because bidders who watched each other learned something and sellers liked to see the room. A third camp, led by the partner who handled public-sector clients, argued that each mandate had its own peculiarity and that any uniform answer was a shortcut. Each camp could point to sales that had gone well under its preferred approach, and each could point to sales that had gone badly under the others'.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurelian Holdings, founded in 2003 and based in New York, was a mid-market alternative-assets house. It ran sales for its own clients and advised other sellers on how to run theirs. Its advisory fees were small relative to the sums involved, which meant that the desk's real asset was its reputation: a seller who believed Aurelian's format had cost it money did not come back, and told others.

The bidding lab was the managing partner's invention. He held that nobody should tell a seller how bidders would behave in a format they had never bid in themselves. The lab ran four small sales internally, one in each of four formats, with Mendel as one of four bidders against three colleagues. Before each sale every bidder was assigned a private worth for the lot, drawn independently somewhere between $0 and $100; each bidder saw only their own. A bidder who won kept the difference between that worth and the price paid, and the desk called the difference surplus. A bidder who lost kept nothing.

## The Situation

At the start of the lab Mendel had won nothing. Her running surplus stood at $0, her count of sales won at zero, and the cumulative revenue her four sales had raised for the notional seller at $0. Her worth for the first lot would be set when the first sale opened, and a fresh worth would be drawn for her and for each colleague before every sale after it.

The four sales differed only in their rules. In the first, an open ascending sale, the price rose from $0 and each bidder dropped out at a moment of their choosing; the last bidder remaining won and paid the price at which the second-to-last had dropped out. In the second, a descending clock, the price started at $100 and fell; the first bidder to call won at the price showing when they called. In the third, a sealed first-price sale, each bidder submitted one number; the highest won and paid that number. In the fourth, a sealed second-price sale, each bidder submitted one number; the highest won and paid the second-highest number submitted.

After each sale the lab's log recorded the format, her worth, her bid, a reference bid, the clearing price, whether she had won and the surplus she had kept. The reference bid was the figure the market-design team's standard calculation assigned to a bidder holding her worth in that format, on the assumption that every bidder reasoned the same way. The log also tracked the revenue each sale raised against the team's benchmark for a sale of that size.

The five mandates were real and the clients were waiting. If the desk sent nothing to the partners' meeting, each client would run whatever format its own staff had drafted, and in three of the five cases that was the format used the last time, chosen before anyone at Aurelian had looked at it.

## What Mendel Controlled

In each of the four lab sales Mendel set one number between $0 and $200. In the open ascending sale it was the highest price at which she would still stay in. On the descending clock it was the price at which she would call. In each sealed sale it was the bid she would put in the envelope.

For the partners' meeting she would choose one of the nine formats for each of the five clients — the art house, the telecom regulator, the Treasury desk, the city procurement office and the search platform — and she would have to submit all five together.

She was measured on three things. The first was how closely her bids tracked the reference bid in each format. The second was how her five recommendations were judged. The third was the total surplus she captured across the four sales, which depended on her bids and on the worths she happened to draw.

## Closing

At 4:15 p.m. Mendel opened the lab's first sale. Her worth for the lot came up on the screen; her three colleagues' worths did not. The price would start at $0 and rise, and the number she typed would decide the moment she stepped out.

**Exhibit 2. The nine formats available for each mandate**

| Format | How it works |
|---|---|
| English ascending (open-cry) | Price rises in public; the last bidder remaining wins and pays the price at which the next-to-last dropped out |
| Dutch descending | Price falls on a clock; the first bidder to call wins and pays the price showing |
| First-price sealed-bid | Each bidder submits one sealed bid; the highest wins and pays its own bid |
| Second-price sealed-bid | Each bidder submits one sealed bid; the highest wins and pays the second-highest bid |
| Uniform-price multi-unit | Bidders bid for quantities at prices; every winner pays one common clearing price |
| Pay-as-bid multi-unit | Bidders bid for quantities at prices; each winner pays the prices it bid |
| Simultaneous multiple-round ascending | Several lots open at once; bidding proceeds in rounds until no new bid is made on any lot |
| Ranked positions, each winner paying the next bid below | Bidders are ranked into positions; each winner pays the bid of the bidder ranked just below it |
| Ranked positions, each winner paying the harm it does others | Bidders are allocated positions; each winner pays the value its presence takes away from the other bidders |

*Source: Aurelian Holdings advisory desk reference sheet (disguised). Any of the nine can be run with a minimum price below which the seller does not sell.*
