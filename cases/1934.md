# Bayfront Professional Education: Sixty Seats

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1934 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1934 — *Program Designer* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

Two documents governed what Adriana Fajardo could build. On Thursday, April 23, 2026, she read them against each other: a draft program brief, and the Dean's memo approving its development. The brief described a fourteen-week non-credit certificate in applied data and artificial intelligence (AI) for managers who did not write code, priced at US$2,400 a seat, with US$600 per learner of variable cost for delivery, coaching and materials. The memo authorised up to US$180,000 to build the thing — curriculum, media, faculty contracting and launch marketing all inside that figure — and made approval conditional on 60 paid enrollments across the first two cohorts. Bayfront measured recovery against the full authorisation, not against whatever a brief happened to commit: at US$1,800 of contribution per learner, US$180,000 took 100 paid seats to recover.

Fajardo had 75 days to settle on a buyer, a format, a budget and a price. At the end of them, Dean Alicia Braddock and the unit's finance reviewer would approve the program for a Summer 2026 launch or shelve it and write the development spend off against Bayfront's margin.

## The Protagonist

Adriana Fajardo, 44, had been Director of Professional Education at Bayfront since 2021. She had joined the unit in 2014 as a curriculum lead, after seven years selling custom corporate programs for a private training firm in Fort Lauderdale, and had taken eleven programs through the Dean's approval gate. Nine had cleared break-even within their first two cohorts. Her budget standing in the next development cycle moved with that record, and so did the size of the team she could keep on contract.

## The Decision

Fajardo had to design the offering end to end and defend it. She would work through it in four passes: who the program was for, how it would be delivered and what building it would cost, what the learner would pay, and what she would say when the finance reviewer pushed on the arithmetic.

## The Company and its Setting

Bayfront Professional Education, established in 2004, was the self-funded continuing- and professional-education unit of a large public university in Miami, Florida. It sat outside the degree-granting colleges and sold short, non-credit certificates, bootcamps and custom corporate programs to working adults and employers across South Florida and, online, nationally. It enrolled roughly 6,000 learners a year across about 40 active programs, employed 22 staff, and produced approximately US$14 million in net revenue at roughly a 30% operating margin.

The unit carried no tenured faculty. Subject-matter experts and instructional designers were contracted program by program. Every new offering was approved against a development-cost cap and a break-even enrollment, and killed if it could not clear them. About 40% of new revenue each year came from learners who returned for a second certificate and from employers who bought a second cohort — the repeat business that paid for the unit's fixed costs and gave Fajardo room to develop anything at all.

The demand signal for this program had arrived from two directions in the same quarter. A regional employer coalition had asked for practical AI training for supervisors and middle managers who were being handed AI tools without any preparation. The unit's own market scan had found the same appetite among individual buyers. Neither source told Fajardo which of those buyers she should build for.

## The Situation

Three groups of buyers wanted something in this space, and they wanted different things (see **Exhibit 1**). Mid-managers were the largest pool and the most price-sensitive, paying US$1,800 to US$2,400 out of pocket or with light employer support; they were time-poor and the unit's records showed them at the highest risk of enrolling and not finishing. Corporate cohorts paid US$3,500 to US$4,500 a seat and bought twenty to thirty seats at a time, but they expected customisation and outcome reporting, and Fajardo's partnerships lead put the realistic corporate demand for a Summer 2026 launch at about 40 seats across both cohorts. Career-changers and upskillers paid US$2,800 to US$3,200 for a credential attached to a clear job result; they were fewer than the mid-manager pool, expected high-touch support, and finished what they started.

The delivery formats differed as sharply (see **Exhibit 2**). A self-paced online build cost roughly US$90,000 and scaled without limit, and about 45% of learners who started one finished it — the unit's lowest learner Net Promoter Score (NPS). A live online or hybrid cohort cost roughly US$150,000, completed at about 70%, and scored strongly on NPS. An in-person intensive cost roughly US$175,000 plus a venue and instructor charge for each cohort, capped the cohort size, and completed at about 85% with the highest NPS the unit recorded.

The draft brief committed US$135,000 across four lines — US$40,000 for curriculum and instructional design, US$40,000 for media production and platform, US$30,000 for faculty contracting and assessment, and a US$25,000 launch-marketing reserve — and left the format open. Its US$2,400 price had been set by marking up the estimated build cost by a margin the unit used on custom work.

Fajardo would be judged on four running measures, and she started low on all of them. **Design fit**, scored 0 to 100 and standing at 20, was the unit's internal read on whether the segment, the format and the price described one coherent product rather than three separate decisions. **Projected enrollments**, at zero, was the paid-seat forecast against the 60 the Dean required. **Margin health**, at 25, measured how comfortably the contribution the program earned recovered the development cost. The **renewal engine**, at 20, tracked completion and learner NPS — the two leading indicators of whether learners came back and employers bought again. A separate line tracked development cost committed against the US$180,000 cap; it stood at zero.

If Fajardo carried the brief to the gate unchanged, the finance reviewer would open it and find a program that needed 100 paid seats to recover the authorised US$180,000, against an approval condition of 60, aimed at the buyers least able to pay for it, priced off the build rather than off anything a learner wanted.

## What Fajardo Controlled

She chose the program's primary learner segment: career-changers, corporate cohorts, mid-managers, or the whole market at once without naming a primary buyer. She chose whether to anchor the eventual price to what her chosen segment would pay for the outcome, or to her own build cost. And she chose how much of the team's remaining time went into sizing addressable demand — establishing whether the segment she picked could in fact supply 60 paid seats — before committing to it.

She then chose the delivery format and set each of the four budget lines herself, in thousands of dollars, against the US$180,000 ceiling.

She set the price per seat and the variable cost per learner she would plan against, decided whether to open a separate employer-paid cohort tier alongside the public price or to run a single public price, and set how hard the launch-marketing reserve would be pushed into acquisition.

At the gate she would answer the Dean's question — why not simply build it self-paced and keep the difference — either on the repeat-enrollment and employer-renewal revenue that completion produced, on the higher margin a cheaper build left per seat, or on the university's standing in premium formats. She would name what she would do if the first cohort opened at 22 seats against the 60: open a lower-priced tier and a corporate cohort to backfill and push full break-even out by one cohort, run the cohort under-filled and miss break-even this cycle, or hold the plan as written. She would decide what the one-page scorecard showed the finance reviewer — enrollment against break-even, completion, net revenue and learner NPS together; net revenue alone; or the enrollment count alone. And she would decide how hard to press the argument when it was challenged.

## Closing

By 9:15 a.m. the price line on the draft brief was blank — she had erased it and had nothing to put back. The three figures that would decide the gate, 180, 60 and 75, were the Dean's, not hers to move.
