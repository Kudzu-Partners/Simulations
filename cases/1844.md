# Hélios Énergie: Shaping the Rule

On the morning of 14 April 2026, Élodie Faure found the Commission de Régulation de l'Énergie's new consultation notice in her inbox before she had finished her coffee. The regulator was opening ninety days of public comment on a draft Consumer Energy Protection rule, closing 13 July, aimed at the teaser-rate discounters whose auto-renewal traps had filled consumer-complaint files all winter. Reading past the headline clauses, Faure found her own company caught in the net: a price cap below wholesale hedging cost, a ban on early-exit fees, and new disclosure duties. One clause alone implied Hélios Énergie's operating margin would fall from 6.5% to 3.8% — a swing of roughly €124 million a year — unless the draft changed before it became law.

Faure had to decide, inside the CRE's ninety-day consultation and the trade association's six-week deadline for a single industry position, not whether to respond to the draft — silence was itself a costly choice — but how: which clauses to fight, which to support, whom to enlist as allies, and how loudly to make the case in public, against a fixed €1.6 million budget.

## The Protagonist

Faure, 44, had run Hélios Énergie's four-person public-affairs unit since 2022, after eight years in a Paris utility's regulatory-strategy group. She had joined Hélios for the pitch that built the company: an energy retailer that refused the teaser-pricing games rivals played, priced consistently, and told customers so. That promise, and the reputation it had earned Hélios among 3.1 million customers across Nouvelle-Aquitaine and Occitanie, was the asset she now had to defend — at the same moment it made her employer an unlikely target of a rule aimed at somebody else. She had a fixed budget of €1.6 million and roughly twelve weeks to spend it.

## The Decision

Every choice she made over the consultation window would be measured on four counts: how far she moved the rule, how Hélios looked for having tried, how much lobbying weight she assembled, and what the campaign cost the fair-price name it had spent a decade building.

## The Company and its Setting

Hélios Énergie S.A., headquartered in Bordeaux and founded in 2007 in the wake of France's energy-market liberalisation, was the third-largest non-incumbent gas and power retailer in the country: 3.1 million residential and small-business customers, €4.6 billion in annual revenue, 2,800 employees, and a 6.5% operating margin. It sat between the historic incumbent supplier and a crowd of discount challengers competing largely on headline price. Hélios had chosen a different position — consistent, disclosed pricing, no teaser rates, no auto-renewal traps — and had built an estimated €180 million of brand value on that promise, the customer-acquisition edge it held over rivals with worse reputations. Hélios belonged to the Union Française de l'Énergie, the sector's trade association, which needed a single industry position by 30 May, six weeks before the CRE's consultation closed.

## The Situation

If the draft passed as written, the consequences were specific. The price cap would cut operating margin from 6.5% to an estimated 3.8% — about €124 million of operating profit a year. A ban on early-exit fees would remove roughly €38 million a year of contract revenue and was projected to lift churn from 14% to 21%. New disclosure and reporting obligations would cost about €22 million to build plus €9 million a year to run (see Exhibit 1). What was contested was not the numbers but whether a draft aimed at the discounters whose complaints triggered it needed to catch Hélios at all. If Faure did nothing — filed no submission, built no coalition, said nothing publicly — the draft would likely pass close to its current text and Hélios would absorb the full hit. The CRE had also signalled that an obstructive campaign would push the Ministry to bypass the regulator and legislate the rule directly, with no further room to negotiate.

## What Faure Controlled

One set of choices concerned how Hélios positioned itself against the draft as a whole: back the clauses banning teaser rates and auto-renewal traps while contesting only the price cap; treat the entire draft as one hostile package and fight every clause; or accept the draft in full and say nothing in public, absorbing the cost rather than being seen to lobby. Layered on that stance was a reading of which argument would carry weight: that consumer-protection reasoning governed the field and any ask needed to sit inside it; that competitive-market economics was the right test to apply; or that neither applied and the matter was simply a contest of lobbying power, framed however suited Hélios. On the price cap itself, she could propose indexing it to hedging cost so it caught teaser pricing but not fair pricing, call for the clause to be struck outright, or take no public position on it. She could also set how rigorously her team quantified the euro cost of each contested clause, from a token estimate to an exhaustive study.

A second set of choices covered how to spend the campaign itself: the central argument for the regulator could be a shared-interest argument that a blunt cap would push fair-pricing suppliers like Hélios out and hand the market back to the discounters the rule targeted; a self-interest argument stating plainly that the cap would cost Hélios about €124 million a year; or a technocratic argument built on a dense economic note about price elasticity, with no public-interest framing attached. She also had to divide the fixed €1.6 million budget across four uses that had to add up exactly (see Exhibit 2): the formal submission to the CRE plus private meetings with regulatory and ministry staff; opinion pieces, public messaging and mobilising Hélios' own customers; an independent economic study of the cap's effects; and outside public-affairs counsel — and decide how much to concede publicly, how visibly to back the clauses Hélios genuinely supported, as the price of being heard on the ones it contested.

A third set of choices concerned coalition-building ahead of the association's 30 May deadline: lead the association toward a position that preserved the disclosure relief her small-business book needed; join whatever consensus the group reached; stay quiet and let the association represent Hélios for free; or file Hélios' own position alone, outside the association altogether. On the anti-teaser clauses, where interests genuinely overlapped, she could bring in a respected consumer-advocacy group as an ally, decline any outside ally to keep the message tightly controlled, or align with the discount-price rivals for extra lobbying weight. Separately, the historic incumbent proposed a joint pact that protected large players but dropped the disclosure relief her small-business customers needed — accept it or reject it. Regardless of allies, she could set how firm a distance to keep from any partner whose reputation might rub off on Hélios.

In the campaign's final stretch, she had to balance private and public channels: run both together, work only through quiet bilateral meetings with no public position, or run an aggressive public campaign with no private ask behind it. Her submission needed a headline argument — the public-interest case endorsing the reform and targeting only the cap's design; the margin case leading with the cost to Hélios; or a legal-threat case signalling litigation if the cap stood. When a journalist asked whether this was about protecting consumers or protecting Hélios' profit, she had to decide what to say: own the fair-price record and argue a blunt cap would reward the worst actors in the market; decline to comment on the commercial detail; or attack the rule and the consumer groups behind it directly. Finally, she could set how much restraint to exercise in that final push, weighing an aggressive campaign against the risk to the brand that made Hélios worth defending in the first place.

## Closing

By the second week of April, Faure had the draft rule, the CRE's timeline, and a blank page where her response plan needed to go. The trade association wanted an industry position in six weeks; the consultation itself closed in ninety days. Whatever she decided about the cap, the frame, the allies and the public statement, the board would ask her, afterward, about all four things at once: what she had moved, what standing she had kept, what coalition she had built, and what the fair-price name was worth when the dust settled.
