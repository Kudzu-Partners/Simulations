## Abstract

FINAMIGO, a Latin American microfinance institution with a USD 42M loan portfolio, 38,000 active borrowers (64% women), and operational self-sufficiency (OSS) of 94%, carried a Portfolio-at-Risk-30-day (PAR30) of 7.8% at the start of 2026 — the aftermath of a drought-flood climate cycle in its largest rural region. Its CEO, Carolina Espín, had six rounds to restore portfolio health, preserve mission depth, and manage the governance conversation with Veritas Capital Partners, a prospective impact investor considering a USD 5M equity injection at a 15% ROE hurdle. Three stakeholder audiences — investor, donor, regulator — each carried a different definition of what "healthy" meant. Round 1 asked Espín to set the loan-write-off rate, the collection-effort intensity, and the group-versus-individual product mix. The case opens on April 20, 2026, as Espín prepares the Round 1 memo for the May 5 board meeting.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate a portfolio-restructuring decision in a microfinance institution under a double bottom line (financial and social).
2. Analyze the trade-off between aggressive write-offs that preserve portfolio integrity and gentle restructuring that hides risk inside the book.
3. Diagnose the interaction of an interest-rate ceiling with break-even economics in a post-shock portfolio.
4. Design a governance package for a prospective impact equity investor that preserves mission depth while conceding on returns and controls.
5. Recommend a Round 1 package and articulate the investor-, donor-, and regulator-facing storylines that would keep all three audiences aligned.

On the evening of **Sunday, April 20, 2026**, **Carolina Espín** sat in her home office in Cuenca and read, for the fourth time, the Risk Committee's portfolio note that her Chief Risk Officer had emailed her at 4:18 p.m. Friday. The note carried six numbers: *OSS 94%. PAR30 7.8%. Portfolio USD 42.0M. Active borrowers 38,000. Women share 64%. Outreach depth 0.31.* Below those numbers, the CRO had added a single sentence in bold: *"Veritas Capital term sheet arrives Tuesday."*

---

## The Protagonist

Carolina Espín, 47, had been Chief Executive Officer of FINAMIGO since 2019. An economist by training, with a master's in development economics from the London School of Economics and an early career at an Andean central bank, she had joined FINAMIGO in 2014 as Chief Risk Officer, built its credit-scoring and PAR-monitoring infrastructure, and been appointed CEO when the founder retired. She had led the institution through the 2020–2021 pandemic cycle without a covenant breach and without a mission retreat. Her reputation across the Latin American microfinance network was for refusing to let either side of the double bottom line dominate the narrative.

---

## The Decision

Espín had to set, by 9:00 a.m. on May 5, the Round 1 portfolio-restructuring package: three decisions that the simulation would treat as Round 1 inputs, plus a fourth framing decision that would govern Rounds 2 through 6.

1. **Loan Write-Off Rate.** The share of the PAR90 bucket to be written off against reserves in the first restructuring wave. A rate of 3.0% of portfolio would clean the book to 5.2% PAR30 at the cost of an OSS dip to 91% and a temporary breach of the Veritas Capital leverage covenant — unless the covenant was renegotiated pre-deal. A rate of 1.5% would hold OSS above 93% at the cost of a persistent PAR30 above 7%.
2. **Collection-Effort Intensity.** A scale of 1 through 10 capturing dunning cadence, field-officer visit frequency, and litigation triggers. Intensity 8+ reduced PAR30 by an additional 60–90 basis points but raised the borrower-churn rate and damaged the social-performance indicator measured by the regulator and by IRIS+.
3. **Group-Versus-Individual Product Mix.** The Round 1 target share for group lending (rural, women-skewed, lower loan size, lower cost) versus individual lending (urban, micro-enterprise, higher loan size). A 58% group / 30% urban individual / 12% agricultural mix protected the women-share and the outreach-depth KPIs; a 40% group / 45% urban individual / 15% agricultural mix improved margin and attracted Veritas Capital's attention but dropped the outreach depth.
4. **Framing Decision.** Whether to run Rounds 2–6 as an *investor-first* story (IRR-led, covenants tight, mission deprioritized), a *donor-first* story (impact-led, growth constrained by grant flow), or a *balanced* story (dual-narrative, two KPI sheets, investor and donor conversations held separately but consistently).

Five subsequent rounds would follow Round 1 along a pre-committed agenda: product mix refinement (R2), interest-rate-ceiling decision (R3), Veritas Capital equity-and-license negotiation (R4), macro-shock response (R5), and stakeholder-audience transparency (R6). Round 1 set the PAR trajectory that every subsequent round would inherit.

## Company Background

FINAMIGO had been founded in 2001 as a non-governmental microfinance organization serving the rural-Andean corridor in its home country. It had converted to a regulated non-bank financial institution in 2011, opened a second regional branch network in 2015, and by the end of 2025 operated 84 branches across four countries with 620 employees, USD 42M in gross loan portfolio, 38,000 active borrowers, and a weighted-average loan size of USD 1,105. The portfolio was 64% women, 58% rural, 52% first-time borrowers, and 31% tied to microenterprise supply chains in agriculture and commerce. Products spanned group-solidarity lending (the original product), individual micro-enterprise lending (since 2013), and an agricultural-specific seasonal line (since 2017).

The institution was funded through a mix of deposits in two of its four markets (where a license permitted), wholesale debt from three regional development banks and one DFI, and retained earnings. The liability structure at the start of 2026 carried a weighted-average cost of funds of 8.2%, a debt-to-equity ratio of 4.1x, and two covenants — PAR30 below 9.0% and OSS above 90% — that Espín's team watched daily.

Veritas Capital Partners, an impact-equity fund with USD 680M under management and 2025 portfolio investments in eleven MFIs across Latin America and Africa, had entered the conversation with FINAMIGO in September 2025. Its proposed term sheet, now six weeks from signing, offered USD 5M of equity at a pre-money valuation of USD 36M in exchange for a 15% hurdle ROE, one board seat, a veto right on write-offs exceeding 2.5% of portfolio, and a five-year path to an MFI-bank license that would open deposit-taking across all four markets.

## Industry and Market Context

The Latin American microfinance sector entered 2026 under four simultaneous pressures. First, climate-driven portfolio shocks had increased in frequency in the Andean corridor, with the 2024–2025 drought-flood cycle producing a regional PAR30 peak of 9.1% — the highest since 2003. Second, regulator-imposed interest-rate ceilings had been tightened in three of the four FINAMIGO markets, with the binding ceiling at 28% nominal against a weighted-average break-even of 31% for institutions below USD 50M in portfolio. Third, impact capital had matured: global impact AUM had reached USD 1.6T by end-2025, a fraction of which was flowing into microfinance, and the standard-setters (IRIS+, Cerise SPI-5) had tightened the outreach-depth and women-share indicators that qualified a portfolio as "deep impact." Fourth, three regional MFIs had, in 2023–2025, attempted a mission retreat under investor pressure and had, in all three cases, lost regulatory and donor support within 24 months.

Empirically, MFIs that had, in the 2019–2025 window, written off 2.5–3.5% of portfolio in a first wave following a climate shock had, on the five-year horizon, stabilized OSS above 102% and preserved covenant compliance; those that had written off less than 1.5% had required a second restructuring round on average 14 months later.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The 2024–2025 drought-flood cycle had lifted PAR30 from a pre-shock baseline of 5.2% to a peak of 9.1% in October 2025, before the CRO's emergency restructuring program had brought it back to 7.8% by March 2026. The Veritas Capital conversation had accelerated in February 2026 when the fund's Head of Latin America had attended FINAMIGO's annual client convention and publicly praised the institution's post-shock response. The draft term sheet had been circulated to Espín on April 11.

### What Espín knew

The Risk Committee's internal model had simulated four Round 1 packages against a forward 18-month macro scenario. A conservative package (write-off 1.5%, collection 6, mix 62/28/10) held OSS at 94% but left PAR30 at 7.1% — uncomfortably close to the 9.0% covenant. A balanced package (write-off 2.8%, collection 7, mix 58/30/12) dropped PAR30 to 5.9% and OSS to 92% for one period before recovering to 96% by month 9. An aggressive package (write-off 3.5%, collection 9, mix 50/38/12) dropped PAR30 to 4.8% and OSS to 89% — a covenant breach unless pre-negotiated. A mission-deep package (write-off 2.2%, collection 6, mix 64/24/12) held the social indicators at their highest level at a PAR30 of 6.4%.

### What she did not know

Whether Veritas would accept a two-tier board structure that preserved a donor-majority in Round 4; whether the regulator would, if pushed to a covenant renegotiation, grant the 30 additional basis points of OSS headroom informally while the rate-ceiling conversation was ongoing; and whether the second-largest rural branch — the branch at the center of the drought-flood cycle — would absorb an aggressive write-off without triggering staff departures.

### What others were saying

**Miguel Hernández**, Chief Risk Officer, argued for the **balanced package**: "Write-off 2.8. Collection 7. Renegotiate the Veritas covenant to 8.5 before signing. Clean the book now; the investor can read a clean book. They cannot read a trailing restructuring."

**Paula Beltrán**, Chief Impact Officer, argued for the **mission-deep package**: "Do not drop group below 64%. Veritas will like the returns less in Year 1 and the cohort study more in Year 3. We cannot win the donor audience back if we lose the women share."

**Diego Farías**, Chief Financial Officer, argued for the **conservative package**: "We are eighteen months from an MFI-bank license. Do not breach the OSS covenant, even for one period. The covenant conversation is not a conversation the regulator has with you in April — it is a conversation they remember in November."

---

## Options Under Consideration

Espín had narrowed the space to three concrete Round 1 packages.

- **Option A — Conservative Hold (the Farías plan):** Write-off 1.5%, collection 6, mix 62/28/10, framing *donor-first*. OSS holds at 94%, PAR30 at 7.1%, Veritas signs at a discounted valuation, women share 64%, outreach depth unchanged.

- **Option B — Balanced Cleanup (the Hernández plan):** Write-off 2.8%, collection 7, mix 58/30/12, framing *balanced*. OSS dips to 92% for three months then recovers to 96%. PAR30 5.9%. Veritas signs at term-sheet valuation after covenant renegotiation. Women share 62%.

- **Option C — Mission-Deep (the Beltrán plan):** Write-off 2.2%, collection 6, mix 64/24/12, framing *donor-first*. OSS 93%, PAR30 6.4%, Veritas pulls back to a secondary term sheet at a discount but stays engaged. Women share 66%, outreach depth +0.04.

Espín's own instinct, not yet shared with the board, was that the framing decision mattered more than the write-off rate — that Rounds 2 through 6 could survive any of the three packages but could not survive an inconsistent story to the three audiences. She had drafted, in her notebook, three parallel half-sentences: one for the investor, one for the donor, one for the regulator.

She closed the note. The board convened at 9:00 a.m. Tuesday. The Veritas term sheet arrived the same day. She wrote one sentence at the top of her agenda: *"Three audiences. One book. Different sentences, same grammar."*
