# Hanseatic Naturkost: A Quarter to Fifty Percent

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1904 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1904 — *Leading Change with Emotion* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 5 March 2026, Annika Rehberg read two lines from the same employee pulse survey and could not make them agree. Seventy-one percent of Hanseatic Naturkost's 1,900 employees said they cared about sustainability. Twenty-two percent said they had changed anything at all about the way they worked. Between the two lines sat an internal memo about "efficiency measures" that had leaked onto the Bremen packing floor eleven days earlier, and 48% of production staff who now told the survey that cutting emissions meant automation and job losses.

The board had given her one quarter. By its June review, Rehberg had to show that half the workforce — roughly 950 people — had taken up at least one concrete change in how they worked, and she had EUR 600,000 with which to do it. Between March and June she had to settle what the company would say about the change, to whom, in what tone, how she would answer the workforce when it answered back, and what the money would buy.

## The Protagonist

Annika Rehberg, 41, had been Head of Sustainability at Hanseatic Naturkost since 2023, having joined in 2019 from a Hamburg logistics group where she ran environmental compliance after training as an industrial engineer. She had built Hanseatic's emissions inventory herself and drafted the figure the chief executive read out to the press in 2025. She reported to the chief operating officer, sat on the executive committee without a vote, and had two people on her team. She held no line authority over anyone in production or logistics.

## The Decision

Rehberg had to decide what Hanseatic would tell its people this change was about, how she would respond when anxious people pushed back at her in a room, and how the EUR 600,000 would be divided among the ways of getting 1,900 people to do something differently — and each decision had to be made before she could see how the one before it had landed.

## The Company and its Setting

Hanseatic Naturkost GmbH, founded in Hamburg in 1989, reported EUR 480 million of revenue in 2025 on dairy alternatives, cereals and frozen ready meals sold under two retail brands to supermarkets in Germany, Austria and the Benelux countries. It ran two production sites, at Hamburg and at Bremen, and employed 1,900 people. Its standing with grocery buyers rested on a reputation for clean origin three decades in the building.

In 2025 the chief executive announced at a press event that Hanseatic would cut its operational emissions — those from its own sites and from the energy it bought — by 42% by 2030. The engineering behind the number was largely specified. Its delivery was not: it ran through refrigeration set points, line-changeover routines, waste sorting, commuting and supplier selection — thousands of small choices made daily by people who did not report to Rehberg.

## The Situation

Year one closed at a 3% reduction, against the 6% the company had set as its own first-year milestone (**Exhibit 1**). The board, which had watched the figure go out under the company's name, asked for a credible plan within one quarter.

The memo leaked in late February. It used the phrase "efficiency measures" nine times and did not mention employment once. The same pulse survey found that 34% of office and administrative staff had stopped paying attention to climate messaging years ago, and that two of the three works-council representatives had recorded a sceptical position on the minutes. The works council could not stop the programme, but it could slow any change to how a line was run for as long as it chose to keep negotiating.

Rehberg would be judged in June on four numbers, three of which had never appeared in a Hanseatic board pack (**Exhibit 1**). The behaviour-change rate was the share of employees who had adopted at least one concrete change in how they worked, taken from the same survey: 22%, against a board target of 50%. The emotion-regulation score, an index from 0 to 100 kept by the people-analytics team, measured how far reported anxiety was converting into a sense that people could act on it: 40. The message-effectiveness index, also 0 to 100, measured the fit between the message a group received and the emotion that group reported, averaged across the workforce: 35. The resistance-risk index, 0 to 100, combined works-council pushback with the survey's cynicism items, and a lower number was better: 55.

Nothing in the position was self-correcting. The behaviour-change rate had come in at 21% and 22% in the two preceding cycles, with an information campaign running throughout. Year one's shortfall made the remaining path steeper than the one already missed. And the target was public: announced to journalists, repeated in the annual report, quoted back by two grocery buyers.

## What Rehberg Controlled

She controlled, first, what the plan said the problem was. Three readings were available: that people did not yet know enough and needed better information; that people knew enough and would change only if required to; or that people already agreed and something they felt sat between agreeing and acting. Alongside it she had to name, for each of three groups, the single thing holding them — for production staff, fear of losing their jobs, guilt at their own share of the emissions, or ordinary optimism awaiting a nudge; for office and administrative staff, having tuned out after years of the same messaging, the same fear of job loss as the line, or anger at management; and for the works council, mistrust that could be worked through, or a settled position that could not.

Second, she controlled the frame. The same facts could be put to the workforce four ways: as urgency, that the planet was in crisis and the company had to act now; as responsibility, that Hanseatic's footprint was harming the future its employees' children would live in; as capability, showing how individual actions added up and what each person could change; or as identity, that clean origin was what Hanseatic had always been and this was the company living it. She could run one message across the whole company or give the production sites a different one. She set the volume, from restrained to the loudest the company had ever spoken, and wrote down the risk her chosen frame carried.

Third, she controlled the room. A town hall was scheduled at Hamburg for 19 March, a works-council session at Bremen the week after, and she already knew three of the questions. A line worker would say the programme was how management justified replacing people with machines; she could name the fear before turning to what people could control, or set out the roadmap and the emissions data, or state that automation was not what the programme was about. Someone would ask whether jobs were safe; she could guarantee outright that no one would lose a job, or set out what she could and could not promise, with consultation, reskilling and choice attached, or refer the question to the board, whose call it was. A works-council representative would say they had heard it all for years and nothing they did mattered; she could show what that representative's own team had already changed and how it added up, or set out again what the target required, or send the full emissions dataset for them to read. Across all three she chose how much of each answer went to what people felt before redirecting it.

Fourth, she controlled the money. The EUR 600,000 could be split, in EUR 25,000 steps, across five things, and the whole of it had to be committed (**Exhibit 2**). She also had to state whether the plan was written as a single campaign period or as a standing programme, and whether people who changed would be reinforced by prizes for the leading teams or by public recognition tied to the company's identity.

## Closing

At the end of the day on 5 March, Rehberg wrote three figures on a sheet of paper — 22, 50, 600 — and beneath them the date of the town hall, fourteen days out. The four measures were on the board's June agenda whether or not she had anything to put against them.

**Exhibit 2. The five uses of the EUR 600,000 engagement budget**

| Use | What the money would buy |
|---|---|
| Peer champions network | Named colleagues on every shift at both sites, trained, released from the line for part of each week and given a small local budget, to talk to their own teams |
| Progress dashboard | A live display, on the floor and on the intranet, of what each team and each site had changed and where that stood against the target, updated weekly |
| Visible quick wins | A set of small changes delivered in the first weeks and left visible on site — sorting stations, lighting, canteen and changeover fixes |
| Manager-led conversations and reskilling | Time, materials and a reskilling allowance for line managers to hold a one-to-one conversation with each of their people |
| Gamified team challenge | An inter-team competition over a fixed period, with a leaderboard, weekly standings and prizes for the leading teams |
| **Total to be committed** | **EUR 600,000, in steps of EUR 25,000** |

*Source: Hanseatic Naturkost internal communications plan, March 2026 (disguised). Any of the five may receive nothing; the full amount must be committed.*
