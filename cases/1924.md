# Liffey Connect Group: The Return on People

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1924 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1924 — *Engage to Win* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 12 January 2026, Niamh Devlin read the two documents that had reached Liffey Connect Group's Dublin office over the weekend and found they described the same eighteen months. The first was a letter from Shannon Mutual placing the insurer's account on formal service watch: customer satisfaction on the dedicated team had fallen from 84% to 71% in two quarters, the team was leaving at 41% a year, and the insurer's procurement office had written "workforce stability and engagement" into the conditions of renewal. The second was the group engagement survey, which put Liffey Connect at 58 out of 100 against 74 eighteen months before.

In 150 days Shannon Mutual's supplier board would decide whether to renew a contract worth EUR 32 million a year — 22% of Liffey Connect's revenue and roughly 310 jobs. Devlin, the group's People Director, had that window to commit a one-off EUR 1.8 million people-investment fund, to show the board the money had bought something measurable, and to defend both in front of Liffey Connect's chief financial officer and the insurer's head of supplier performance.

## The Protagonist

Niamh Devlin, 44, had been Group People Director since 2023, after seven years running resourcing and then operations HR. Her first decade had been in retail banking, judged on cost per hire and time to fill. She wrote the paper that produced the EUR 1.8 million herself, in November 2025. The board approved the one-off tranche and attached a condition: a further EUR 600,000 a year of recurring funding would be released only against evidence that the first tranche had worked. Two of the seven board members had voted against the fund.

## The Decision

Devlin had to decide, across four stages inside the 150 days, what had gone wrong, where the EUR 1.8 million would go, how she would establish that any improvement was hers, and what she would say when both were challenged.

Five numbers on the group's people dashboard were reviewed at each stage. The engagement score, on a scale of 0 to 100, stood at 58. Voluntary attrition on the Shannon Mutual team, annualised as a percentage of that team's roles, stood at 41% against an outsourced-services benchmark near 25%. Customer satisfaction on the insurer account, the figure written into the service-level schedule, stood at 71%. A fourth, kept by the commercial director, scored from 0 to 100 the confidence the board and the client placed in Liffey Connect's people case; it stood at 25. The fifth was the share of the EUR 1.8 million committed.

## The Company and its Setting

Liffey Connect Group, founded in Dublin in 2007, provided outsourced contact-centre, field-service and back-office support to utilities, insurers and public bodies in Ireland and the United Kingdom. It employed 2,100 people across Dublin, Cork, Galway and a UK hub in Leeds. FY2025 revenue was EUR 145 million at an 8% operating margin, or EUR 11.6 million. Payroll ran at roughly 62% of total cost, and client contracts were priced on service-level performance: answer rates, resolution times, satisfaction scores.

In 2021 the group had won a regional Employer of the Year award on the strength of an employer-sponsored volunteering programme that put half its workforce into community projects on company time. Participation had since fallen from 48% to 23%. Two further accounts, together 28% of revenue, came up for renewal within twelve months.

## The Situation

Group voluntary attrition ran at 34% a year against a sector benchmark near 25%. Finance costed a frontline leaver at EUR 9,500 fully loaded — recruitment, onboarding, and eight weeks of reduced productivity while a replacement ramped — putting the annual attrition bill at roughly EUR 6.8 million. On the Shannon Mutual team alone, 41% of some 310 roles came to about 127 leavers a year, or EUR 1.2 million (see **Exhibit 1**).

Four colleagues had read the same survey and reached four different diagnoses. Dara Okonkwo, who ran people analytics, pointed out that the four sites sat within five points of one another while teams under bottom-quartile managers scored 44 and those under top-quartile managers scored 73 (see **Exhibit 2**). Fergal Mulcahy, operations director for the insurer account, noted that his team sat on the group's lowest pay band and had absorbed a 14% rise in contact volume without additional headcount. Sinéad Barry, who held the community and responsibility brief, had coded eighteen months of exit interviews and found "the community stuff disappeared" and variants of it in a quarter of them. Rónán Kehoe, the chief financial officer, observed that the score had fallen 16 points across every site and every tenure band, asked what made this anything other than one company-wide problem, and noted that Irish contact-centre wages had risen 9% while half the country's operators reported the same attrition.

Nothing in the run rate corrected itself. Attrition at 34% reproduced the EUR 6.8 million bill each year, and no measure on the dashboard moved before the satisfaction score the client was reading. If the account went, EUR 32 million of revenue and about 310 roles went with it, against an operating profit of EUR 11.6 million, and the two accounts renewing later in the year would be watching.

## What Devlin Controlled

At the first stage Devlin had to name the single driver she would treat as the highest-leverage one — the quality of frontline managers, the lapse of the volunteering programme and the purpose attached to it, pay and workload on the insurer team, or the 16-point company-wide fall treated as one uniform problem. She had to name where the money would be aimed: the insurer team together with the weakest manager cohort, all 2,100 staff on equal terms, the teams already scoring highest, or new hires and the first ninety days. And she had to record, as a percentage, her confidence that disengagement had caused the attrition and satisfaction decline rather than merely moved alongside it.

At the second stage she had to commit exactly EUR 1.80 million, in steps of EUR 50,000, across five lines: coaching for the bottom-quartile managers; a pay and workload correction on the Shannon Mutual team; a relaunch of the volunteering programme; job-redesign and autonomy pilots; and recognition and wellbeing supports.

At the third stage she had to design the proof, in four parts. The measure she would report first as the early signal: a quarterly engagement pulse with volunteering participation and manager-behaviour ratings, or training hours and volunteering days delivered, or attrition and satisfaction watched directly. How she would establish that a change was hers: a phased rollout treating the insurer sites first and holding the others as a comparison against a recorded baseline, a recorded baseline and a later re-measurement without a comparison group, or a single group-wide rollout and the headline movement. The form of the return she would put to Kehoe: attrition points avoided at EUR 9,500 a leaver plus the EUR 32 million held, the engagement score's recovery as the return itself, or the training and volunteering delivered. And a number, in months between 1 and 24, for how long the board should wait before the business numbers moved.

At the fourth stage she had three minutes with Kehoe and then three with the insurer. The metric she would stake her credibility on: the insurer team's attrition brought to the benchmark, the engagement score back toward 74, or the training and volunteering delivered. How she would set the responsibility story beside the commercial one: both together, the retention savings alone, or the purpose alone. Her answer when Kehoe challenged the volunteering line: that it carried a participation target and a stated link to retention, that it was worth it for the employer brand and the award, or that it should be withdrawn and the money moved to pay. And, as a percentage, how firmly she held whatever position she took.

## Closing

At 8:20 Devlin put the survey and the insurer's letter side by side and wrote five figures at the top of a page: 58, 41, 71, 25, and EUR 1.8 million. Shannon Mutual's supplier board would sit in 150 days. Kehoe wanted the return in euros before he released another EUR 600,000, and the insurer wanted evidence of workforce stability before it renewed EUR 32 million. Devlin had four colleagues, four diagnoses, and one fund.
