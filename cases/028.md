## Abstract

Meridian Consulting Group, a 2,800-person global professional-services firm with USD 720M in revenue across four practices (Strategy, Digital, Healthcare, and Operations), entered Week 1 of a declared global pandemic on Monday, March 9, 2026. Within 72 hours, its CEO, Aisha Okonkwo, would have to make the first of fifteen decisions that a board-approved adaptive-leadership playbook organized into four phases: Crisis Onset, Mid-Crisis Adaptation, Strategic Pivot, and New Normal. Six leadership KPIs — Trust, Temperature (a Heifetz-style productive-distress metric to be held between 45 and 65), Resilience, Financial Health, Cultural Integrity, and Adaptive Capacity — would be tracked across the playbook. Decision 1 — the workforce announcement — would set the Trust and Temperature baselines. The case opens on the Friday afternoon before Okonkwo's Monday all-hands and sets up Round 1 of the simulation.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Apply Heifetz's adaptive-leadership framework to a live crisis-communication decision, distinguishing technical from adaptive work.
2. Evaluate the trade-off between reassurance and honesty in a CEO's first crisis communication to employees.
3. Analyze the concept of "productive distress" (the Temperature metric) and the cost of keeping it too low or too high.
4. Diagnose the compounding effect of Decision 1 on the remaining fourteen decisions in the playbook.
5. Recommend a framing, a content structure, and a set of specific commitments for the Monday all-hands, and articulate the assumptions behind each.

On the afternoon of **Friday, March 6, 2026**, **Aisha Okonkwo** sat at her desk in Meridian Consulting Group's Singapore headquarters and looked, for the fifth time, at the twelve-sentence draft her Chief Communications Officer had emailed her at 2:47 p.m. The subject line read: *"Monday all-hands — options A, B, C."* The three drafts were visibly different in framing. The first was warmly reassuring. The second was explicit about what was unknown. The third alternated honesty about uncertainty with clarity about next steps. Below the three drafts, Okonkwo had written one line in her notebook, in black ink: *"The temperature I set on Monday is the temperature of every decision after it."*

---

## The Protagonist

Aisha Okonkwo, 54, had been Chief Executive Officer of Meridian Consulting Group since January 2023. A graduate of the University of Lagos and of Oxford's Saïd Business School, she had spent twenty-eight years in consulting: fifteen years at a global strategy firm, six years as regional managing partner for Africa and the Middle East, four years on Meridian's board as a non-executive director before her appointment as CEO. She had led the firm through the 2024 European cyber-incident with a measured, progressive-disclosure communication approach that had been widely studied internally. Her contract carried a performance component tied to a composite leadership-effectiveness score that the board had formally adopted in 2024.

## The Decision

Okonkwo had to decide, by 8:00 a.m. Singapore time on Monday March 9, the content and tone of the all-hands communication that would be Decision 1 of 15 in the adaptive-leadership playbook. The simulation would treat her choice as the first Round 1 input. The three options on the table carried materially different KPI movements:

- **Reassuring-but-Vague**: "We have a plan. We will keep you informed. We are monitoring the situation closely." Expected KPI movement: Trust −3, Temperature −10 (too low; complacency), Adaptive Capacity −3.
- **Fully Transparent Including Uncertainty**: "We do not yet know how deep this will go. We are not making any workforce decisions this week. We will make decisions on a rolling basis and we will tell you when and how." Expected KPI movement: Trust +4, Temperature +12 (productive zone), Adaptive Capacity +4.
- **Honest About Uncertainty + Clear on Next Steps**: "Here is what we know. Here is what we do not yet know. Here is what we will decide by end of Week 2 and how you will hear about it. Here are the three commitments that hold regardless: no layoffs in the first six weeks; full pay for quarantined staff; daily 15-minute open Q&A with me for the duration." Expected KPI movement: Trust +5, Temperature +8 (productive zone), Adaptive Capacity +5.

Fourteen subsequent decisions — including the formation of the crisis team (Decision 4), the strategic pivot in Phase 3 (Decisions 9–11), and the legacy message in Phase 4 (Decision 15) — would sit on top of the Trust and Temperature baseline Decision 1 established.

## Company Background

Meridian Consulting Group had been founded in 1994 in London as a mid-market strategy firm and had expanded over three decades into a 2,800-person global firm with offices in 27 cities across Europe, Asia, the Americas, and Africa. 2025 revenue was USD 720M with an operating margin of 14.6%. Revenue decomposition: Strategy 38%, Digital 24%, Healthcare 22%, Operations 16%. Client concentration was moderate: top-ten clients 34%, top-fifty clients 68%.

The firm was partner-owned under a classic consulting partnership structure: 148 equity partners, of whom 27 were on the Operating Committee. The 2019 Partnership Agreement included an "emergency authorities" clause that gave the CEO unilateral authority for up to 45 days on workforce, pricing, and partnership-distribution decisions during a board-declared crisis. The Meridian board — seven seats, three external directors — had declared a crisis state on Thursday March 5 in a 2:00 a.m. Singapore-time virtual meeting after the World Health Organization's characterization of the outbreak.

Meridian carried USD 120M of revolving-credit capacity (undrawn), a cash position of USD 95M, and a historical partnership-distribution schedule that paid roughly USD 140M of distributions to equity partners across each calendar year. The firm's financial cushion was adequate for six months of moderate revenue impairment and inadequate for twelve months of severe impairment without distribution cuts.

## Industry and Market Context

The professional-services sector entered the crisis with three inherited features that shaped the adaptive-leadership decision space. First, consulting firms had historically relied on in-person client engagement for 70–80% of project delivery; a rapid shift to remote delivery was both technically feasible and culturally uncomfortable. Second, the industry had during the 2020–2022 pandemic shown bifurcated performance: firms that pivoted to digital and healthcare practices early had grown 9–14% while firms that held legacy delivery models had contracted 3–8%. Third, senior-leader communication during crisis onset had been empirically correlated, in post-2022 retrospectives, with 18-month partner-attrition rates (firms rated high on honest-plus-clear communication had attrition 9 percentage points lower than firms rated high on reassurance-without-content).

Three findings from the adaptive-leadership literature were consequential for Okonkwo's decision. First, the "productive-distress zone" (Temperature 45–65 on a 100-point scale) was the range in which adaptive work was most likely to occur; below it, people grew complacent, and above it, people withdrew. Second, the first crisis communication from a CEO established a communication norm that was extremely difficult to move later in either direction. Third, specific, verifiable commitments made in the first communication ("no layoffs for six weeks," "full pay for quarantined staff") built trust at a far higher rate than general reassurances of the same duration.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The pandemic declaration had come on Thursday afternoon. Two Meridian offices — Singapore and Milan — had already closed on Thursday. Three major clients had asked for engagement reviews on Friday. The Meridian Operating Committee had met for three hours on Friday morning. Three partners had, in separate sidebars, asked Okonkwo whether distributions would be cut, whether layoffs were on the table, and whether the firm would match the aggressive work-from-home policy a major competitor had announced that morning.

### What Okonkwo knew

The internal analytics team had modeled the three communication options against a sixteen-week KPI trajectory. Reassuring-but-Vague produced an initial comfort followed by a Trust collapse at Week 4 when the first real bad news landed. Fully Transparent without Next-Steps produced an initial Temperature spike above 70 (anxiety zone) that drove two percentage points of partner-attrition risk. Honest-plus-Next-Steps produced the cleanest Temperature trajectory and the highest Trust and Adaptive-Capacity baseline across the remaining fourteen decisions.

### What she did not know

How deep the pandemic would be (base case: 12 weeks of severe impact; upper case: 16 months). Whether the specific commitments in the Honest-plus-Next-Steps option — six-week no-layoff commitment, full pay for quarantined staff, daily 15-minute open Q&A — would remain deliverable if the upper-case scenario materialized. Whether the three partners who had sidebar'ed her on Friday would accept whichever framing she chose or would begin mobilizing independently.

### What others were saying

**Harish Rao**, Chief Financial Officer, argued for **Reassuring-but-Vague**: *"Do not commit to six weeks of no-layoffs. Do not commit to full pay for quarantined staff. We do not know the duration. Protect your future optionality."*

**Eva Lindström**, Chief People Officer, argued for **Honest-plus-Next-Steps**: *"The three commitments are expensive and they are the whole point. People do not remember what you said. They remember what you committed to. Honor the commitment or change it visibly."*

**James Akinola**, Managing Partner of the Healthcare Practice, argued for **Fully Transparent**: *"Say everything. Commit to nothing. The partners will figure it out. The staff will respect the truth."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Okonkwo had narrowed the space to three options.

- **Option A — Reassuring-but-Vague (Rao plan):** "We have a plan. We will keep you informed." No specific commitments. Expected Trust −3, Temperature −10 (complacency), Adaptive Capacity −3. Future optionality preserved.

- **Option B — Fully Transparent (Akinola plan):** "Here is what we do not know. Here is the full range of possibilities." No specific commitments. Expected Trust 0, Temperature +18 (anxiety-zone risk), Adaptive Capacity +2.

- **Option C — Honest-plus-Next-Steps (Lindström plan):** Three explicit commitments (six-week no-layoff floor; full pay for quarantined staff; daily 15-minute CEO Q&A); clear Week-2 decision calendar; explicit statement of what is unknown. Expected Trust +5, Temperature +8 (productive zone), Adaptive Capacity +5.

Okonkwo suspected Option C dominated the expected path in the base case (12-week impact) and in the upper case (16-month impact) if she honored the commitments or modified them visibly. Option A preserved the most optionality and cost the most trust. Option B was the option her Chief People Officer had labeled "the partner-attrition option." At 5:48 p.m. Okonkwo turned to the draft-on-screen and began editing Option C into the version she would deliver on Monday morning.

The first call would be Monday at 8:00 a.m. Singapore time. The second call would be one hour later in London, then Frankfurt, then New York, then São Paulo, then Johannesburg. The temperature the first call set would be the temperature all five cascaded to.
