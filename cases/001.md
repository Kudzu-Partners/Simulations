## Abstract

Verdant Consumer Group S.A., a forty-year-old Colombian FMCG group with USD 1.2 billion in revenue, carried an MSCI ESG composite of 30 — a B rating, three rungs below the BBB threshold that governs institutional ESG mandates — at the start of 2026. Its pillar scores were Environmental 25, Social 30, and Governance 20, lifted to a composite of 30 only by a financial overlay of 71 earned on a healthy 14.2% EBITDA margin. A new Chief Sustainability Officer, Claudia Restrepo, had been given a five-year budget of USD 20 million per year to transform the group's ESG profile and protect it from a known activist-investor campaign, an in-flight greenwashing investigation in the European Union, and the risk of ESG-fund divestment. Restrepo's first act was to propose a Year 1 allocation across the three pillars — and a strategic stance (balanced, environmental priority, social priority, or financial priority) — to the board. Each pillar was materially underfunded. Each was the target of a different looming crisis. The case puts the reader inside Restrepo's office the evening before the board meeting, as she weighs three internally coherent allocations advocated by three different senior voices.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate a capital-allocation decision across non-financial performance pillars under budget constraint and uncertainty.
2. Analyze how a weighted-average composite rewards concentration in a single pillar while threshold-gated events punish it, and the portfolio implications that follow.
3. Design a multi-year ESG strategy that withstands adversarial events (activist campaigns, regulatory investigations, supply-chain shocks).
4. Diagnose the trade-off between ESG investment and near-term EBITDA margin, and the conditions under which the two cease to trade off.
5. Recommend a defensible first-year allocation and articulate the falsifiable assumptions behind it.

On the evening of **April 14, 2026**, **Claudia Restrepo** stood at the window of her fourteenth-floor office in Verdant Consumer Group's Bogotá headquarters and read, for the third time, the one-page agenda her assistant had printed for the next morning's board meeting. The agenda carried a single substantive item: *"ESG Transformation Plan — Year 1 Allocation."* Below her, the Avenida El Dorado was already dark. On her desk sat a draft slide with three numbers, each a different colored highlighter, none of them yet agreed. In less than fourteen hours she would have to present a single proposal.

---

## The Protagonist

Claudia Restrepo, 44, had joined Verdant six months earlier from a global consumer-goods company where she had built an ESG reporting function from scratch over seven years. Before that she had been an environmental economist at the Banco de la República and, briefly, a partner at a local sustainability consultancy. The Verdant board had recruited her specifically on the strength of two things: her work disclosing Scope 3 emissions across a complex agricultural supply chain, and her reputation for refusing to greenlight "headline projects" that did not move audited metrics. Her contract included a clause — unusual in Colombia — tying a portion of her long-term compensation to the MSCI composite score Verdant carried at the end of Year 5.

---

## The Decision

Restrepo had to recommend, by 9:00 a.m. on April 15, how to split Verdant's Year 1 ESG budget of USD 20 million across three pillars — Environmental, Social, and Governance — and declare a strategic focus: *Balanced, Environmental Priority, Social Priority,* or *Financial Priority*. The board would vote the recommendation up or down as a single package. A no-vote would default the company to the prior year's allocation (USD 5M / 5M / 3M across E/S/G, strategy *balanced*, total USD 13M) and forfeit the remaining USD 7M back to operating cash. Restrepo's mandate from the Chair was simple: "Move the composite. Don't blow up the P&L."

Two constraints shaped the arithmetic before any judgment entered it. First, the pillars did not absorb money equally: the finance committee had capped annual Environmental and Social programme spend at USD 15 million each, and Governance at **USD 8 million** — governance work in the group meant director searches, committee restructures, whistleblower systems and audit capacity, none of which could be accelerated by writing a larger cheque. Second, the USD 20 million was a base, not a fixture. The committee had agreed to release a further USD 5 million in any year that closed with an EBITDA margin above 14%, and to withhold USD 2.5 million for every unmitigated ESG incident in the preceding year, with a floor of USD 8 million. A bad year therefore cost twice: once in score, once in the budget available to repair it.

## Company Background

Verdant Consumer Group was founded in 1986 by the Zuluaga family in Cali as a regional processor of palm oil, sugar, and dairy for the domestic market. Over four decades the group expanded into packaged snacks, personal care, and private-label manufacturing for European grocers, building manufacturing capacity in Cali, Barranquilla, and Medellín, and a distribution network across the Andean region. At the start of 2026 Verdant employed 4,200 people directly and roughly 12,000 across its agricultural supply chain. Annual revenue for 2025 was USD 1.2 billion with an EBITDA margin of 14.2%.

The Zuluaga family still held 46% of the equity through a family holding company and two of the nine board seats. The group had listed on the Bolsa de Valores de Colombia in 2014 and was a constituent of the COLCAP index. Its float was held primarily by domestic pension funds (31%), a single European ESG-screened fund (12%), and retail (11%). The European fund's mandate required a minimum MSCI rating of BBB — a composite of 50 — at the time of portfolio rebalancing, an event due in the third quarter of 2027. At a composite of 30, Verdant was twenty points below threshold, and the fund had signaled in its Q4 2025 stewardship letter that it was "monitoring" the position.

Verdant's culture, forged in the family years, was operational, pragmatic, and margin-centric. Sustainability had, until Restrepo's arrival, been run out of Corporate Affairs as a mix of communications, community relations, and occasional environmental compliance. There was no dedicated ESG data function. There was, however, a well-respected internal audit team and a new Data & Analytics unit stood up in 2024 that Restrepo believed could be retasked.

## Industry and Market Context

The Latin American consumer-goods sector entered 2026 under three simultaneous pressures. First, EU carbon border and deforestation regulations had begun to bite: exporters with palm-oil, soy, or beef exposure faced traceability requirements that would cost incumbents approximately 1.2–2.0% of export revenue to implement. Second, ESG-screened capital — both debt (green bonds, sustainability-linked loans) and equity (screened funds) — had grown to represent roughly USD 38 trillion globally and was increasingly conditional on *pillar-level* performance rather than composite. Third, social-media-driven reputational events on packaging, labor, and sourcing had moved from a "once-a-year" frequency to a "once-a-quarter" frequency for regional FMCG players.

The MSCI ESG rating methodology, in the version most relevant to Verdant, was a straight weighted average: Environmental and Social at 35% each, Governance at 20%, and a 10% overlay derived from financial performance. Rating bands ran in ten-point steps — 30 for B, 40 for BB, 50 for BBB, 60 for A, 70 for AA. There was no weakest-pillar cap and no industry adjustment in the published version. A company could therefore buy its way to BBB on one pillar alone, and several regional peers visibly had.

What the composite did not price, the market did. Activist investors had learned to read the pillar detail rather than the headline: three separate open letters in the Colombian and Mexican consumer-goods space during 2025 had explicitly cited a governance score of 40 as the trigger for a public campaign, and European regulators had opened greenwashing files against issuers whose environmental claims outran a governance score of 50. The pattern was consistent — a high composite carried on a single pillar attracted precisely the scrutiny it was meant to deflect.

Peer benchmarks available to Restrepo showed a composite range from 32 (Viña Austral, Chile) to 71 (AlpineFoods, Perú), with the sector median at 48. Verdant's 30 placed it in the bottom decile.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The board's mandate to transform Verdant's ESG profile had been crystallized by two events in the preceding six months. In October 2025, EU regulators had opened a preliminary greenwashing investigation into a Verdant palm-oil packaging claim ("100% deforestation-free since 2022"). In January 2026, a North-American hedge fund had circulated a draft open letter — not yet public — demanding a governance-pillar score floor of 40 and the addition of two independent directors. Neither event had hit the public tape. Both would, probably within Year 1 of Restrepo's plan.

### What Restrepo knew

The internal analytics team had modeled, to a first approximation, how each pillar responded to investment. Per USD million committed, Governance moved fastest inside its own pillar — roughly 1.5 to 2.2 points against 1.2 to 1.8 for Environmental and 1.0 to 1.5 for Social — but carried only a 0.20 weight in the composite against Environmental's 0.35. On the number the board actually watched, the ranking inverted: at the Year-1 baseline a million dollars bought about 0.44 composite points through Environmental, 0.35 through Social and 0.32 through Governance. Every pillar showed diminishing returns as it rose, each point of score reducing the yield of the next dollar, so late spending on a high pillar was materially less productive than early spending on a low one.

The margin arithmetic was smaller than the boardroom assumed. The analytics team put the drag at roughly 0.4 percentage points of EBITDA margin per USD 20 million of programme spend, against a standing recovery of about 0.3 points a year from ordinary operating improvement — a net cost close to nil at full commitment. An unmitigated incident, by contrast, cost 0.8 points in a single hit where it touched the P&L, and removed USD 2.5 million from the following year's budget in every case. Once the average of the three pillar scores passed 50, an ESG premium began to accrue on shelf pricing (European retailers) and on debt financing (local green-bond spread), partly offsetting the drag; the *Financial Priority* stance halved both the drag and that premium.

### What she did not know

Which crisis would fire first. How severely the Zuluaga family would resist a governance change that required adding independent directors (which would dilute family board representation from 2/9 to 2/11). Whether the EU investigation would be closed quietly or escalated. How rapidly the activist letter would move from draft to public.

### What others were saying

**Santiago Zuluaga**, Vice Chair and family representative, argued for *Environmental Priority*. "We are a food and personal-care company. The public reads environmental. Fund the visible story — solar, recyclable packaging, traceability. Build the brand. Governance is an internal matter."

**Valeria Ochoa**, CFO, argued for *Financial Priority*. "We commit USD 13M, not USD 20M, and only to things that audit clean. The margin cannot take a full 2% hit in Year 1. Pace the transformation. Hit BBB by Year 4, not Year 2."

**Miguel Angel Reyes**, General Counsel and de-facto governance lead, argued for *Balanced* with an emphasis on governance. "The activist letter drops at governance below 40. The greenwashing file opens at governance below 50. Environmental at 25 is bad, but governance at 20 is the fuse — and it is the one pillar we cannot buy in a hurry. Eight million a year is the ceiling, so from 20 it is two years to 40 and three to 50 whatever we decide in Year 3."

---

## Options Under Consideration

Restrepo had narrowed the space to three concrete allocations. Each had an internal advocate.

- **Option A — Environmental Priority (the Zuluaga plan):** Allocate USD 15M to Environmental, USD 3M to Social, USD 2M to Governance; declare Strategy *Environmental Priority* (E multiplier +30%, S and G multipliers −15%). Launch the solar-conversion of the Cali plant, recyclable packaging for the VIVE brand, and a public net-zero commitment. The visible story would be strong and the composite would move fastest of the three plans: modelling put Environmental near 48 by year-end, Social at 32, Governance at 23, and the composite at 39 — one point short of BB. Governance would barely move. The activist letter, if it fired, would land directly on a governance score of roughly 23, and on the group's own trajectory would still be under 40 in Year 5.

- **Option B — Balanced with Governance Emphasis (the Reyes plan):** Allocate USD 7M to Environmental, USD 7M to Social, USD 6M to Governance; declare Strategy *Balanced* (no multipliers). Fund an independent-director search, a compensation-committee restructure, a whistleblower system, and a Scope 3 traceability platform. Year-end modelling put Environmental at roughly 32, Social at 37, Governance at 29, and the composite at 36 — three points behind Option A. The activist threshold of 40 would still not be crossed in Year 1, but Governance would reach it in Year 2 and 50 by Year 4, and the *trajectory* would be legible enough that the letter, if circulated publicly, would lose its sharpest point.

- **Option C — Pace Conservative (the Ochoa plan):** Allocate USD 5M to Environmental, USD 5M to Social, USD 3M to Governance; declare Strategy *Financial Priority* (all pillar multipliers −30%, EBITDA drag halved — and the ESG premium halved with it). USD 7M returned to operating cash. The composite would crawl to roughly 32 by year-end and the margin would hold above 14%, which would in turn release the USD 5M performance tranche. Everything would remain option-open for Year 2, but on most modelled runs the B rating would persist into Year 3 and BBB would not be reached inside the five-year horizon at all.

Restrepo's own instinct, not yet shared with anyone, was that the board would accept whichever plan she recommended provided she could defend it for a hostile forty-minute Q&A. The Chair had told her, in a private call on April 11, that "a plan I can't explain to a pension-fund trustee is worse than no plan."

(See **Exhibit 3** for modeled pillar trajectories under each option and **Exhibit 4** for the event-risk table mapping each of ten known event types to its triggering pillar threshold.)

---

Restrepo closed the laptop, turned off the desk lamp, and looked one last time at the three-highlighted slide. The board would convene at 9:00 a.m. She had fourteen hours to choose among Option A, Option B, and Option C — or to propose a fourth she had not yet written. Whichever she chose would, in the language the Chair preferred, need to be defended "to the pension-fund trustee in the back row." The composite was 30. The budget was USD 20 million. The year had begun.
