# Puerto Roble and the Solana Mall Closure

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1212 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1212 — *Unemployment Types — Frictional, Structural, Cyclical at Your Local Mall* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, September 7, 2026, the owner of Solana Mall told its remaining tenants that the centre would close in sixty days. By the afternoon the news had reached Elvira Mansilla Ortúzar, Mayor of Puerto Roble, who had already been asked twice that month what the city meant to do about an unemployment rate of 8.0%. She called a working session for 09:00 on Thursday, September 10, and gave it forty-five minutes.

Natalia Ugarte Salvatierra, the labour-market analyst the Mayor had hired in January, was to bring three things to that table: a reading of who, exactly, the closure would put out of work; a way of describing the city's 8.0% that the Mayor could repeat in public; and a plan for the CO$50 million the city council had set aside for labour-market programmes. The Mayor had a press conference at 10:00.

## The Protagonist

Ugarte was thirty-six. She had spent nine years at the National Statistics Office of Costaria, the last three running the regional tables of its quarterly labour-force survey, before the Mayor recruited her to build a two-person analysis unit inside city hall. She knew how the official unemployment rate was put together and what it left out. She had never designed a programme budget, and she had never had to explain a statistic to a microphone.

## The Decision

The working session had three parts, taken one after the other. First, Ugarte had to classify eight representative workers from the mall, whose files the city's employment office had prepared (see **Exhibit 2**). Second, she had to choose the framing the Mayor would use for the city's unemployment rate. Third, she had to split the CO$50 million across five programmes. The city's planning unit would then run her allocation forward twenty-four months to see where the rate, and the eight workers, ended up.

## The Company and its Setting

Solana Mall opened in 1998 on the southern edge of Puerto Roble, a mid-sized city in the Republic of Costaria. At its peak it held sixty shops. It employed 220 people directly and supported about 80 more through cleaning and security contractors, food trucks and the businesses around its car parks.

The decline had lasted five years. Foot traffic was 35% below its 2019 peak. Three anchor stores had closed. Online sales had reached 28% of all retail spending in Costaria, against 9% a decade earlier. The tenants still trading were mostly fast-food outlets, a cinema and a fitness centre. Other malls in the city had already shut.

The closure came in a difficult year. Costaria was in a general downturn that had cut national output by 3% in 2026. At the same time, cleaning contractors across the city were replacing floor staff with robotic cleaners, a shift expected to remove a quarter of the sector's jobs over five years. Some employers were still hiring: coffee shops in the city centre, and logistics, hospitality and service firms looking for experienced supervisors.

## The Situation

Puerto Roble's unemployment rate stood at 8.0%. For the session, Ugarte's unit had broken it into three parts using the statistics office's method (see **Exhibit 1**). About 3.0 percentage points were frictional: the movement between jobs that the city carried in any normal year. About 2.0 points were structural: workers whose skills no longer matched the jobs on offer at the wages they expected. About 3.0 points were cyclical, attributed to the current downturn.

The eight files did not all describe the same kind of loss. One worker was still employed, but at 12 hours a week when she wanted 35. One had stopped looking for work after thirty unsuccessful applications. One was a contract electrician who would lose his largest client but not all of his work. The other five had lost, or were about to lose, their jobs outright, each for reasons the file set out.

If the city did nothing, the 8.0% had no obvious reason to fall before the national economy turned, and Solana Mall's workers would meet the labour market on their own in sixty days. Holding the money back was not an option: the council's resolution committed the whole CO$50 million to the five programmes on the table that year.

## What Ugarte Controlled

For each of the **eight workers**, Ugarte chose one category: frictional, structural, cyclical, seasonal, underemployed (working part-time against his or her wishes), or discouraged worker (out of the labour force). The employment office's chief economist would compare each choice with the office's own reading of the file.

For the **framing**, the communications office had drafted three lines. The first called the problem a 3% recession: a cyclical issue, the same story the national government was telling, and easy to say in ten seconds. The second called it a structural decline: retail shrinking for good and automation reaching jobs like cleaning, a story that matched what Puerto Roble had watched at its malls for five years. The third presented the 8.0% as three different problems inside one number, each needing its own response. It was the most detailed of the three, and the hardest to fit into a headline.

For the **budget**, five departments had submitted costed proposals (see **Exhibit 1**). The employment office proposed job matching: a better public job-search platform, recruitment events with employers and a small search subsidy for each registered job-seeker. The education department proposed retraining grants, programmes of six to twelve months to move workers from retail into logistics or eldercare. Public works proposed to bring forward a planned road project and a school renovation. Social services proposed targeted programmes for the hardest cases: basic literacy support, outreach to employers on age bias, and paid work-experience placements, at about CO$45,000 per participant. The finance office proposed wage subsidies and short-term jobs of last resort with public and non-profit employers, for anyone out of work more than six months. As costed, the five proposals came to CO$50 million together. Ugarte could fund any of them above or below its proposal, or not at all, provided the five amounts summed to exactly CO$50 million.

The Mayor's dashboard would follow the session. It showed the city unemployment rate, 8.0% at the start and the twenty-four-month projection once the budget was set; how many of the eight files Ugarte had classified as the employment office had; the framing she chose; how many of the eight workers her programmes would reach; and a mark out of 100 for each of the three parts. The Mayor would judge the morning on the three marks together.

## Closing

At 08:20 on Thursday, Ugarte laid the eight files across her desk in two rows. The Mayor's chief of staff had already sent a message asking for "one sentence on the 8%" before the press conference. The budget sheet was open on her screen, five cells empty, and a total at the bottom that had to read CO$50 million.
