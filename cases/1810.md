# Northlight Software: The Belonging Gap

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1810 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1810 — *Building Belonging* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, March 9, 2026, Priya Anand read Northlight's quarterly engagement pulse for the Payments Platform squad before trusting the number underneath it. The headline looked fine: belonging across the twelve-person squad averaged 3.4 out of 5. Split by tenure and background, newer and under-represented members averaged 2.6. That week, a senior engineer who had joined eight months earlier resigned, citing "never quite fitting in," and a written complaint reached Priya describing how two other squad members had been talked over in a design review and left out of the after-work channel where decisions got made. Priya had led the squad for six weeks. She had one quarter to close the gap between 3.4 and 2.6 — and a GBP 3.5 million client release was due in ten weeks. Over that quarter she would have to decide how to read the data, where to spend a capped effort budget, and how to handle the complaint and the engineer behind it.

## The Protagonist

Priya Anand, 34, had spent four years as a senior engineer on the Payments Platform squad before being promoted to lead it seven weeks earlier — the promotion Neil Sturgess, the squad's longest-tenured engineer, had expected for himself. Sturgess had shipped more of the squad's core payments code than anyone else and prefaced most objections with some version of "we've always done it this way." Priya's promotion came with Northlight's fastest-growing product line, and a probationary read from her manager on whether a first-time people leader could hold the delivery calendar and the team together. She had no formal training in either.

## The Decision

Priya's task was not one choice but a sequence across a team cycle: how to interpret the pulse data; which interventions, funded from an effort-and-time budget Northlight capped at GBP 40,000 for the quarter, changed who got heard rather than appearing to; how to handle the complaint and the engineer who had set the squad's tone without worsening delivery pressure; and what to leave behind once her attention moved elsewhere.

## The Company and its Setting

Northlight Software Ltd was a Manchester-based B2B SaaS company with GBP 46 million in annual recurring revenue and 410 employees — double the headcount it had carried two years earlier. That growth had outpaced the culture it grew from: a "high-talent, high-trust" engineering ethos suited to a smaller, homogeneous company, never rebuilt for a workforce far more mixed in background, tenure, and working pattern. The Payments Platform squad — engineers, a designer, a product manager, a QA lead, and a data analyst, twelve in total — carried Northlight's newest, fastest-growing product line, which was why old culture and new headcount collided there first: the squad shipped well, and its newest, most different members were the quietest in the room and the first to leave.

## The Situation

Four numbers anchored the quarter ahead (see Exhibit 1). Belonging — feeling valued and able to be oneself on the team — stood at 2.6 out of 5 for newer and under-represented members, against 4.2 for the long-tenured core and a company benchmark of 3.9; Northlight wanted the newer-member figure at 3.5 or above by quarter's end. Psychological safety — feeling safe to speak up, disagree, or admit a mistake without being humiliated for it — sat at 41 out of 100 for the same members, against a company average of 72. Squad engagement, a 0–100 measure of how invested people said they were, had slid to 61 from 76 three quarters earlier, entirely among newer joiners. Regretted attrition — the share of unwanted departures — ran at 22% annualized among members with under eighteen months' tenure, against a 12% ceiling and 5% among the core. A further pattern sat underneath: about 70% of what got said in squad meetings came from three long-tenured voices, and decisions that did not get made there tended to reappear in the after-work channel.

## What Priya Controlled

Her first task was how to read the pulse: split it by tenure and work from the 2.6 underneath, call the 3.4 close enough to the 3.9 benchmark to be a minor tune-up, or read the resignation as one person's story rather than a wider signal. She also had to decide what she was measuring — psychological safety on its own, with belonging treated as only a softer, secondary extra; two distinct things, being valued for themselves and feeling safe to speak up, reinforcing each other; or the same underlying engagement problem, simply relabeled and presented under two different names — and to name a cause: the specific individuals who spoke the least, or dominated the most; no real structural cause at all, just a morale event expected to help; or three recurring patterns — dominant voices in meetings, decisions migrating to the after-work channel, and new proposals losing out to established routine. Underneath sat a fourth call: how much weight the 2.6 carried against the 3.4.

Her second task was where to spend effort. As a headline move: restructure meetings so airtime rotated and written input counted as much as spoken; send a values email and put an inclusion poster in the kitchen; or hold a public all-hands celebrating the squad's diversity, with two of the excluded members telling their story to the company. On the after-work channel: ban it and after-hours socializing outright, with no exceptions; move its decisions into a forum the whole squad could see and join; or leave it exactly as it was, with no changes at all. For anything longer-lived: set up onboarding buddies and sponsorship alongside norms the squad wrote together; name one newer member the visible face of the squad's diversity; or build nothing new and let things settle — funded from a capped GBP 40,000 quarterly budget the planning tool defaulted to GBP 14,000.

Her third task ran through her own conduct as much as anyone else's: change her meeting habits — inviting disagreement, drawing out quieter members, admitting when she was wrong — or leave them as they were. On the two excluded members: say nothing and treat the release as the priority; acknowledge it openly, repair it with them directly, and change how that decision got made; or have a quiet word telling them it had not been meant that way. On Sturgess: leave him alone given how much the release depended on him; correct him openly in front of the squad; or coach him privately, naming the behaviour and enlisting him in resetting the norm. And when the in-group argued there was no time for this before the release shipped: set the work aside for ten weeks; offer a smaller gesture now and the larger work later; or argue that safety and belonging were themselves part of delivering the release.

Her fourth task was what would outlast the quarter. She could wait for the next quarterly pulse score to come in on its own; track nothing formally in between, and simply judge the climate by feel; or watch weekly indicators — who spoke, how often newer members raised ideas, one-to-one sentiment, early signs someone was about to leave. She could write whatever changed into the team's working system — norms, meeting formats, onboarding, handover; run a one-off campaign with a launch event; or leave it dependent on her own continued attention. In the three-minute pitch to the People Director and Sturgess: an optimistic assurance the numbers would follow; a case built on the indicators already in motion; or a defensive case that inclusion was right regardless of the deadline. How firmly she held that belonging belonged to the system, not a one-time campaign, was hers to decide.

## Closing

At 8:50 a.m., Priya closed the pulse dashboard and opened a blank page to plan the quarter. The GBP 3.5 million release was ten weeks out, and the in-group's view — that there was no time for this until it shipped — remained. By the quarter's end, Northlight would check whether newer-member belonging had reached 3.5 out of 5 and whether regretted attrition had come down to 12% or below (see Exhibit 2). If either number missed, two more flight-risk engineers were expected to resign before the release was finished.
