# Acme: One Message to a Stranger

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-939 · **Companion simulation:** Express 939 — *ColdOutreach — The Subject Line, the Ask, the Close* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, April 21, 2026, at 8:30 in the morning, Daniel Ferrán Soler had been looking at a blank message for twenty minutes. He needed twenty-five minutes of a stranger's time, and he had one message in which to ask for them. Every draft he had started sounded presumptuous, desperate or generic.

Ferrán, head of operations at Acme, wanted to send it before noon. The recipient was Ingrid Solberg, a pricing adviser whose May 2024 article had argued that customer retention was a pricing problem rather than a customer-success problem. Acme's board would review the company's pricing in four weeks. He wanted her view before then, and she owed him nothing.

## The Protagonist

Ferrán was thirty-nine. He had spent twelve years at a large payments company before joining Acme as a vice president in 2019, held an MBA from a business school in Barcelona and had started his career as a management consultant. He had written hundreds of messages to clients and colleagues. He wrote to strangers perhaps twice a year, and most of those had gone unanswered.

## The Decision

Ferrán had to decide what the message said, and he worked through it in three passes. First, the subject line. Second, the body: how he introduced himself, why he was writing to Solberg in particular, what exactly he asked for, and whether he gave her an easy way to say no. Third, the close: how he signed off and whether, and how often, he would follow up if she did not reply.

## The Company and its Setting

Acme was a Barcelona-based company that sold subscription software to mid-sized business customers across Europe. Its customers renewed every year, and over the previous twelve months more of them had been leaving at renewal than the year before. Ferrán's team ran a quarterly business review with every large account. After reading Solberg's article in 2024 they had changed how that review worked, and the change had been one of his arguments for putting pricing on the board's agenda.

Solberg did not know him. Maria Chen, a former colleague of Ferrán's who had worked with Solberg on a pricing project, had suggested he write and had said he could use her name. Solberg's public profile listed her as an adviser to several software companies. She wrote often and spoke at conferences; people who knew her said her inbox ran to about two hundred unread messages on a normal day, and that she decided in a few seconds, from the subject line, the sender and the first sentence, whether to read further (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The message was blank. There was no subject, no body and no signature, and so no reply. If Ferrán did not send anything, the board would review pricing in four weeks without Solberg's view, and the question of whether she would have helped would stay open.

Advice was not in short supply. Acme's sales director said the best subject line was "Quick question," because people could not resist opening it. A friend in marketing told him to lead with his full background, since credibility was what got replies from senior people. Acme's co-founder said persistence won: three follow-ups, spaced out, and most people answered eventually. Chen told him to keep it short and to say exactly what he wanted.

## What Ferrán Controlled

For the subject line he had five candidates: "Quick question"; "Connecting"; "Re: your recent post"; "Maria Chen suggested I write — 25 min on B2B pricing"; and "Question on your 2024 article on B2B churn."

For the body he made four choices. The opening line: "I hope this email finds you well"; a one-line introduction as head of operations at Acme, writing because he had read her churn piece carefully; or his full background, pasted from his professional profile. The reason he was writing to her: a general compliment on her career; a specific reference to her 2024 point that retention was a pricing problem, and how it had changed Acme's quarterly review; or no reason line at all. The ask: to pick her brain sometime soon; that he would love to connect; or for twenty-five minutes in the next three weeks. And the easy out: a line saying that if the timing was bad there was no problem and he would value being able to write again, or no such line.

For the close he chose a sign-off, either his name, role, company and one link to Acme's work, his full profile link with three credentials, or just his first name. He also chose a follow-up plan: none; one follow-up on day seven, then move on; or three, on days three, seven and fourteen.

As he worked, a writing tool Acme's sales team used showed three readings of the draft (see Exhibit 2). The first was a predicted reply rate, as a percentage. The second was a specificity score out of 20, a measure of how much of the message could only have been written to this one person. The third was the length of the message in words. After each pass it logged all three. Ferrán would judge the message the way Solberg would: by whether she answered.

## Closing

At 8:50 Ferrán typed a subject line, deleted it and typed another. Chen had replied to his text from the night before: "Use my name if it helps. She's busy, not unkind." The cursor sat at the start of the first line of the body.
