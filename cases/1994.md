# Reef Coast Futures: The Twelve-Month Mandate

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1994 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1994 — *Place-Based Venture* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Wednesday, 21 January 2026, two letters reached Robyn Casterton four hours apart. The first, from the mining-services contractor that had anchored the regional economy for three decades, confirmed a wind-down: 600 direct jobs, and an estimated 900 more in the firms supplying it. The second, from the state's regional programs office, renewed Reef Coast Futures' grant and attached a condition: of the agency's A$8 million annual program budget, A$3.2 million — 40 per cent — would be released only against outcomes demonstrated twelve months out, namely ventures activated, jobs created, and private capital drawn in alongside the agency's own.

Casterton had been chief executive for nine days. Before that review she had to decide how to spend the agency's discretionary money across a region of 230,000 people, what enterprise support to buy with it, and which institutions to fund as partners. The letter added, without elaboration, that the state was comparing Reef Coast Futures with another regional agency for a larger allocation the following year.

## The Protagonist

Robyn Casterton, 46, had grown up in a sugar town south of Townsville and left the region at nineteen. She spent eleven years in regional commercial banking, then six years in Brisbane running industry-development programs for a state department, where she drafted funding guidelines like those now applied to her. The Reef Coast Futures board recruited her in November 2025 on one instruction: stop funding programs the agency could not show results for. Her predecessor had not been reappointed. Casterton's contract fell due three months after the state's review.

## The Decision

Four questions had to be answered in sequence, each settled before the next could be tested: what this regional economy was made of; where the discretionary budget should go across the ten ventures in the pipeline; what form the support program should take and who should be paid to deliver it; and what Casterton should put in front of the state at the review.

## The Company and its Setting

Reef Coast Futures was a not-for-profit regional development agency in Townsville, North Queensland, funded by state grants, local-government levies and private contributions. Its mandate was jobs and durable enterprises across a regional economy of 230,000 people that had leaned for decades on mining services, sugar and tourism, all exposed to commodity prices and weather.

The region had the highest rate of small-business formation in regional Queensland and one of the lowest survival rates: 42 per cent of ventures were still trading at three years, against a national regional benchmark of 55 per cent (see **Exhibit 1**). People started businesses here; few of them grew. Local capital was thin, customers and suppliers were a long way off, and specialist labour was scarce. Against those constraints sat three capabilities the agency's own briefing papers called under-used: a marine-science base, renewable-energy potential, and agricultural-technology work adjacent to the sugar industry.

## The Situation

The previous attempt was recent: A$1.5 million on a twelve-week accelerator modelled on capital-city programs. Two years after the first cohort, three of the ventures that passed through it were still trading. The board had read the file.

Casterton would be measured on four numbers, and the state had named which (see **Exhibit 2**). Ventures activated counted enterprises demonstrably scaling — not grants issued, not businesses registered. Jobs created counted new private-sector positions. Funding leverage measured private money committed for every A$1 the agency put in; it stood at 0.4x, and the state had named 1.5x. The fourth was an ecosystem-engagement score, an index from 0 to 100 the agency kept of the health of its relationships with the university, the lenders, the councils and the industry bodies. It stood at 40; the first two numbers stood at zero.

Of the A$8 million, A$4.8 million was discretionary, the portion Casterton could direct at ventures rather than committed operating costs. Ten ventures sat in the pipeline, across sectors and stages. The grants team had historically written cheques of between A$80,000 and A$600,000, and the arithmetic ran one way: A$4.8 million would fund all ten thinly or three or four deeply, not both.

If the agency spent 2026 as it had spent the two years before, the review was not hard to anticipate: leverage near 0.4x, since nothing in the existing program obliged a partner to commit money of its own; survival near 42 per cent; and the A$3.2 million unclaimed while the state set the agency's numbers beside a rival region's.

## What Casterton Controlled

**Reading the region.** Three diagnoses were available. One held that this was a regional economy like others, and that a model proven elsewhere would work here. A second held that mining services, sugar and tourism were what the region knew, and that recovery meant rebuilding around them. A third held that the marine-science, renewables and agricultural-technology capabilities were under-used, and that support should be built around them. The same paper had to say why the accelerator left three survivors: that it was under-funded; that the region's founders were not strong enough; or that it was a design imported from cities unlike this one. And it had to say whose ventures the programs would reach: the established operators the agency knew; Indigenous enterprise and the young, under-employed workforce the state weighted in its measures; or whichever group would move the jobs total fastest. The paper closed by naming how many sectors the agency would concentrate on, anywhere from one to six.

**Allocating the money.** The A$4.8 million could go to all ten ventures in small amounts, to about six at a middle depth, or to three or four backed at depth. The region's distinctive capabilities could carry no weight, so that funding followed applications as they arrived; some weight, as a tiebreaker between comparable ventures; or heavy weight, favouring the marine, renewables and agricultural-technology ventures. The 600 jobs could take direct support at the contractor to hold them longer, transition funding moving those workers and their suppliers into other ventures, or nothing at all.

**Buying a program, choosing partners.** Three program shapes had been costed: a twelve-week accelerator of the kind the agency had run; a sector incubator in marine and renewables with a founder skills course and a market-access track; or direct grants with no program attached, leaving founders to buy their own support. Partner funding could be split evenly across the university, the banks, three councils and four industry bodies; directed at the most visible of them; or concentrated on the two or three that would put co-investment and capability in alongside it. A community-enterprise and Indigenous-business stream could be funded in full, funded as a symbolic line item, or left unfunded. From the partners she prioritised, Casterton would seek co-investment of nothing, A$0.60, or A$1.40 for every A$1 the agency put in.

**The review.** At twelve months the renewal could fund the same spread again, concentrate on whatever had produced jobs and leverage, or go into the contractor's wind-down to hold the jobs total up longer. She could lead with the volume of grants distributed and how evenly they were spread across the region's towns; with the outcomes and the case that the agency's money had caused them; or with the single strongest number in the file. Assessors were required to ask whether the outcomes would have happened anyway: she could claim the region's results broadly, separate what the agency had caused from what it had not, or treat causation as the state's problem rather than hers. The submission closed by asking her to state, from 0 to 100 per cent, how much confidence she placed in her own reading of the region against a broad and familiar spread.

## Closing

At five o'clock on 21 January, Casterton pinned three sheets to the meeting-room wall: the pipeline list, the four measures, and the state's letter. The contractor's first redundancies were scheduled for March. The review was twelve months out; the A$4.8 million had to be committed well before then.
