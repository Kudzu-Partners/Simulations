# Maasvliet Mobility Systems: The Cost Gap and the Border

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1912 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1912 — *Going Global* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Wednesday, 10 June 2026, Sanne Verhoeven read two documents within the same hour and saw that they had to be answered together. The first was a joint letter from Maasvliet Mobility Systems' three largest customers — commercial-vehicle manufacturers that between them bought 61% of what the company made — requiring a price reduction of 8% to 12% at contracts falling due in 120 days, failing which they would place a second source with a lower-cost Asian competitor. The second was a note from the company's trade counsel on export-control and tariff measures adopted that week, which changed the legal and landed cost of moving advanced battery-management technology across several borders Maasvliet already used.

Verhoeven was Maasvliet's director of strategy. The board wanted one plan, on 8 October 2026, that recovered at least EUR 45 million of annual cost, decided how the company would serve a North American order pipeline worth USD 90 million — about EUR 83 million — needing a local-presence decision within two quarters, and did neither of those things the way the company had in 2019.

## The Protagonist

Sanne Verhoeven, 46, joined Maasvliet in 2011 as a manufacturing engineer and had been director of strategy since 2023. In between she spent eighteen months on the team that unwound the 2019 offshoring programme, which meant she drafted the memo that closed it and was in the room when the board read it. Two of the four executives who would sign off her plan had voted for that programme.

## The Decision

Verhoeven had to settle four things in sequence and defend them as one plan: what kind of work the cell-balancing design and battery-management software were; the shape of the sourcing footprint; how Maasvliet entered North America; and how all of it was put to a board that had told her it would be listening for 2019.

## The Company and its Setting

Maasvliet Mobility Systems B.V., founded in Rotterdam in 1994, made electric drivetrains, battery-management systems and charging electronics for commercial vehicles. It employed 3,100 people on revenue of EUR 780 million at a 9% operating margin, selling across Europe with a small, fast-growing North American order book. Its engineering was not the problem: fully loaded European manufacturing and back-office costs ran 30% to 40% above the Asian benchmark.

The company had tried to close that gap once. In 2019 it moved battery-pack assembly, including the cell-balancing step, to a contract manufacturer abroad; quality defects followed, and so did a copy of the proprietary design. The work came back in 2022. Nobody called 2019 a success, but there was no settled account of what had gone wrong — the vendor, the jurisdiction, the activity, or the fact of moving at all.

## The Situation

The arithmetic in the letter was not in dispute. The three customers accounted for 61% of EUR 780 million, roughly EUR 476 million of revenue; a cut of 8% on that was about EUR 38 million of contribution and 12% about EUR 57 million, against group operating profit of some EUR 70 million. Conceding and changing nothing else removed more than half the operating profit in one renewal cycle; refusing started the threatened dual-sourcing, and volume moved to a second supplier did not return. Hence the board's fixed target of EUR 45 million a year (see **Exhibit 1**).

Counsel's note added a constraint absent in 2019: a tariff swing against the wrong footprint could add 6% to 10% to landed cost, and a listing could stop a lane rather than merely reprice it. Separately, the incentives that made the USD 90 million pipeline worth winning were gated by a local-content test — product below the threshold did not qualify, whatever it cost to ship. The 2019 file supplied one further figure: on the company's estimate, the copied design had compressed gross margin by six to eight points over the following two years.

The board would read the plan against four measures and had given Verhoeven their opening readings (see **Exhibit 2**): recurring annual cost recovered, at zero against the EUR 45 million target; retained control over the technology behind the company's pricing power, at 70 out of 100; how far North America was being served, at 30; and robustness to a tariff move or a listing, at 50. Only the first carried a required figure.

## What Verhoeven Controlled

The first commitment was a classification and two statements following from it. The cell-balancing design and the battery-management software could be recorded as proprietary work kept in-house whatever the saving; as assembly work like any other, relocatable wherever the saving was large enough; or as undetermined, priced like any other cost line. Against that she would name the activities open to global sourcing: only those that were not proprietary — back office, power-electronics assembly, testing, after-sales; every activity carrying a saving, cell-balancing assembly included; or none. And she would state what 2019 had been: a mismatch between where an activity went and how critical it was; a poor choice of vendor; or an accident carrying no transferable lesson.

The second commitment had five parts, the first two of them independent of each other. Where the relocated work sat: a nearshore group of sites in Central and Eastern Europe and Morocco, with Mexico serving the North American lane; an offshore footprint in Asia; or the Netherlands. Who ran it: Maasvliet-owned operations; third-party providers; or company-run near the technology and third-party elsewhere. Where the cell-balancing and battery-management work physically sat: in the Netherlands in-house; nearshore in a Maasvliet-run site; or offshore alongside everything else. How much moved: the share of the relocatable cost base relocated, from none of it to all of it in steps of five points, standing at 40. And whether the main relocated lane ran through two jurisdictions at slightly higher unit cost, or a single hub at the lowest available.

The third commitment was North America, beginning with the mode. Exporting from Europe stood up fastest, tied up the least capital, and was priced by tariffs and measured against the content test. Licensing a US partner was also fast and capital-light, and placed the technology in the partner's hands. A joint venture split the cost and the risk, counted as local presence for the incentives, and left Maasvliet governing alongside someone else. A wholly owned plant was entirely Maasvliet's, counted fully as local presence, took about 30 months to open and absorbed the most capital. She would also say whether anything served the pipeline meanwhile — a lighter arrangement now with a transition to higher control later, none at all, or none needed because the chosen mode already shipped; how far she had modelled the policy — tariff exposure and the content threshold both, freight and cost only, or neither; and how much capital to commit now, from nothing to EUR 120 million in steps of five, standing at 15.

The fourth commitment was the board meeting, and the disturbance behind the fourth measure: a tariff increase on whatever offshore lane the plan used, the same week an export-control listing named a joint-venture partner. Should that land, she could move to a second lane or fallback mode already written into the plan; absorb the added landed cost and the delay; or rebuild the footprint in the room. She could put the plan as one set of trade-offs across all four measures, lead with the EUR 45 million the customers were demanding, or lead with the control the board remembered. And she had three versions of the sentence to use if someone said the plan sounded like 2019: that the technology stayed in-house and off flagged borders with a named second source; that the economics were better and the upside carried the risk; or that the team was more experienced and it would not happen again. Last, she would state how firmly she held the position, as a number from 0 to 100.

## Closing

That evening Verhoeven printed the activity list on one sheet and the four measures on another. The renewal letters had arrived with 120 days on them, and the North American pipeline was already being quoted by a competitor who shipped there. She had not chosen which sentence to use if someone said 2019.
