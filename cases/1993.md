# Brisbane Metro Utilities: The Ninety-Day Compliance Pilot

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1993 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1993 — *Nudge to Comply* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 9 February 2026, Tessa Kavanagh laid four documents side by side in Brisbane Metro Utilities' seventh-floor operations room. A fine notice issued to a pensioner who had breached nothing. A field report on a household disconnected in 39°C heat. A letter from the state utilities regulator opening an inquiry into what it called the company's punitive and ineffective approach to compliance. And a page of management accounts showing that chasing customers had cost A$6.8 million in the past year.

The regulator's interim report was due in ninety days. Kavanagh had those ninety days, and a pilot budget capped at A$3 million, to put something else in front of it.

## The Protagonist

Kavanagh, 44, had joined Brisbane Metro Utilities Ltd (BMU) in November 2025 as its first Head of Customer Transformation, out of a state revenue office, where she spent six years rewriting payment letters and lodgement reminders. She had been hired on an argument she made in her final interview: that BMU's arrears and rule-breaking were not a moral failing to be punished but a behaviour with identifiable causes, and that the company had never tested the assumption. The board funded the pilot. If the regulator wrote BMU's compliance practice for it, the role she had been hired to create would go with the argument that created it.

## The Decision

Kavanagh had to choose which behaviour to work on, what to build for it, how widely to deploy it, and what to put to the regulator at the end — each in sequence, the previous choice already spent. A finding against BMU carried a penalty of up to A$5 million and a binding order to overhaul compliance practice.

## The Company and its Setting

BMU supplied water and electricity to 1.4 million residential and small-business customers across South East Queensland on annual revenue of A$1.9 billion. Assembled two decades earlier out of municipal providers, it met customers through three channels never designed together: a self-service app, two call centres, and field crews who walked onto properties to read meters. Its prices were set in a periodic review run by the same regulator now examining its conduct — an adverse finding would sit in the file the next time BMU argued for its tariffs.

## The Situation

Four behaviours made up the compliance problem, and they did not resemble each other (see **Exhibit 2**). Non-payment and late payment ran at 11% of accounts — roughly 154,000 — and tied up A$47 million in overdue receivables. During a Level-3 water restriction in the record heat of late January, an estimated 18% of households used water outside the rules, drawing reservoirs down about nine days faster than the operating model assumed and bringing closer a Level-4 restriction binding all 1.4 million customers. Field crews were being turned away at the gate. And the hardship register, which suspended collection for customers who could not pay, carried claims the credit team could not substantiate.

BMU's answer had been the one in the manual: 4,200 automated fine notices and a run of disconnection warnings, issued during the heatwave. Within a fortnight a wrongly fined pensioner and a disconnected hardship family were circulating on social media, complaints were up 34% on the prior quarter, the Net Trust score had fallen 12 points, and the regulator's letter had arrived (see **Exhibit 1**).

Left alone, enforcement would keep costing A$6.8 million a year, the A$47 million would keep sitting on the balance sheet, and in ninety days the regulator would write its own remedy and set the penalty where it liked.

Inside the building the argument had hardened. Deepa Rangan, general manager of customer operations, held that enforcement had not failed but had been aimed badly, and that the answer was better targeting. Julian Petrakis, who ran digital channels, held that only the app and smart-meter data could reach and measure 1.4 million customers inside ninety days, and inside the budget. Outside it, a consumer-advocacy coalition chaired by Fiona Ostrowski was preparing a class complaint over the wrongful fines and had asked what BMU would do instead.

## What Kavanagh Controlled

The ninety days broke into four windows, four commitments in each.

In the first she picked the behaviour to address: hardship-claim fraud, where the conduct was deliberate and the case for a consequence was strongest; non-payment, the largest sum of money and the largest number of accounts; restriction breaches, the behaviour that had produced the headline and the reservoir shortfall; or all four in one campaign, leaving nothing unaddressed. She fixed how its drivers would be read: as an unwillingness to do the right thing, whose lever was consequences; as one undifferentiated problem with one intervention behind it; or split by whether the barrier was ability, access or willingness. She chose what the reading rested on: management's own knowledge of its customers, the ledger of who had been fined most, or segmented account data, journey maps and complaint transcripts. And she set, on a dial from a quick pass to an exhaustive one, how much of the window went to sizing and splitting the problem before design began.

In the second she specified the intervention. The mechanism was one of four: a last-warning message counting down to disconnection; a comparison telling customers how their behaviour sat against their neighbours'; a bundle of an opt-out payment plan, one-tap hardship registration and pre-deadline reminders; or a pledge asking customers to commit to a target themselves. The channel was digital, through app prompts and smart-meter alerts; human, through frontline scripts, outbound calls and community ambassadors; or a blend routed by who could be reached which way. On transparency, equity and consent she could leave the question, on the ground that a prompt is not an enforcement action; keep the pressure and soften how it read; or run a full check before release. And she committed the build spend — A$200,000, A$600,000 or A$1.4 million of the A$3 million.

In the third she set the deployment. Scope was a randomised pilot on the target group; a randomised test running human and digital arms side by side; a single-suburb trial in one postcode; or immediate release to all 1.4 million customers. Against it she set a comparison: none, measuring before and after; a suburb chosen for resemblance; or a randomly assigned control group. She placed enforcement — withdrawn entirely, left running unchanged alongside the new work, or narrowed to the deliberate cases. And with Ostrowski's complaint and the report date approaching, she set on a dial how hard to push speed against rigour.

In the fourth she would read what came back and decide what BMU would say. She could take the overall movement as the result; note any sub-group that moved the wrong way and set it aside as immaterial; or break the results out by segment and channel. She would scale everything, stop everything and return to enforcement, or scale, redesign and stop by piece. She would give the regulator the single strongest number, a commitment to tougher enforcement described in the new vocabulary, or the comparison, the effect by segment and the guardrails. And she would state, on a dial in front of a regulator taking notes, how firmly she held the position she was hired for.

Five measures followed her the whole way (see **Exhibit 1**): projected compliance uplift, in percentage points over what enforcement alone would have delivered; a behaviour-change index scored 0 to 100 on how well the diagnosis, channel, ethics and evidence held together; adoption, the share of the target group actually reached and moved; what was left of the A$3 million; and BMU's standing with the regulator and the advocacy coalition, reported in words rather than numbers: at risk, under watch, or trusted. It stood at the first of those.

## Closing

At 8:20 a.m. Kavanagh cleared the table and wrote the four behaviours on the whiteboard, the number of accounts beside each. Rangan was due at nine, Petrakis at ten, and Ostrowski had asked for a date. Ninety days, A$3 million, and a regulator that had already written down what it thought of BMU's judgement.
