# Severnvale University Hospitals: Forty-Eight Hours

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1942 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1942 — *Lead Through the Storm* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

At 18:40 on Friday, 7 November 2026, Ruth Adeyemi took two calls four minutes apart. The regional ambulance controller reported a chemical release at a transport interchange: dozens injured, and people who had not waited for an ambulance already walking into the Emergency Department at Severnvale University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust. Her chief information officer reported that a ransomware attack had locked the electronic patient record and the bed-management system. Both were down. Across two hospital sites, 8,200 staff had gone to paper.

Regional command would review the trust at hour 48. Adeyemi, Severnvale's Chief Executive, had until 18:40 on Sunday to show that the surge had been contained, that patient flow was stable, and that the trust's own command had held together. If the review found otherwise, regional command would impose external incident management, and Severnvale's leaders would stop deciding anything.

## The Protagonist

Adeyemi, 52, had come up through hospital operations: bed manager at a district general, head of urgent care, then Chief Operating Officer at a neighbouring trust before Severnvale's board made her Chief Executive in 2022. Her mandate had been elective recovery, delivered by tightening the centre — a daily executive grip meeting, one reporting line for capacity, one version of the numbers. The board liked the grip. The June 2026 staff survey recorded that most clinical respondents felt decisions about their departments were taken somewhere they could not see. She had answered with a listening exercise and no change to the reporting line.

She had run three major-incident exercises since her appointment. All three had assumed the patient record system stayed up.

## The Decision

Four decisions faced her across the 48 hours, each closing before the next opened: what the trust would treat as true while it could not see itself; how the response pool would be rationed; where decisions would be taken, and by whom; and what she would say to the wards.

## The Company and its Setting

Severnvale served roughly 620,000 people across a Midlands city and its rural hinterland, running two hospital sites and 840 inpatient beds on an annual operating budget of GBP 620 million. It was the designated major-trauma and emergency-surge centre for the region: when something went wrong across the area, the ambulances came to Severnvale.

The trust ran with almost no slack. Occupancy at 18:40 stood at 94%, leaving about 50 free beds across both sites, and a normal day brought about 350 people through the Emergency Department. The culture was conventionally hierarchical: decisions travelled up to the executive and came back down. Until that evening, the executive had always been the best-informed group in the building.

## The Situation

By 19:30 the ambulance service had firmed up its estimate: about 560 Emergency Department attendances in the first 24 hours, against the usual 350 (see **Exhibit 1**). Reports reaching the executive cell did not agree: the Emergency Department, ambulance command, the information-technology team and the paper ward sit-reps each described a different hospital.

Four things could be moved, and each was finite (see **Exhibit 2**): six further theatres and resuscitation bays, 40 staff who could be pulled from recovery, day-case and community services, 12 critical-care beds to ration, and a GBP 1.2 million contingency for agency shifts, patient transfers and mutual aid. Every line was over-subscribed within the hour.

The regional major-incident protocol required each trust to log three indicators, which regional command would read at hour 48. **Flow stability** was the share of arriving patients moving through the hospital rather than stopping inside it; the review required at least 70%, and Severnvale logged 30%. **Frontline trust**, scored 0 to 100 from staff-side reporting, measured whether tired staff felt safe to act on their own judgement; it stood at 25. **Command coordination**, also 0 to 100, measured whether the two sites worked to one intent instead of solving the same problem twice; it stood at 20. The log also carried what the trust had committed — contingency spent, staff redeployed — and what it had held back.

Arrivals were entering a hospital with about 50 free beds and no working view of them.

## What Adeyemi Controlled

**The first six hours.** She had to settle how the trust would know what was happening. One option was a single paper board at the executive cell. Another was runners and site huddles, taking verbal reports from the charge nurses and registrars standing in the departments. She could also commit to no picture until the record system returned, or work from the occupancy figures logged just before the outage. She had to name the largest risk of the next six hours: the thin Friday-night workforce giving out, exit block leaving arrivals nowhere to go, the press reporting the outage mid-incident, or hour 48 arriving without the documentation the regulator would want. And she had to set the share of urgent decisions she would hand to those standing in the departments rather than take herself, from none to all.

**The response pool.** She had to publish a rule for who got the next theatre, bed and pair of hands. First call, first served, on the argument that adjudication cost time she did not have. A written clinical-priority order to every site lead, naming a reserve she would not release, drafted while the phones were going. Escalation-led, capacity going wherever clinicians pressed hardest, because departments knew when they were in trouble. Or an even split across the two sites, needing no information the centre lacked. She then divided the 40 redeployable staff between the trauma surge in resuscitation and theatres, decontamination and the Emergency Department front door, and urgent non-incident care — the heart attacks and strokes that arrived regardless. Whatever she did not deploy stayed in reserve. And she set how much of the GBP 1.2 million to commit in the first six hours, from nothing to all.

**The next 24 hours.** She had to place the decision rights. Every bed and theatre call could route up to the executive cell. Operational calls could sit with ward, Emergency Department, theatre and site leaders working inside a stated intent. Or strategic calls could stay central while operational calls went to the edge. She had to choose how the two sites stayed joined without the record system: the cell telephoning wards as it needed them, a named liaison at each site on a fixed sit-rep timetable feeding one shared picture, or each site running its own response with no shared cadence. And she had to decide how sharp the single line of intent would be, from a general statement of priorities to a sentence a tired charge nurse could act on at three in the morning.

**Hour 48.** She had to write to the frontline, or not. One draft was a calming note: the situation was in hand, instructions would follow. A second named the threat plainly, said she trusted their judgement inside the stated intent, and said they were not on their own. A third told staff to use their own judgement, with no frame attached. The fourth was no note that night, the words going into the briefing instead. She had to decide what the response was being run for — the ground, the review, or both in parallel — and what sat at the centre of her three-minute briefing: the headline numbers, how the leadership model, allocation and message fitted together with live flow evidence, or what the ransomware had made impossible. And she had to decide how hard to press her own position when regional command challenged it.

## Closing

At 19:45 Adeyemi wrote three figures on the whiteboard in the executive cell — 30, 25, 20 — and beside them, 18:40 Sunday. The Emergency Department had taken 40 arrivals in twenty-four minutes. No one could say when the record system would return. She had forty staff, GBP 1.2 million, six bays and twelve critical-care beds. The three figures on the board were what regional command would read at 18:40 on Sunday.
