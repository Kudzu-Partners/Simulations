# Forgiatura Emiliana: Winning Hearts

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1876 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1876 — *Winning Hearts* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 20 April 2026, Silvia Bonomi's first morning as Chief Executive of Forgiatura Emiliana S.p.A. opened with a single number on a slide. The firm's organizational identification index — a pulse-survey reading of how strongly its 1,850 employees still felt the company belonged to them — had fallen to 31 out of 100, against a sector norm of 60 and the 74 the same survey had recorded in 2021. Bonomi was the third chief executive the private-equity owners had installed in two years, and the fund's mandate was blunt: restore the earnings line without finishing off what was left of the workforce's goodwill. She had 45 days before a union ballot on strike action closed, and 24 months before the board expected an EBIT margin of 8%.

Her toolkit was narrow by design. She could not raise wages across the board or promise her way out of two years of mistrust; her only instruments were symbols, words, and a €6 million people-and-engagement envelope to commit over the months ahead, against a workforce primed to read every gesture for sincerity.

## The Protagonist

Bonomi, 52, had spent two decades in Italian industrial operations, most recently running a mid-sized automotive-components group through a change of ownership without a strike or a mass departure of skilled staff. The fund's partners had recruited her for that record, not for a standard cost-cutting playbook. Her two predecessors had each lasted under a year, and the board had been explicit that a third departure inside three years would be read, inside and outside the company, as confirmation that Forgiatura Emiliana could no longer be led. Her compensation and the fund's patience were tied to the same twenty-four-month clock.

## The Decision

Bonomi had to decide, in sequence, how to diagnose what had actually broken in the workforce's relationship with the company; what story to tell about the two years since the sale and how visibly to carry it in person; how to spend the engagement envelope on signals that reconnected individual employees to the collective; and what commitment to take into a works-council meeting before the strike ballot closed — all while defending to the board why belonging should recover before earnings did, not the other way round.

## The Company and its Setting

Forgiatura Emiliana S.p.A. was a precision metal-forging and components manufacturer in Modena, in Italy's Emilia-Romagna "Motor Valley," with FY2025 revenue of €310 million and an EBIT margin that had slipped to 4.1%. For six decades the company had been known internally as "la famiglia" — a paternalistic, family-owned employer with long tenures and a workforce that spoke of the place with genuine pride. Two years earlier a private-equity fund had bought the company, cut costs, carried out two rounds of layoffs totaling 220 jobs, frozen the historic profit-sharing scheme the family had run for decades, and gone through two chief executives in as many years. The pride the workforce once took in the place had curdled into something closer to cynicism.

## The Situation

The identification index of 31 was itself a composite of three separate readings the survey tracked. One, scored as Cognitive ID and standing at 28, asked whether an employee could still say clearly what the firm stood for and where they fit inside it. The other, Affective ID, at 22, asked whether they still felt pride, warmth or connection to the place and the people in it. A third reading, Credibility, at 20, asked a blunter question: did a workforce primed for cynicism actually believe what the CEO said and did. All three sat well below the sector norm and the company's own 2021 level.

The consequences already showed in the numbers. Voluntary attrition among the most skilled forgers ran at 19% a year, costing an estimated €4.2 million annually in replacement effort. Employees' willingness to go beyond the minimum of the job — discretionary effort — was down 40%. A lean-production system central to the earnings plan sat stuck at 22% adoption, treated on the shop floor as something done to the workforce rather than with it. A trade union had scheduled a strike ballot for 45 days out; a walkout timed to peak customer demand was estimated to cost roughly €1.2 million a week. If none of this moved, the trajectory was easy to state: attrition would keep draining the firm's most skilled people, the earnings plan would stay stuck behind an unadopted lean programme, and the ballot would close with the board watching a third chief executive preside over the same numbers as the first two (Exhibit 1).

## What Bonomi Controlled

Her first task carried no price tag. She had to decide which identification reading had actually broken — both together; only the sense of belonging and shared identity; only the pride and emotional attachment; or neither, on the view that the dip was an ordinary post-sale adjustment that would correct itself — and, separately, whether the root cause was a broken set of commitments to employees (the frozen profit-share, the layoffs, a sense that the new owners were extracting value rather than investing), softer demand across the automotive market leaving people anxious about their jobs, or resistance among longer-tenured staff to any new ownership. On a scale from 0 to 100%, she also had to set how directly to name the 220 jobs and the frozen profit-share when she spoke to the workforce.

Every choice after that carried a cost against the €6 million envelope (Exhibit 2). She first had to settle the story she would tell about the two years since the sale: naming the layoffs and the frozen profit-share directly, connecting the moment to the firm's "la famiglia" history, and describing a path forward; framing the change of ownership as a fresh start centered on the opportunities ahead; or focusing the message solely on future plans. Alongside that story came how to show up to the workforce in person: a listening tour of both plants over several weeks, meeting people by name on the shop floor; a single broadcast town hall from headquarters, with questions submitted in advance; or an all-staff video message and an intranet post. On the stalled lean programme she could co-design a renamed version, "Officina Nostra," together with the employee council and shop-floor crews; rename the programme and relaunch it on a plan set by plant leadership; or keep its existing name, require adoption, and track progress through periodic reviews.

On the profit-share itself — the central broken promise — she could reinstate it partially against a shared trigger tied to the turnaround, reinstate it fully and immediately alongside a one-time cash payment to every employee, or leave it frozen and revisit the question once EBIT recovered. On recognition, she could fund a craft-mastery scheme honouring long-tenured forgers, fund individual cash incentives tied to personal output, or fund no recognition scheme that round. And she could commit any amount from nothing up to €1.5 million to equipping front-line supervisors, the people through whom most employees actually experienced the company day to day.

Finally, with the ballot approaching, she had to choose what to bring into the works council: a package built around a trigger-linked profit-share and a jointly agreed process for handling any future layoffs; a broader package combining immediate full reinstatement of the profit-share, a guarantee against further restructuring, and a 5% pay increase; or no new commitment, declining to negotiate further. She also had to tell the board, one of three ways, how identification and EBIT related: that belonging would be rebuilt first, with retention, effort and adoption following, and ask the board to hold that sequence; that both identification and EBIT could be delivered together inside six months; or that EBIT should be pursued immediately, through further cuts, with engagement addressed afterward. A final figure, from 0 to 100, captured how firmly she was prepared to defend that position under board pressure.

## Closing

By the end of her first week, Bonomi had logged a diagnosis, a narrative, and a first slice of the engagement envelope — with the larger reward decisions, the works-council commitment, and the conversation with the board about sequencing still ahead of her. Forty-five days remained before the ballot closed. Twenty-four months remained on the board's clock. Somewhere between the two sat the question of what, exactly, would make 1,850 people believe the company was theirs again.
