# Foyer Connecté: The Cold-Snap Backlash

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1828 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1828 — *Co-Creation or Co-Destruction* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 9 March 2026, Camille Rousseau read the overnight complaint log at Foyer Connecté SAS, then read it again. A February cold snap had driven household energy use up across the country, and thousands of subscribers had opened bills higher than the app had promised to prevent. The complaints had spread across social media before dawn, and a newspaper that morning ran the story under the headline "L'appli qui ne fait pas d'économies" — the app that saves nothing. In four weeks Rousseau would stand in front of the board and explain how the service-design team intended to redesign the customer journey rather than patch it. The next cold snap, and the bill cycle that would follow it, was roughly sixty days out.

## The Protagonist

Camille Rousseau, in her early forties, led service design at Foyer Connecté, the Lille-based subscription that paired a home-energy app with a coaching service meant to help households cut their bills. She had spent several years building the customer-operations side of the business after moving over from a regional utility's call-centre operation, and had argued for the coaching model when the company chose how to differentiate its subscription from a plain savings app. Her standing with the board rested on two figures she did not control alone — the rate at which subscribers cancelled each month, and how satisfied the ones who stayed said they were — and both had just moved the wrong way at once.

## The Decision

Rousseau had to decide, over the coming weeks and with a small cross-functional team, how to redesign the customer journey rather than patch it. That meant first agreeing where in the journey value had actually collapsed and whose actions had caused it; then choosing which points of contact to rebuild, inside a budget finance controlled; then writing down what the company would guarantee subscribers and what it expected of them in return, alongside a decision on a cluster of subscribers who had found a way to trigger a bill credit without earning it; and finally, with the next cold snap still ahead, sequencing what got fixed first and taking a case to a board that did not agree with itself.

## The Company and its Setting

Foyer Connecté SAS sold a subscription combining an app and in-home coaching, priced to help households cut their energy bills; it had grown to 240,000 subscribers at an average of €18 a month each, generating roughly €51.8 million a year in revenue at a contribution margin of 34% before the cost of running customer operations. Its promise to subscribers was direct: follow the app's guidance and the bill would fall. But the saving was never something Foyer Connecté could simply deliver — it appeared only when a household did its part: setting an honest occupancy schedule, sharing when the home was actually in use, and acting on the coaching messages it received. When that exchange worked, subscribers saved money and stayed; when it did not, bills came in higher than promised and the household blamed the company that had sold it the promise.

## The Situation

The cold snap had exposed how often that exchange was failing. Rousseau's team tracked three scores on a common 0-to-100 scale: one for how much value the journey was creating through the interaction, standing at 30 that morning; a mirror score for how much value was being actively destroyed at specific points of contact, where lower was better and which stood at 58; and a score for subscriber satisfaction and trust, at 34. Underneath those sat the harder numbers. Monthly cancellations had jumped from a normal 1.8% to 3.4%; held at that rate for a year, the company's own modelling put roughly 33,600 additional subscribers, and about €7.3 million of annual revenue, at risk (see Exhibit 1). A diagnostic run after the backlash found that 62% of the households who had complained had never finished the basic set-up steps — an occupancy schedule, a savings goal — and 28% described the coaching messages they received as preachy or irrelevant. Separately, roughly 4,000 subscribers had found a way to trigger the app's savings-guarantee credit, worth €22 each, without changing their behaviour; the payout had reached about €88,000 for the current cycle and was climbing as word spread. Finance had capped new spending on the redesign at €2.0 million and would not accept a contribution margin below 30%, four points under where the business stood that morning. If the four weeks before the board review passed without resolving any of this, cancellations would stay near double their normal rate, the misuse payout would keep growing, and the business would meet the next cold snap in roughly the shape that had just failed the last one.

## What Rousseau's Team Controlled

The team's first task was diagnostic: naming which two points in the journey concentrated the collapse — onboarding, where most of the unfinished set-ups sat; the coaching cadence, where the "preachy" complaints clustered; the acquisition marketing that had first sold the promise; or the pattern of subscribers simply leaving at contract renewal — and then deciding whether responsibility sat with Foyer Connecté's own process, with subscribers who had not shared what the app needed, with the exchange between the two, or with neither, on the view that the pattern was ordinary churn with no particular point to fix. A separate dial let the team set how strongly it treated the whole episode as a design problem rather than a pricing one.

The redesign choices that followed each carried a price tag (see Exhibit 2). Onboarding could stay as it was for no cost, move to a guided set-up capturing most of the occupancy data with less friction for €350,000, or require a full schedule before activation for €300,000. The sign-up promise could stay untouched for nothing, reset "comfort versus savings" for €150,000, or gain three new app features and a louder savings claim for €600,000. Coaching could stay generic for €50,000, move to usage-based personalisation for €450,000, or add sixty human coaches calling every household for €1,600,000. A further, optional choice was a subscriber community for peers to help each other — closed at no cost, or funded at any level, with roughly €80,000 the point at which it would be properly moderated.

On the misuse cluster, the choices ran from clawing back credits and tightening terms for all 240,000 subscribers, to redesigning the credit trigger so the exploit stopped working, to converting the group into rewarded power-users, to leaving the €88,000 exposure alone — each paired with a fix budget of the team's choosing, and a dial for how much weight to place on the message sent to the 236,000 subscribers who had gamed nothing. The team also had to put the arrangement with subscribers in writing — what the firm would guarantee, what it would ask in return, and what happened if either side fell short — choosing among one setting the same obligations for both firm and subscriber; one promising the outcome regardless of the subscriber's input; one placing the burden of proof on the subscriber first; or leaving the arrangement unwritten, as it already was.

Last, with the next cold snap still ahead, the team would decide which subscribers to re-engage first, whether to pursue new subscribers instead, or hold every change until after the board met; how to adjust onboarding friction — easing it by deferring non-essential steps, removing the schedule requirement entirely, or requiring it more strictly; and which pitch to bring the board — a retention discount for everyone, dropping the guarantee entirely, or the redesigned touchpoints and the written agreement instead — plus a final dial for how forcefully to press it.

## Closing

Rousseau closed the complaint log, opened a blank page, and wrote the two touchpoints she suspected were failing across the top. She had four weeks before the board meeting and about sixty days before the next bill cycle would test whatever she decided. The €2.0 million budget and the 30% margin floor were not going to move. Everything else was still hers to design.
