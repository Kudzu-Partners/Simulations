# Helvetia Climatec AG: The Migrating Value

**Case Code:** STR-2026-2012 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2012 — *Convergence Play* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Tuesday, 5 May 2026, Nadja Brunner read the announcement a second time before she carried it up to the executive floor in Zug. A global consumer-technology platform had launched a smart-energy operating system: a single application that connected any heating unit, any electric-vehicle charger and any solar inverter in a building and optimised them against dynamic electricity tariffs. The company's own phrase for the product was that it would "own the home's energy brain." It was hardware-agnostic by design. It did not care who had manufactured the box. Within four days, two Swiss utilities confirmed they would bundle it with their tariffs.

Brunner had until 31 July to tell that platform whether Helvetia Climatec AG wanted one of the founding-partner places in its developer programme, and until the board meeting of 3 September to present the plan that decision belonged to. She was Head of Strategy of a company that had sold physical equipment, and only physical equipment, for fifty-five years.

## The Protagonist

Nadja Brunner, 44, had been Head of Strategy at Helvetia Climatec since 2022. She joined the company in 2014 to run product planning for the heat-pump range, after six years at a Zurich advisory firm covering industrial goods. Three strategy cycles had passed through her office: one on plant capacity in Aargau, one on installer coverage in Romandy and Ticino, one on the heat-pump roadmap. Each had asked what the company should build and where it should sell it. None had asked what the company charged for, or who held the customer after the equipment was fitted.

## The Decision

Brunner had to decide where Helvetia Climatec would earn its money once the equipment it manufactured became one component inside somebody else's system — and to hold that position through four cycles of commitment: a diagnosis of where the money was moving, a redesign of the commercial model, the choice of a partner, and the reallocation of the research budget that paid for all of it.

## The Company and its Setting

Helvetia Climatec AG was founded in 1971, headquartered in Zug, with production in Aargau. It made premium boilers, heat pumps and ventilation units. Revenue in 2025 was CHF 480 million at an 11% EBIT margin, or CHF 53 million (see **Exhibit 1**). The company employed 1,650 people and held roughly 18% of the Swiss premium climate-equipment market. It reached that market through about 1,200 independent installers, who specified the equipment, fitted it and returned to service it. Research and development ran at CHF 38 million a year, close to 8% of revenue, and had been spent almost entirely on mechanical and thermal engineering.

Two hundred and forty thousand of its units were active in Swiss homes and buildings. Across all 240,000, Helvetia Climatec booked CHF 2 million of recurring revenue a year.

## The Situation

Operating systems, dynamic tariffs, demand-response contracts with the grid, predictive maintenance and building-management software formed a layer of the Swiss market above the equipment itself. The CFO's team put Helvetia Climatec's share of that layer at about 4%. The platform that had announced on 5 May had 50 million users worldwide and spent on the layer, each year, roughly ten times Helvetia Climatec's entire research budget.

Underneath, the equipment business was moving the other way. Analysts covering the sector had published a commoditisation scenario in which the premium hardware EBIT margin fell from 11% toward 6–7% within three years as the box became an interchangeable component. On CHF 480 million of revenue that scenario removed roughly CHF 20–24 million of EBIT a year. Corporate development kept an index of the company's partner position — the quality of its partners, its bargaining power inside those relationships and its ability to absorb a partner walking away — scored from 0 to 100. It stood at 30. The same team rated the risk of the equipment becoming an unbranded endpoint inside another company's system as high.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic ran as follows. The 240,000 installed units would go on producing CHF 2 million a year. The hardware margin would compress toward 6–7%, taking CHF 20–24 million of the CHF 53 million EBIT with it. The energy-and-data layer would keep growing and would accrue to whoever owned the application in front of the customer.

Two constraints framed everything Brunner could propose. The board had refused to raise the CHF 38 million research budget; the technology lead had costed a software, connectivity and energy-services programme at about CHF 14 million a year, which meant any such programme had to be paid for out of hardware projects already running. And in late May one of the two utilities told Helvetia Climatec's commercial director that it was evaluating an exclusive arrangement with the platform for its own customers.

The 1,200 installers sat across the second half of the problem. They earned their living selling and fitting boxes, and a subscription sold directly by the manufacturer took the customer relationship away from them. They were also the only distribution the company had, and the only party in Switzerland that visited the installed base.

## What Brunner Controlled

Brunner committed in four cycles, and recorded after each one the four numbers the board had asked for: the share of the energy-and-data layer Helvetia Climatec captured, group EBIT in Swiss francs, annual recurring revenue in Swiss francs, and the partner-position index — together with the risk rating and a single line describing the move she had made.

In the first cycle she stated where in the industry the money was actually moving; which Helvetia Climatec asset — the brand, the installed base and installer relationships, or the thermal and mechanical patents — she would build the recapture on; what the EBIT exposure came to if the box commoditised and she did nothing; and, as a percentage, how much urgency she attached to the announcement.

In the second she redesigned the commercial model: whether to build a better box and keep the one-time sale, to set the hardware aside and compete as an energy-software business, or to sell hardware plus a subscription on the installed base covering energy optimisation and predictive maintenance. She decided in the same cycle how to treat the 1,200 installers — bypass them and go direct, say nothing and launch, or design the service with them as its channel — and set, as a percentage, how hard to push the recurring-revenue ramp against the eroding hardware margin.

In the third she picked the partner arrangement before the founding-partner window closed (see **Exhibit 2**); set her contingency for the utility going exclusive — carry on as though it would not happen, concede the layer on that utility's grid, or spread the company across several partners and tie the installed base to itself directly; and stated her reading of the lock-in: who, under her chosen arrangement, would own the customer, the usage data and the recurring revenue.

In the fourth she split the CHF 38 million between software, connectivity and energy-services research on one side and legacy hardware research on the other, with the two sides required to sum to CHF 38 million or less; chose what her three-minute defence to the board would lead with — the software story, the group EBIT arithmetic, or reassurance that the hardware business was untouched; and set, as a percentage, the conviction with which she held her own position.

## Closing

On the evening of 5 May, Brunner wrote four figures on a single sheet: 4%, CHF 53 million, CHF 2 million, 30. Under them she wrote the two dates, 31 July and 3 September, and the budget line, CHF 38 million. The rest of the sheet was empty.
