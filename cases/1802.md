# Atlantique Global Business School: One Programme, Five Campuses

On the afternoon of September 21, 2026, Élise Cortier left the Executive Committee room at Atlantique Global Business School's Lille campus holding the mandate for a new MSc in Global Sustainable Management: launch in September 2027, on at least three of AGBS's five campuses, under one set of learning outcomes and the school's signature International Week. The Dean had already told the board, and two corporate partners, it would happen. Cortier, AGBS's Program Portfolio Director, had sixteen weeks to bring a design through the school's internal accreditation-readiness review, and twelve months to put it in front of students.

## The Protagonist

Cortier, 44, had run AGBS's program portfolio office since 2022, after eight years in the school's international relations and accreditation unit, where she had guided two of the five campuses through their first EQUIS site visits. She reported to Dean Henri Vasseur and sat, without a vote, on the Executive Committee. Her mandate was narrow and permanent: no programme launched, merged, or closed without her sign-off on how it fit the rest of the portfolio. She had watched a previous multi-campus launch drift into five different syllabi before an external reviewer asked what was actually shared across them. This launch sat on the board's public calendar, with her name attached to the design.

## The Decision

Cortier's task was to architect one programme five campuses could deliver as a single brand, not five local versions of a shared name — in four stages. First, fix where the programme would live and which pressure the rest of the design would have to bend around. Second, decide how much of the programme would be identical everywhere and how much would flex by market. Third, staff it and design its signature week without breaking the faculty or the budget. Fourth, defend the finished design to the Dean and an internal EQUIS/AACSB readiness panel before the sixteen-week gate closed. Every stage would be judged against the same four numbers: how coherent the design looked to an accreditor, how many students actually enrolled net of overlap with AGBS's other programmes, how hard the core faculty were being worked, and how much of the launch budget remained.

## The Company and its Setting

AGBS, founded in 1989 and headquartered in Lille, held all three accreditations — EQUIS, AACSB and AMBA — and ran on an annual operating budget of €142 million across 11,000 students and five campuses: Lille, Paris, Sophia Antipolis, Raleigh, and Suzhou. It offered 38 programmes from undergraduate to executive level and competed on one promise: a globally consistent, locally relevant experience, anchored by an International Week in which cohorts from every campus converged, in person or online, around a shared business challenge. That promise was the product AGBS sold. A multi-campus programme that campuses quietly turned into five different courses would not just fail; it would undercut the claim AGBS made for every other programme it ran.

## The Situation

Four numbers described where the new MSc stood before Cortier made a single decision (Exhibit 1). A program-coherence score — how much of the design an accreditor would recognize as one shared architecture rather than a label over five local courses — sat at 45 out of 100, against a 75-point floor AGBS treated as the minimum an accreditation panel would accept. Faculty: only six qualified core professors existed for a subject area needing roughly nine full-time-equivalent positions across three campuses, and the utilization of those six was already at 88%, the level AGBS's own faculty-planning data flagged as the edge of burnout, before a single hour of the new programme had been assigned. Money: the launch carried a hard cap of €2.4 million for design, hiring, the International Week and marketing combined, and the most direct way to run a multi-campus programme — flying faculty to teach at every campus — had already been costed at €1.1 million alone. Enrolment: break-even at the agreed tuition of €24,000 a year required 120 students in year one; unpositioned demand across the campuses most likely to lead the launch was estimated at 95, a figure that took no account of how many of those students might otherwise have enrolled in AGBS's existing Master in Management. The International Week itself, the experience that most distinguished a multi-campus AGBS degree from a single-site one, would cost €480,000 — a fifth of the entire budget — if all 120 students converged on one campus (Exhibit 2). If Cortier changed nothing, AGBS would carry a 45-point coherence score, six already-stretched faculty, and 95 students into an accreditation review built to reject all three.

## What Cortier Controlled

The design unfolded in four rounds. First, Cortier chose which three of the five campuses would lead the launch: Lille, Paris and Suzhou, adding a distinct Asian market to the two French sites; Lille, Paris and Sophia Antipolis, three French campuses on a single market but the simplest to coordinate; Lille, Raleigh and Suzhou, spanning three continents and three accreditation regimes; or all five campuses from day one. In the same round she named the single pressure the rest of the design would have to bend around — faculty scarcity, program coherence, the budget cap, or no binding constraint — and set how rigorously the design would account for the new programme drawing students away from AGBS's existing Master in Management, on a scale from barely at all to fully built into the enrolment math.

Second, Cortier designed the architecture: what share of the programme's credits, from 0 to 100%, would be common to every campus, with the rest left for local adaptation; whether the assessment an accreditor would audit for equivalence should be a shared spine of common rubrics and a capstone with locally chosen cases, an identical exam sat by every student on every campus, or an assessment each campus set for itself; and how to position the programme to lift demand from 95 toward 120 — through a globally consistent core paired with locally relevant cases and partners, through the weight of the triple-crown brand and the International Week, or by cutting tuition to €18,000 to build volume across every campus at once.

Third, Cortier covered the nine-FTE teaching requirement from four sources, each with its own cost: her six existing core faculty, at no incremental cost but rising utilization the more of them she assigned; new faculty hired at €220,000 per full-time position, sustainable once in place but slow to arrive; visiting professors at €90,000 per position, flexible but with weaker continuity year to year; and shared online delivery at €40,000 per position, the cheapest way to add coverage. In the same round she chose and costed the International Week format: a hybrid of virtual cross-campus weeks plus one residential core at €260,000; a hub-and-spoke design routing regional cohorts through a shared challenge at €180,000; full residential convergence of all 120 students at €480,000; or a fully virtual week with no residential component at €40,000.

Finally, Cortier brought the design to the Dean and the internal readiness panel. She chose whether to build the accreditation case on shared learning outcomes, assessment-equivalence evidence and campus-by-campus faculty-sufficiency data; on a narrative built around the brand and the International Week; or on the headline coherence number against the accreditation floor. She decided whether to add campuses four and five on a staged two-year timeline backed by a faculty-hiring plan, to bring all five online within the first year, or to hold the programme at three campuses indefinitely. And when the Dean and panel asked directly whether this was one coherent, financed, deliverable programme, she chose what to tell them: a full account of the design with its evidence and its remaining gaps, a projection of 130 students enrolled across every campus with staffing questions left for later, or a request to approve now and close the gaps after launch — and set, on her own scale, how firmly to defend whichever answer she gave.

## Closing

The accreditation clock had started at zero that afternoon. Sixteen weeks from now, a panel would ask whether AGBS had built one programme or five. Cortier had four rounds of decisions to make before then, and no way yet to know which ones the panel would remember.
