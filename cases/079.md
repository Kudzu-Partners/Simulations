## Abstract

MiEmpresa was, in the case universe, a founder-run fashion-accessories micro-business based out of the founder's kitchen-table workspace in the residential north side of the capital. On Thursday, January 8, 2026, the business carried USD 4,200 in starting cash from the founder's personal savings, zero inventory, a production cost of USD 8 per unit from a vertically integrated supplier-and-sewing-contractor arrangement, no employees, and no brand recognition beyond a 420-follower Instagram account. Lucía Moreno, the founder, had registered the business on December 18, 2025 after eighteen months of part-time prototyping alongside a full-time job in retail merchandising. The starting position gave her four months of runway before the savings base would run down; the question was whether four months of disciplined small decisions would convert the starting cash into a cash-positive operating foundation or into a lesson. The case opens at 9:12 p.m. on Thursday, January 8, 2026, at her kitchen table, as Moreno finalizes the Month-1 operating commitment for the Friday January 9 production-and-marketing launch.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the Month-1 production-versus-demand-learning trade-off at 80, 120, or 180 units, and diagnose when each dominates on a four-month cumulative cash outcome.
2. Analyze the Month-1 pricing decision across USD 22, USD 28, and USD 34 price points, and recommend a posture consistent with the founder's zero-brand-recognition starting position and the four-month cash runway.
3. Diagnose the Month-3 hiring trigger — the point at which founder-time constraint becomes the binding bottleneck — and design a part-time-packer commitment that pays for itself inside the first month.
4. Design a Month-4 pricing decision that tests a USD 2 bump against the founder's story-based positioning without triggering demand attrition.
5. Recommend a complete Month-1 commitment and identify the mid-cycle decision most correlated with the four-month composite outcome.

On the evening of **Thursday, January 8, 2026**, **Lucía Moreno** sat at her kitchen table at 9:12 p.m. with a spreadsheet, a notebook, and a cup of tea going cold beside a carton of three-color canvas tote handles. The Month-1 operating-commitment worksheet on her laptop carried three columns — Production: 80 / 120 / 180 units; Price: USD 22 / USD 28 / USD 34; Marketing: USD 400 digital and USD 300 traditional — and a single orange cell at the bottom flagging the Friday January 9 production-and-marketing launch. Twelve hours separated her from the decision.

---

## The Protagonist

Lucía Moreno, 29, had spent the three years after her 2022 undergraduate-degree completion in retail merchandising at a mid-market national apparel retailer, where she had, across 2023–2024, led the accessories-category refresh that produced a 14% category sales uplift. A graduate of the Universidad Nacional with a degree in industrial design and a one-year intensive in textile pattern-making at a local technical institute, she had, across 2024–2025, prototyped a twelve-SKU accessories concept on nights and weekends and had, in November 2025, resigned from her retail job to launch MiEmpresa full-time. Her starting savings of USD 4,200 represented fourteen months of accumulated side-venture proceeds and a lump-sum from a family-gift-loan her mother had converted into equity at no interest. Her notebook carried, on its first page, a single handwritten instruction to herself: "Stay solvent. Grow second."

---

## The Decision

Moreno had to commit, by 6:00 a.m. on Friday, January 9, the Month-1 operating commitment that would set the four-month operating rhythm:

1. **Production Volume** — whether to produce 80 units (conservative, preserves USD 3,560 cash cushion), 120 units (moderate, commits USD 960 to inventory), or 180 units (stretch, commits USD 1,440 to inventory).
2. **Price Point** — whether to price at USD 22 (minimum-viable margin, discoverable price), USD 28 (mid-range, positions against mid-market direct-to-consumer peers), or USD 34 (aspirational, tests the story-first positioning).
3. **Marketing Split and Hiring Posture** — whether to split the Month-1 marketing budget across USD 400 digital (Instagram-led) and USD 300 traditional (local weekend-market booth), or to concentrate on a single channel; and whether to defer any hiring to Month-2 or later.

The three sub-decisions would interact and would compound across four monthly rounds. A Month-1 commitment that produced 120 units, priced at USD 28, split marketing USD 400 digital and USD 300 traditional, and deferred hiring produced, on Moreno's spreadsheet, a Month-1 end-of-period cash of USD 5,056, a margin of 56%, and a demand-signal of 98 units sold with 22 units in inventory — enough signal to scale a Month-2 production stretch without overcommitting cash.

---

## Company Background

MiEmpresa had been conceptualized by Moreno in August 2024 as a complement to her retail-merchandising day job and had, across eighteen months of part-time prototyping, narrowed the initial twenty-three-prototype portfolio to twelve SKUs across three product families — canvas tote bags (four SKUs), fabric-stitched card wallets (four SKUs), and keychain-and-small-accessory combinations (four SKUs). The launch focus for Month-1 was a single anchor SKU — a three-color structured canvas tote with a signature contrast-stitched handle — priced to test the USD 22 to USD 34 range.

The production arrangement at the case opening was a three-party vertically integrated supply chain: a local canvas-and-fabric supplier (fabric USD 3.20 per unit), a neighborhood sewing contractor (labor USD 3.60 per unit), and a small-hardware packaging supplier (hardware and packaging USD 1.20 per unit), yielding a total per-unit cost of USD 8.00. The arrangement could scale to approximately 240 units per month without contract renegotiation and carried a forty-eight-hour production-cycle turnaround from fabric-cut to packaged-finished-good. Moreno herself was, at the case opening, the business's sole operator — responsible for design, procurement, inventory, marketing, customer service, and packaging.

The fashion-accessories direct-to-consumer landscape in Moreno's catchment carried three competitive archetypes. A group of low-priced Instagram-led peers at USD 14–USD 22 price points competed on volume and paid-social ad-buying. A mid-market cluster at USD 28–USD 42 competed on design and material story. A small premium boutique cluster at USD 60+ competed on brand and retail placement.

## Industry and Market Context

The LATAM direct-to-consumer fashion-accessories landscape in 2026 was characterized by three structural features the Month-1 decision sat inside. First, the post-2022 Instagram-led micro-brand cycle had, in the Latin American capital markets, matured to a point where paid-social customer-acquisition cost had risen above USD 9 per first-purchase customer for the sub-USD-30 price segment, making story-first and community-led acquisition models — weekend-market booths, in-person events, and founder-authentic content — more capital-efficient than paid-social for first-year founders. Second, the retail weekend-market scene in the capital had, since 2023, grown to include thirty-four documented weekend-fair venues with average footfall of 2,400 visitors per venue per day — a channel that favored SKU-presence, story-telling, and direct-customer-conversation. Third, the first-year-founder survival pattern across a 2020–2024 sample of eighty-one tracked micro-businesses had correlated, with a 0.41 correlation coefficient, with founder cash-discipline at Month-1 and Month-2 — specifically, Month-1 end-of-period cash at or above 110% of starting cash predicted Month-12 survival at 78% against 34% for founders whose Month-1 ended below starting cash.

Empirically, the post-2018 first-founder research had two robust findings the Month-1 commitment had to respect. First, a production-volume-to-expected-demand ratio between 105% and 125% had, across a 2020–2024 sample of fifty-two first-year product micro-businesses, produced first-year survival outcomes 2.8 times higher than either undershooting (below 95%) or overshooting (above 150%) — the signal value of the Month-1 demand residual was the most valuable output of the first period. Second, first-founder pricing decisions that anchored on the mid-range of the competitive-positioning band, rather than on the lowest competitor price, had produced twelve-month margins 14 percentage points higher with demand-attrition cost averaging 2–4% — a net-positive trade on a cash-discipline criterion.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Moreno had, across November–December 2025, completed two pilot weekend-fair appearances at the Mercado de los Sábados venue, where she had sold 38 units of her anchor SKU at USD 28 across the two weekends. The pilot sales had, in her notebook, produced the core working assumption that the USD 28 price would convert — but the sample size was, she acknowledged, too small for certainty. Her mother, whose family-gift-loan-converted-to-equity had funded 34% of the starting cash, had, in a Christmas-Day conversation, flagged two concerns: the hiring question ("do not work alone past Month-3 if demand grows") and the pricing test ("do not stay at USD 28 if the story is selling"). The weekend-fair booth calendar for Month-1 carried four confirmed slots across January 11, 18, 25, and February 1.

### What Moreno knew

She had built three Month-1 commitments on the spreadsheet. A Conservative Commitment (80 units, USD 28 price, USD 700 marketing, no hiring) projected Month-1 cash ending at USD 4,640 with a demand-signal of 72 units sold and 8 units short — a stock-out-risking position. A Moderate Commitment (120 units, USD 28 price, USD 700 marketing, no hiring) projected Month-1 cash ending at USD 5,056 with a demand-signal of 98 units sold and 22 units in inventory — balanced. A Stretch Commitment (180 units, USD 28 price, USD 700 marketing, no hiring) projected Month-1 cash ending at USD 4,576 with a demand-signal of 98 units sold and 82 units in inventory — over-committed to production against a single-weekend signal.

### What she did not know

Whether the pilot USD 28 price would translate to the broader weekend-fair audience at scale (her two-weekend sample was 38 units, small); whether the paid-social USD 400 spend would produce above or below the per-capita USD 9 first-purchase cost benchmark on her catchment (her December test at USD 80 spend had produced 7 first purchases, implying USD 11.40 per acquisition); whether the sewing contractor could reliably deliver 120–180 units per cycle without quality degradation (her pilots had been 38 units each); and whether she herself could physically pack 120 units around the four weekend-fair slots without missing an order.

### What others were saying

**Her mother (a family-gift-loan equity holder)**: *"Start at one-twenty. Learn the number by the end of January. Do not go to one-eighty in month one; you do not know the number yet."*

**Her former retail-merchandising manager (an informal advisor)**: *"Price at twenty-eight. If the story works, test up in Month 3. Do not price-fight below twenty-two; you will be a commodity."*

**The sewing contractor**: *"I can do one-eighty if you order three days before. I would rather do one-twenty every month and build the rhythm."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Moreno had narrowed the space to three Month-1 commitments.

- **Option A — Conservative (80 units, USD 28, USD 700 marketing, no hire):** Stock-out-risking. Cash-protective. Small demand-signal.

- **Option B — Moderate (120 units, USD 28, USD 700 marketing split digital/traditional, no hire):** Matches mother's counsel. Balanced demand-signal. Preserves Month-2 flexibility. Four-month composite path 83.

- **Option C — Stretch (180 units, USD 28, USD 700 marketing, no hire):** Over-commits to production against a single-weekend pilot. Exposes cash to an inventory overhang.

Moreno's private view, written in her notebook just before 9:30 p.m., was that Option B was the right Month-1 commitment and that the single subsequent decision most correlated with the four-month composite was the Month-3 hiring trigger — specifically, the commitment to hire a part-time packer at USD 600/month when the founder-time constraint began to cost orders at the Saturday weekend-fair, combined with a two-dollar price-test to USD 30 at Month-3 on the back of month-two demand evidence.
