# École Lumière Business School: Repositioning Against Institut Concorde

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1838 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1838 — *Designing the Distinctive Offer* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Friday, 5 June 2026, Élise Vasseur, Dean of Programs at École Lumière Business School (ELBS) in Montpellier, read the announcement a colleague had forwarded from a rival's press conference: Institut Concorde, a larger and better-funded French business school, was launching a flagship "Sustainable Business & AI" master's degree backed by a €20 million investment, aggressive scholarships, and a global media campaign — aimed squarely at the ambitious, impact-minded applicants ELBS had spent years trying to attract. That afternoon, ELBS's board gave Vasseur ten weeks to bring back a credible repositioning plan for the school's program portfolio, before the autumn recruitment cycle opened, or it would cut programs and faculty lines on its own terms.

Vasseur had run ELBS's program portfolio since 2023, after eleven years split between the school's admissions office and its executive-education unit. She had built a reputation for careful stewardship rather than bold moves — the portfolio had grown steadily under her, by adding programs rather than sharpening them. Her performance was reviewed against enrolment, revenue, and the school's standing relative to named regional rivals; Concorde's launch put all three in question at once.

## The Protagonist

Vasseur, 44, held a doctorate in management from a Paris institution and had advised smaller professional-services firms before joining ELBS. Colleagues described her as data-literate and risk-averse, preferring a defensible plan over an ambitious-sounding one. She had inherited a portfolio nobody had built badly on purpose: each of ELBS's nine programs had a sponsor and a modest but real stream of applicants. Nothing in her mandate had ever asked her to decide which of them should stop.

## The Decision

Over the ten weeks, working with ELBS's finance, faculty-affairs, and admissions leads, Vasseur had to answer a sequence of linked questions: what ELBS actually had that Concorde could not quickly copy, how to spend a fixed repositioning budget across nine programs without simply spreading it thin, how to design and defend one flagship program against a live competitor move, and how to convince the board that whatever she built would still make money.

## The Company and its Setting

ELBS was a private, accredited business school founded in 1994, enrolling 2,800 students and generating €62 million in annual revenue across a Grande École master's, a full-time international MBA, a cluster of specialised master's programs, and a growing executive-education unit — nine programs in total. It operated on an 8% operating margin, roughly €5.0 million a year, with 140 core faculty of uneven depth across programs (see Exhibit 1).

The one asset that stood out in an independent brand study was a faculty-built specialism in sustainability and impact management, anchored by a research centre and a set of corporate partnerships; on a 100-point scale of perceived value to target employers, it was the only ELBS attribute to score above 70. Every other program scored in a narrow band close to its rivals. A fixed strategic-investment budget of €6 million had been set aside for the repositioning.

## The Situation

The same brand study placed ELBS's overall Differentiation Index at 42 out of 100, in a cluster where most rivals sat between 38 and 55; a position generally considered genuinely distinctive started at 70. A second measure, tracking how well ELBS's growth matched the specific segments it wanted to own rather than growth generally, stood at 30 out of 100. A third measure, gauging how well any position ELBS built would hold up against a much richer competitor, stood at 25 out of 100. Schools regarded as genuinely distinctive typically ran three or four programs, not nine.

If ELBS changed nothing, the board's own estimate was that Concorde's launch would cost the school roughly 12% of MBA and specialised-master applications within two recruitment cycles — about €5.4 million of tuition revenue, close to the school's entire annual operating surplus. Concorde's scholarships were already drawing interest from students ELBS had admitted for the coming year, and an accreditation renewal visit and a rankings cycle both fell within the following eighteen months.

## What Vasseur Controlled

First, Vasseur and her group had to name, among several ELBS assets, the one genuinely valuable, rare, and hard for a rival to copy — the sustainability specialism, the school's rankings position, its comparatively lower tuition, or the breadth of its nine-program catalogue. They also had to judge where Concorde was actually weak despite its budget — built faculty depth around sustainability, nowhere at all, brand reputation, or the depth of its own new AI offering — and set, as a percentage, how candidly the group would admit ELBS could no longer be all things to all applicants.

Next, with the €6 million fixed, Vasseur had to choose an overall shape for the spend: concentrating it on three programs built around ELBS's chosen capability, spreading it across four or five programs more lightly, dividing it evenly across all nine (about €667,000 each), or committing the whole sum to one new program mirroring Concorde's own artificial-intelligence angle. She had to decide, as a number between zero and nine, how many programs to discontinue or wind down, and split the €6 million across four uses: the flagship program's faculty, curriculum and partnerships; targeted brand and admissions spending in the segments ELBS wanted to own; a scholarship discount sized to match Concorde's; and funding to keep the remaining, less-differentiated programs from feeling cut. The four amounts had to add to exactly €6.0 million (see Exhibit 2).

Then a live complication arrived: Concorde added a tuition discount and an AI module to its launch, and separately approached ELBS's lead sustainability professor with an offer. Vasseur had to settle what position the flagship would claim — built on the research centre, corporate guarantees and faculty Concorde lacked; matching Concorde feature for feature and undercutting its tuition; claiming leadership in artificial intelligence and quantitative finance, outside ELBS's current faculty; or a solid, broadly appealing generalist degree. She had to choose which bundle of features to build — a corporate-partner guarantee with a research-based curriculum and named-faculty outcomes; a discount with a new brochure and logo; or an AI module layered onto the curriculum — and decide, separately, whether to retain the courted professor through a research-chair package, a modest raise, or by letting her go, backing that choice with a retention investment of up to €2 million from a distinct faculty-retention reserve, separate from the €6 million budget.

Finally, before presenting to the board chair and an external strategy advisor, Vasseur had to decide how the flagship would pay for itself — a defensible premium price combined with costs freed by discontinued programs, deep discounts chasing enrolment volume, or running at a loss hoping a rankings improvement fixed the finances later; the board had signalled that a flagship losing €2 million a year would not pass its test. She had to set the priority of an eighteen-month roadmap spanning the rankings cycle and the accreditation visit — a sequenced plan launching the flagship while protecting faculty and building an evidence file; a fast round of cuts leaving governance and accreditation for later; or a wait-and-see approach holding off until Concorde's launch had played out. She had to answer the board chair's question of whether the plan was both distinctive and financially sound — with a case built on evidence, a pledge to keep matching Concorde's spending until ELBS won the segment, or an appeal to distinctiveness paying for itself once students noticed — and state, as a percentage, her own conviction that owned capability beat out-spending the rival.

Throughout, four measures moved with every choice: the Differentiation Index starting at 42, segment-fit starting at 30, operating margin starting at €5.0 million and needing to stay above zero, and resilience starting at 25 — alongside a running tally of the €6 million budget actually committed.

## Closing

By nine that evening, Vasseur had the brand study, the portfolio P&L, and Concorde's own launch brief spread across her desk, next to a blank page headed with the nine program names. The board wanted its plan in ten weeks. She had €6 million, one asset that scored above 70, and a sheet still empty of any decision at all.
