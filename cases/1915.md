# Pennine Executive Learning: The NorthGrid Mandate

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1915 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1915 — *Designing the Programme* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 8 June 2026, Rhona Delaney read the NorthGrid Utilities request for proposal, and then read the costing her programme manager had run against it overnight. The request asked Pennine Executive Learning to make 120 senior leaders "ready to run a decarbonised, digitised business within 12 months." The costing, built the way Pennine had always built a flagship custom programme — content written for one client, senior faculty in the room — came to £2.6 million. NorthGrid had set the budget at £1.8 million.

NorthGrid wanted a decision in four weeks and the first cohort in a classroom ten weeks after the award. Delaney had those four weeks to put a design, a price and a launch date in front of NorthGrid's chief human resources officer and its procurement lead, against two other bidders.

## The Protagonist

Rhona Delaney, 44, had been Director of Custom Programmes at Pennine Executive Learning since 2022. She had spent eight years in the learning function of a manufacturer before moving to the provider side, and had built her standing at Pennine on mandates clients renewed rather than on mandates clients enjoyed. Her bonus moved with the contribution margin on custom work and the renewal rate on the accounts she led. Gareth Pryce, appointed managing director the previous year, had told the unit twice in six months that he would not approve a custom bid below 30% contribution margin, and had set a two-year target of 38%.

## The Decision

Delaney had to decide what Pennine would offer NorthGrid, at what price, delivered in what form, and by what date — and to defend that answer in a room with Miriam Cassidy, NorthGrid's chief human resources officer, and the group's procurement lead, four weeks later. The drafted design was £0.8 million above what NorthGrid had budgeted, and rested on a build that ran 16 to 18 weeks against a date ten weeks out.

## The Company and its Setting

Pennine Executive Learning was the open-enrolment and custom executive-education unit of a research-intensive business school in Manchester. Turnover was £14.2 million at a blended contribution margin of 32%. Seventy per cent of custom revenue came from six anchor clients, which made every renewal structural rather than merely commercial. NorthGrid Utilities, a £6 billion regulated energy group, was the second-largest of the six.

Two engines drove the unit's economics. Open-enrolment programmes spread a course's cost across many companies: general fit, small marginal cost per seat. Custom work concentrated the whole cost on one client and was paid for on that fit. Custom renewals turned on whether a client could show its own board that something had changed in the business, not on how a cohort had rated the week.

## The Situation

The brief was contested inside NorthGrid, and the three memos attached to the request said so. Cassidy, the named sponsor, wanted leaders who ran a decarbonised, digitised business differently. The divisional managing directors, who held the technical budgets, wanted the energy and digital skills gaps in their own teams filled. Sian Petrie, NorthGrid's head of learning, wanted a scalable, repeatable programme her function could own and re-run. Delaney's team had priced a design that answered all three; it ran to roughly 14 days per learner.

The arithmetic was plain (see **Exhibit 1**). NorthGrid's £1.8 million bought £15,000 per participant, all in; the drafted bespoke design cost £2.6 million, leaving £0.8 million to find. Only three of Pennine's five senior strategy faculty were free in the launch window; the other two were committed to open programmes earning £0.6 million, and pulling them would take that revenue with them. Losing the bid would forfeit 24% of Pennine's custom pipeline, visibly, in front of the other five anchor clients.

Pennine's bid review scored any proposal on four readings, and Pryce read all four (see **Exhibit 2**). *Client fit* was the panel's judgement, out of a hundred, of how closely a design answered the client's brief. *Contribution margin* was the share of the fee left after the direct cost of designing and delivering. *Launch readiness* was its confidence that the first cohort could sit down on the date promised. *Win probability* was its estimate of the chance of beating the field. On 8 June the sheet stood at 20, 15 and 25, the margin line still carrying the unit's blended 32%. Beneath the four sat the design's cost against the envelope.

If the bid went out as drafted, it asked NorthGrid for money it had not budgeted and named a date the build could not meet.

## What Delaney Controlled

Four things had to be settled, and her team had already argued for alternatives on each.

**The diagnosis.** Delaney had to name what NorthGrid was buying: a change in how 120 leaders behaved back in the business; a technical upgrade in the energy and digital knowledge the divisional managing directors said their people lacked; a repeatable asset NorthGrid's learning function could own and re-run; or all three at once, so that every voice in the room was answered. She had to say whose judgement would settle the renewal — Cassidy's, the divisional managing directors', Petrie's, or all of them weighted equally. She had to treat the 120 as one population, or as two designed differently, general-management and technical — or leave the mapping until an agenda existed. And she had to set how far the written objectives ran, from a list of subjects to be covered to measurable behaviours tied to named NorthGrid business outcomes.

**The architecture.** Three forms were available: a fully bespoke build written end to end for NorthGrid by Pennine's own faculty; a route that put most of the 120 through Pennine's existing open programmes; or a hybrid, a custom NorthGrid spine plus modular open components for the generic material. A hybrid also required her to fix the custom-spine share of the content, anywhere from none to all of it. She then had to say how the gap would close: by changing format and delivery mode, by scoping the objectives to what £1.8 million would buy, or by bidding the full £2.6 million design and asking NorthGrid to fund the brief it had written.

**The configuration.** Delaney had to set the per-learner days across four delivery modes, totalling between six and ten: faculty-led in-person intensives, virtual-live sessions, asynchronous digital modules, and coaching with on-the-job application projects. She had to place the three available senior strategy faculty — on two or three moments only, across the whole programme, or scarcely at all, staffing the mandate with practitioner-facilitators and digital content and leaving the senior academics on their open programmes. She had to decide what would be measured: whether leaders behaved differently afterwards and whether NorthGrid's business results moved; or reactions at the end of each module and a knowledge test; or nothing formal. And she had to sequence a 16-to-18-week build against a ten-week date: a proof cohort live on the date with the full build behind it, the full bespoke build promised on the date, or a request to move the date to sixteen weeks.

**The room.** She could frame the pitch on NorthGrid's business outcomes and how they would be evidenced, on the programme itself — days, modules, faculty names, platform — or on price. If Cassidy asked for two extra days per learner at the same fee, Delaney could hold the price and say what came out if days went in, add the days against a year-two scale agreement, or add them at no charge. If procurement pressed for 15% off, she could decline anything below the 30% floor and narrow the scope instead, offer a smaller phased first-cohort commitment that scaled once impact was shown, or meet the cut and carry the lower margin. She had also to decide how hard to argue value against price in the room.

## Closing

Delaney put the two documents side by side — the request that named a date and a number, and the costing that met neither. Cassidy and procurement would sit down with her in four weeks. She had £1.8 million, 120 leaders, ten weeks to a first cohort, three senior faculty, and a 30% floor.
