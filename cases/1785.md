# Verene Composites: Thirty Days to Rebuild Trust

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1785 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1785 — *Fair Process* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of 9 June 2026, an internal email meant for a small circle of directors at Verene Composites SAS reached the entire company instead. It described a plan to close the company's Limoges plant, move its 250 jobs to the larger sites in Tours and Clermont, and book roughly €14 million a year in savings. Nobody outside that circle had been asked anything first. By the next morning, a snap survey across all three sites put trust in management's decision-making at 34%, down from 62% before the leak. Camille Rousseau, Verene's Site Transformation Lead, had thirty days — the board's own deadline — to decide not whether Limoges closed, but how the rest of this played out.

## The Protagonist

Camille Rousseau, 44, had spent a decade at Verene Composites, the last four years leading the transformation office that handled the company's harder internal changes. Rousseau had a reputation for being handed problems only after they had already gone wrong: the economics behind closing Limoges were not really in dispute inside the leadership team, but reactions ran hotter than expected, with no agreement on why. Rousseau reported directly to the executive committee and would present a plan to the works council and, eventually, the board.

## The Decision

Rousseau's task did not stop at deciding to close Limoges — that decision had, in effect, already been made and had already leaked. What remained open was the sequence of moves across the next thirty days: working out why trust had collapsed so fast; deciding how much say to give employees, the works council and the receiving sites before anything more was finalised; choosing how much of the underlying reasoning to share and in what order to tell people; and converting whatever acceptance resulted into people willing to carry out the move. Each stage carried its own fork, and getting one wrong could not be corrected in the next.

## The Company and its Setting

Verene Composites SAS was a French manufacturer of advanced composite components for aerospace and wind-energy customers, based in Tours. It employed 1,400 people across Tours, Clermont and Limoges, generated €380 million in annual revenue, and ran at a 9% operating margin. Aerospace demand had recovered faster than wind demand, and Limoges, the smallest of the three sites, had become the one plant running below scale and losing money. Verene had a strong tradition of works-council relations, and a long habit of telling employees what had been decided without much explanation of why or how.

## The Situation

The leak did more than embarrass the leadership team. French law required Verene to inform and formally consult its works council, the CSE, before finalising a change like this; getting the sequence wrong risked a legal challenge freezing the plan for four to six months, taking a large share of the €14 million with it. Separately, anxiety across the three sites threatened output badly enough that Verene's own estimate put roughly €9 million of the target at risk from lost productivity alone. A regional newspaper had asked for comment, a major aerospace customer had asked whether its supply was now at risk, and the two senior engineers at Limoges on whom the transfer depended had let it be known that how this was handled would decide whether they stayed or left first (see **Exhibit 1**). Left as it was, none of this would resolve itself: the board's clock would keep running, the risks to the €14 million would stay live, and the newspaper, the customer and the two engineers would still be waiting for an answer thirty days on.

## What Rousseau Controlled

The first decision was diagnostic: what had actually destroyed trust at Verene — how the plan surfaced, with no consultation before it went public; the substance of the decision itself, 250 people losing their jobs; disbelief in the underlying economics, a sense that Limoges was not truly too small to keep open; or the works council's own institutional resistance to change, regardless of merit. A related, more granular call followed: from a standard list of five things any orderly rollout includes — bringing the right people in early, working through options and concerns together before anything is final, explaining the reasoning once a decision is reached, carrying it out with clarity, and checking afterward how it landed — Rousseau had to say which ones this leak had actually skipped. A third dial set how candidly, internally, her team would own responsibility for what went wrong — anywhere from full, public ownership of the failure down to treating it as an unfortunate slip best left unexamined.

The second decision set how much room to leave for other people's input before anything more was locked in: consult deeply on whatever remained genuinely open while being clear about what was settled; send out the finalised plan without further discussion, valuing decisive clarity over debate; hold one listening session and then publish the original plan unchanged; or put the closure itself in front of the works council as something to be voted on. Alongside that, she would mark which matters — timing and phasing, transfer terms and redeployment, the severance and support package, or whether Limoges closed at all — were genuinely open. Once input came back, a further choice governed it: state openly which suggestions changed the plan and which could not be accommodated; quietly adopt a few easier ones while staying silent on the rest; or note it for the record and proceed as written.

The third decision covered what to say and in what order. On transparency: lay out the full economics — the €14 million figure, the sub-scale numbers, the alternatives considered and set aside — or give the reasoning in general terms without the figures, or state the decision as final and keep the underlying reasoning confined to management. On sequence: brief the works council first and move outward to Limoges staff, the receiving sites and external audiences; hold one company-wide session with the works council alongside everyone else; or get ahead of the regional newspaper by briefing the press before staff heard anything. On tone: be direct about what would be difficult while promising only what could actually be delivered; build the message around reassurance, including no forced redundancies and full redeployment within 30 kilometres; or keep it brief, stating the decision and moving straight to what followed.

The fourth decision, in the final third of the thirty days, covered execution. Rousseau would set a euro figure, from zero up to a €9 million ceiling, for redeployment, retraining and severance for the departing staff — too little would read as abandonment, too much would give away the point of the consolidation. She would keep the two key engineers by offering a named role leading the transfer plus relocation, a retention bonus with no role change, or simply assuming they would stay unless told otherwise. She would build a formal 60-day check combining a fairness survey, a works-council review and tracked delivery of the support package; rely on informal manager check-ins, trusting supervisors to flag problems as they arise; or treat the matter as closed once announced. Asked whether supply was at risk, the aerospace customer could be offered the transfer plan and the retained engineers as evidence of continuity, told nothing until the transfer was complete, or promised zero disruption with every delivery date guaranteed immediately.

Across all four stages, the rollout would be read against four measures — perceived fairness, a commitment-to-execute score, a process-integrity score, and how much of the €14 million saving remained intact — plus three risk indicators covering the works council, the two engineers, and the thirty-day clock itself (see **Exhibit 2**).

## Closing

At half past eight, Rousseau printed the four decisions onto a single sheet — diagnosis, consultation scope, communication, execution — and set the thirty days beside them. The plant would very likely still close; on the economics, that argument was close to over. What was not decided was everything else: who would be asked, what they would be told, in what order, and what Verene would do to make sure everyone who was not leaving still believed the company had earned the right to make this call.
