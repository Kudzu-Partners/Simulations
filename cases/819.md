# Quillmoor Capital Partners: The REIT Play

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-819 · **Companion simulation:** Express 819 — *The REIT Play* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, February 4, 2025, Carolyn Reyes opened a letter from a special servicer in Charlotte informing Quillmoor Capital Partners that one of its Dallas office loans had been transferred out of ordinary servicing. It was not the first such letter. Four commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) loans on Quillmoor's Fund III office buildings, totalling US$78 million, would mature within 18 months, and on three of them the buildings no longer earned enough to cover their own debt service.

The Fund III Limited Partner Advisory Committee (LPAC) met in three weeks. Reyes had to bring it a complete workout plan — what to give up, what to exchange, how to settle with the lenders, whether to take a roll-up offer from a non-traded REIT, and how to end the fund — and then carry that plan out, step by step, over the fund's remaining life.

## The Protagonist

Carolyn Reyes, 52, had been Managing Partner of Quillmoor since 2016, after fourteen years in acquisitions at a Dallas pension advisor. She had raised Fund III in 2018 on a value-add thesis for suburban Sunbelt office and had personally signed the investment memos for most of its buildings. As general partner, Quillmoor stood to earn a promote — a share of profits above the limited partners' preferred return — and was already marketing Fund IV to many of the same investors.

## The Decision

Reyes had to decide how much of a shrinking portfolio to defend, how to convert part of it into something better without triggering a tax bill, how to resolve the maturing debt, whether to trade four of the buildings for partnership units, and what to recommend to the LPAC about the fund's term and the general partner's own economics.

## The Company and its Setting

Quillmoor Capital Partners was a Dallas-based private real estate manager with US$420 million of assets under management. Fund III held 11 Class-B suburban office buildings across Dallas, Houston, Phoenix and Raleigh. Its investors were state and municipal pension plans, university endowments, family offices and foreign investors, each with its own tax sensitivities (see **Exhibit 1**).

Suburban Class-B office had repriced after the pandemic. Portfolio vacancy had risen from 9% to 22%. The limited partners had made clear there was no appetite for a capital call.

## The Situation

Investors had contributed US$152 million to Fund III. Its remaining equity stood at US$113 million, roughly 0.74 times the money put in. Reserves totalled US$18 million. The fund was in year seven of an eight-year life, with two optional one-year extensions under the partnership agreement.

Two transactions were on the table (see **Exhibit 2**). A Sunbelt operator was selling a 320-unit Class-A apartment community in suburban Austin for US$68 million with a US$44 million assumable CMBS loan, requiring US$24 million of equity. Quillmoor could sell office buildings into a §1031 like-kind exchange to buy into it, deferring the gain — provided replacement property was identified within 45 days and the exchange closed within 180. Separately, Wrenfield Peak NTR, a non-traded REIT sponsor, had proposed taking the Raleigh pair and the Phoenix pair — four buildings, US$156 million gross — into its operating partnership under §721 in exchange for OP units at a stated net asset value of US$10.25 per unit.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was plain. Three loans could not be serviced from building income, the servicers would control the outcome at maturity, the reserves would be spent holding all 11 buildings, and the fund would reach the end of its term below the money investors had contributed.

## What Reyes Controlled

**Portfolio triage.** Her partners had argued for three postures:

- *Divest:* return the keys on three buildings and run the rest to cash, shedding debt fast while losses were still contained.
- *Balanced workout:* release the weakest two and fight for the core, concentrating money where leases could still be signed.
- *Hold everything:* keep all 11 and hand nothing back, preserving every recovery option if the office market turned.

Alongside it she would set the share of the US$18 million reserve to spend now on tenant improvements, leasing commissions and loan paydowns. Whatever she did not spend remained dry powder.

**The exchange.** She would choose how many buildings, from one to six, to sell into the §1031 pool — four had to stay for a possible roll-up — and pick a qualified intermediary to hold the proceeds:

- *Budget:* a regional firm at US$8,000, uninsured, keeping costs low.
- *Mid-market:* a national firm at US$22,000, bonded against its own failure.
- *Institutional:* a US$55,000 firm with escrow insurance on the funds held.

She would also choose how to identify replacement property: up to three replacement properties, whatever their combined value; any number of properties worth no more than 200% of what was sold; or any number, provided 95% of the value identified actually closed.

**The lenders.** For the remaining maturing debt she could pursue a full refinance at SOFR plus 400 basis points; a two-to-three-year modification funded by an equity infusion; a discounted payoff, raising capital to retire the loans below par; or a deed in lieu, handing the buildings to the servicer. She would also set her posture with the servicers: negotiate in good faith only, keeping the loan carve-outs untouched but leaving her nothing to bargain with beyond the numbers; or threaten a Chapter 11 filing on one property company, gaining leverage at the table but risking, if a court judged it bad faith, carve-outs that reached Quillmoor directly.

**The roll-up.** Wrenfield Peak's offer allowed four responses:

- *Accept:* contribute all four buildings at stated net asset value and secure the deferral on the full block.
- *Counter:* reopen the unit value and the lockup before contributing, testing the sponsor's own pricing.
- *Reject:* sell the Raleigh pair to strategic buyers for cash, keeping Phoenix and full control.
- *Partial:* contribute the Raleigh pair alone, holding Phoenix back for further lease-up.

She would also propose an OP-unit lockup between 90 and 720 days; Wrenfield Peak had asked for 12 months.

**The close.** On the fund's term she would recommend one of three paths:

- *Terminate on schedule,* returning capital on the date investors had been promised.
- *Extend one year,* giving the remaining buildings another full leasing season.
- *Extend two years,* the maximum, leaving the most time for values to recover.

On the general partner's economics she would propose one of three treatments:

- *No waiver:* take the full promote if earned, keeping the terms investors had signed and the team's incentive intact.
- *Partial clawback:* waive promote below the hurdle, sharing the shortfall with investors while keeping some upside.
- *Full clawback:* return all promote to investors, giving up the upside to support the Fund IV raise.

Six measures tracked the plan: fund equity; gain deferred from tax; the share of the US$78 million maturity wall resolved; an LP trust index, starting at 70 out of 100, reflecting how the committee regarded Quillmoor's conduct; Quillmoor's personal recourse exposure, starting at zero; and dry powder. Each stage would also receive an execution score out of 100, and the fund's annualised net return and equity multiple would be restated after every step.

## Closing

At 9:15 a.m. Reyes laid the servicer's letter on top of the Wrenfield Peak term sheet and wrote five headings on a legal pad. The LPAC met in three weeks. The loans matured within eighteen months.
