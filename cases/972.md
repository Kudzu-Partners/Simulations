# Brisolva: Ten Days to the Board

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-972 · **Companion simulation:** Express 972 — *BoardInvestorConvo — The Board Meeting You Actually Want* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On the evening of Monday, April 13, 2026, Irene Valcárcel, co-founder and chief executive of Brisolva, scrolled to the last slide of a 47-slide deck and read its title: *Where we need your help.* Four items sat under it. The board of the Valencia software company would meet in ten days, on Thursday, April 23, for ninety minutes. The deck was the March pack with the numbers updated.

Since March, five hard facts had piled up that the four other directors did not know. Two sat on slide 31 as figures without comment; three appeared nowhere in the deck. Valcárcel had ten days to decide what she wanted the board to leave the room having done, what the directors would hear from her before the meeting, and what the ninety minutes would look like. The board-preparation coach that her lead investor offered to portfolio founders, an AI coaching assistant, split the ten days into four steps (see **Exhibit 2**) and logged each one.

## The Protagonist

Valcárcel was thirty-eight. She had run delivery planning at a Valencian food distributor before founding Brisolva in 2019 with Tomás Echeverri, a colleague from the same company, who became chief technology officer. Her background was operations, not sales. She had raised a seed round in 2020 and a €14 million Series A in 2024, and had run eleven board meetings since. She prepared each one alone, over two evenings, from the previous pack.

## The Decision

Valcárcel had to decide what the April 23 meeting was for, and then build it backwards: the ask, the disclosures, the conversations before the meeting, and the deck. Each step had its own day on the coach's calendar, and each closed before the next one opened.

## The Company and its Setting

Brisolva, S.L. sold route-planning and delivery-scheduling software to mid-sized food and beverage distributors in Spain and Portugal, typically fleets of twenty to 150 vehicles. At March 31, 2026 it had 212 customers, 74 employees and €6.8 million of annual recurring revenue (ARR) (see **Exhibit 1**).

In 2025 three national distributors had asked for pilots. One had converted into a contract worth €380,000 a year, Brisolva's largest. Valcárcel wanted to move upmarket. Selling to national distributors meant long cycles, procurement committees and a different sales team, and nobody at Brisolva had led enterprise sales. Echeverri wanted to stay in the mid-market and spend the money on product.

The board had five seats. Gonzalo Arrieta-Uribe, partner at Velauria Capital, which had led the Series A, chaired it. Henrik Aalto represented Aurelle Growth Partners, the Series A co-investor. Nuria Castellví of Tesselane Ventures had led the seed. Pilar Sandoval-Ortega, an independent director since 2025, had been a sales director at a European logistics-software company. Echeverri had given up his seat at the Series A, when the investors asked for an independent. The board met roughly every seven weeks; the last meeting had been on March 5.

## The Situation

The first-quarter numbers had missed the plan. New ARR in Q1 was €0.58 million against a plan of €1.0 million, leaving ARR at €6.8 million against €7.2 million planned. Net burn had risen from €310,000 a month in Q4 2025 to €470,000 in Q1, mostly from the enterprise pilots and six hires. Cash stood at €7.1 million.

Three other facts had arrived since March. In the first week of April two mid-market customers had given notice of non-renewal, together €290,000 of ARR; one had asked for a price proposal before its notice took effect. Lucía Ferrán, the engineer who had written most of the routing engine and the only one who knew its optimisation core in full, had an offer from a competitor and had said she would answer by April 30. And Valcárcel and Echeverri had disagreed about the enterprise move since February. Since mid-March they had held two of six scheduled weekly one-to-ones. Echeverri had asked her to keep the disagreement "between the two of us until we've talked it through."

The directors had views on how meetings should run. After the March meeting Arrieta-Uribe had told her, "I would rather hear things with everyone at the table," and added that he read everything she sent. Castellví had asked for "the whole pipeline, deal by deal" in the next pack. Sandoval-Ortega had said in March that "boards that vote on everything stop thinking." Aalto had twice offered to "help with hiring" without saying how.

The four items on slide 47 were an approval to hire a chief revenue officer with enterprise experience and move €900,000 of the 2026 budget into an enterprise sales team; the investors' commitment to lead a €3 million extension before year-end; introductions to enterprise sales leaders; and views on enterprise pricing. In her notebook, the line under *what I need* read: *help with go-to-market.*

If nothing changed, the directors would receive the 47 slides on the Monday before the meeting, as in March. The Q1 figures would be on slide 31, the four items on slide 47, and the other three facts nowhere. No calls with directors were booked.

The coach's log tracked three running figures. Board trust, the coach's reading of how far the directors trusted her, had been logged at 60 out of 100 after the March meeting. Preparation, the points earned for the work done, stood at 0. Meeting value, what the meeting was set to produce in decisions, commitments and introductions, also stood at 0. The log also showed the number of active asks and the days left before the meeting. After each step it recorded the trust reading, the value and preparation added, and a note from the coach. The meeting would be judged on what it produced and on the trust the board left with.

## What Valcárcel Controlled

At the first step, ten days out, she settled the **ask**. How many asks to bring into the room: one, the four on her slide, or any other number. Which kind of ask led: a **decision** the board would vote on, a **commitment** of action from the directors, **introductions** to people, or **advice** in open discussion. And how specific it was: as loose as *help with go-to-market*, a middle form such as *introductions to enterprise sales leaders*, or as tight as *two introductions to enterprise chief revenue officers by the end of May*.

At the second step, eight days out, she settled **disclosure**. Which of the five hard items the board would hear before the meeting: the rise in burn, the missed forecast, the churn notices, the key-person risk around Ferrán, and the disagreement with Echeverri. And whether the disclosures came with a **remediation plan**: every one of them, only the easy ones, or facts alone.

At the third step, six days out, she settled the **one-to-ones**. How many of the four directors to call before the meeting. How many days before the meeting to hold the calls. And what to say on them: a heads-up on the issue, her current thinking, and a question about the director's instinct; a status update; or the final ask and nothing else.

At the fourth step, four days out, she settled the **deck and the ask moment**. How many slides the core deck would carry, with everything else in an appendix. When in the ninety minutes to put the ask on the table: at the opening, framing the meeting around it; in the middle, after the strategic question; in the last ten minutes; or never explicitly, leaving the board to offer. And what to do if a director answered the ask with advice instead of a decision: press for a decision that day, park it as a follow-up vote, or take the advice and move on.

## Closing

At 22:40 Valcárcel opened a blank page beside the deck and typed the sentence the coach had asked for: *I want the board to leave this meeting having…* She stopped there. Four items sat on slide 47, three facts sat outside the deck, and the chair's assistant had written that morning to ask when the pack would arrive.
