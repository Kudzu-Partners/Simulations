# Rheinwerk Components: Bond Before You Bargain

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1772 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1772 — *Off the Hook — Bond Before You Bargain* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

At 6:40 on the morning of Monday, February 9, 2026, Nora Albrecht's phone filled with photos of six hundred people standing motionless on the assembly-hall floor of her own plant. Overnight, a memo proposing a €22 million cost reduction — including relocating one production line out of Düsseldorf to a lower-cost site — had leaked to the night shift. By the time Albrecht reached the gate, the line had been down for close to an hour, the works-council chair was refusing to meet her, and the day-shift handover at 14:00 was seven hours away.

She had until then to get six hundred people back to work in a way that did not spend the twenty-two years since the Düsseldorf plant had last lost a single day to industrial action — a record every plant director before her had been able to take for granted.

## The Protagonist

Albrecht, 51, had been Plant Director of Rheinwerk Components' Düsseldorf facility since 2021, after eleven years in manufacturing engineering at a rival tier-one supplier, with a reputation for reading a shop floor accurately and keeping cost programs on schedule without a grievance filed. The €22 million restructuring plan had been drafted at group level, above her, including the decision to keep the works council out of the process. She would be the one standing in the hall when the workforce found out.

## The Decision

Across the seven hours before the handover, Albrecht had to decide, moment by moment, how to read the man across from her, how to open the conversation, how to reframe what looked like a zero-sum fight, and finally what terms she would actually put in writing — all while managing how she herself reacted when the exchange got hard.

## The Company and its Setting

Rheinwerk Components GmbH, founded in 1968 and headquartered in Cologne, was a tier-one automotive supplier of braking and thermal-management systems to three European OEMs: group revenue of €880 million at a 9% operating margin, 3,400 employees across two German plants. Düsseldorf was the larger of the two — 1,250 employees, €410 million of output — and the one now standing still.

Rheinwerk operated under Germany's co-determination system: a works council with real standing, an IG Metall presence, and, until that morning, twenty-two consecutive years without a strike, slowdown, or wildcat action at either plant. Management had come to treat that record as a given, not an asset requiring active maintenance.

## The Situation

The line-down cost was running at roughly €95,000 an hour. Two of Rheinwerk's OEM customers ran just-in-time production with no more than six hours of buffer stock, and the largest of them had told Rheinwerk that a stoppage lasting past eighteen hours would trigger a formal supplier-risk review and a €1.4 million contractual penalty. Rheinwerk's second plant had signaled informally that its own works council was watching the standoff closely. Local press already had photographers at the gate. The numbers that separated an ordinary Monday from this one fit on a single card (Exhibit 1).

Whatever she did next would be read on four measures: how far the hall moved toward returning to work, how much of the plant's twenty-two-year trust balance survived the day, how steady her team stayed once things got difficult, and how many euros the stoppage had cost by the end. If the morning passed with no meeting at all, the penalty clock kept running and the second plant kept watching regardless of what she chose next.

## What Albrecht Controlled

The four moments of the day, and what stayed open at each of them, also fit on one card (Exhibit 2).

Before walking near the floor, around 07:00, she had to work out two things at once. On the works-council chair, Werner Kessler, twenty-eight years at the plant: was he holding the line because underneath the noise was a wage or compensation number he hadn't yet named, because job security by itself was the whole fight and nothing else registered, because he felt undercut by the secrecy and wanted trust and respect restored, or because it was a demonstration that the works council could still stop production? Separately, she had to name Kessler's stated position — not the same thing as his reason — reading it as either a pay raise for the shifts currently occupying the hall, relocation being off the table before any conversation could start, or nothing short of her own resignation as plant director. And she owed herself an honest answer, on a scale from zero to a hundred, to how strong her own pull was, right now, to walk in and lead with company rights and hard numbers rather than with the person standing in front of her.

By around 09:00, with five hours left and both just-in-time customers inside their six-hour buffer, she had to decide how she actually opened the conversation. She weighed asserting the company's position: "We have a legal right to restructure, and the business case behind it is real"; owning the secrecy outright: "We handled this badly. You should have heard it from us"; proposing a process: "Let's get a meeting scheduled by 09:00"; or naming the shared stake: "This plant and these jobs matter to me too." Whichever opening she used, she also had to decide what share of the first ten minutes she let Kessler do the talking, as a percentage, and where the conversation itself happened — calling Kessler to the director's office, walking onto the floor alone with no entourage, or addressing the whole hall over a megaphone instead.

By 10:30, with three and a half hours left and the eighteen-hour penalty clock now a live consideration, she had to decide how to reframe the standoff itself: as a question of what each side's legal rights actually were, as a straight trade-off in which relocation and jobs could not both survive, or as a shared problem — finding the €22 million and protecting the plant's future together. Attached to whichever reframe she chose was a single move meant to unlock the conversation: opening the books to the council on the OEM penalties and the plant's margins; pausing the relocation decision pending a 60-day review conducted jointly with the council; working to negotiate directly with the shop stewards, separately from Kessler's stated position; proposing a joint cost-reduction taskforce that seated the works council and management together; or tabling a phased proposal immediately, with a concrete timeline and a set of concessions attached. At some point in that exchange Kessler was likely to raise his voice and slam the table, and when he did, Albrecht would have to choose, in the moment, whether to match his intensity and hold her ground at the same volume, go quiet and withdraw whatever offer was currently on the table, or stay calm and name what she was seeing.

By noon, with two hours left, the last decisions were about what she would actually put in front of six hundred people. On relocation, she could offer it paused, with the final call made jointly after the 60-day review; confirmed as proceeding on the original schedule, non-negotiable; or taken off the table unconditionally and permanently, with no review attached. On timing, she could ask for a full agreement before any return, by the 14:00 handover; an immediate return with a signed joint process attached to it; or an immediate return with the rest of the details worked out afterward. On communication, she had to decide who to align with first — the press already waiting at the gate, to shape the story before anyone else did; a joint statement from Kessler and herself together, to all six hundred workers at once; or the OEM, to try to head off its supplier-risk review before it formally opened. And afterward, she and her team would be rated, as a percentage, on how well they held their composure once the deadline hit.

## Closing

By 6:55, Albrecht had the four questions written on one card and no answers filled in yet. Kessler was still standing at the hall's north doors with roughly two hundred workers around him, the line above them silent, and the clock reading seven hours and five minutes to the handover.
