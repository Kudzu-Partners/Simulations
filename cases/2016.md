# Banco Atlântico Norte: The Duramax Term Loan

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-2016 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2016 — *Risk Underwriter* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, June 15, 2026, Inês Carvalho opened the credit file on Cerâmica Duramax, S.A. and found two earnings figures for the same twelve months. The audited statements reported EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — of €14.2 million on revenue of €88 million. The private-equity sponsor buying the company had filed a bridge that added €3.3 million to that figure and called the result an adjusted EBITDA of €17.5 million. The €45 million senior term loan the sponsor wanted Banco Atlântico Norte (BAN) to write had been sized against the second number.

Carvalho ran credit underwriting on BAN's corporate and leveraged-finance desk in Porto. The sponsor had given the bank 72 hours. She had until the credit committee sat on Thursday, June 18, to recommend whether BAN should lend, at what price, and on what terms. The sponsor had said it would otherwise take the mandate to a rival bank.

## The Protagonist

Inês Carvalho, 41, had joined BAN in 2011 and had led credit underwriting on the corporate and leveraged-finance desk since 2022. Her first four years at the bank had been spent in its restructuring unit, working out loans other people had written. She had signed some forty leveraged credits since moving to the desk. The desk's scorecard measured two things that did not always move together: the risk-adjusted return on the loans it booked, and the credit quality of those loans three years afterwards. The recommendation that went to committee carried Carvalho's signature.

## The Decision

Carvalho had to decide whether BAN would lend €45 million against the buy-out of Duramax, how much of that amount the bank would keep on its own books, what it would charge for it, and what it would require of the borrower in return.

She worked the file in four stages, committing each one before opening the next, because each stage set the inputs for the stage after it: the read of the borrower's accounts, the default-risk score, the term sheet, and the recommendation she would defend in front of the committee.

## The Company and its Setting

BAN, founded in Porto in 1991, was a mid-sized Portuguese commercial bank with a €6.8 billion loan book. Its corporate and leveraged-finance desk originated roughly €900 million of new credit a year and operated under an internal limit of €1.2 billion on its leveraged-finance sub-portfolio. The desk booked a loan only where the return it produced on the capital the bank had to hold against it — the risk-adjusted return on capital, or RAROC — cleared 12.0%.

Cerâmica Duramax, S.A. manufactured industrial tiles in Aveiro. It had revenue of €88 million and an audited EBITDA margin of 16.1%. Its demand tracked construction activity, and the standing scenario the desk ran on the sector was an EBITDA decline of 25% to 30% in the second or third year of a facility.

The sponsor had closed two facilities with BAN before and had paid a margin of Euribor plus 350 basis points on both. It expected the same terms here.

## The Situation

The front sheet of the file carried four running numbers and a rating line (see **Exhibit 1**).

The first was the underwriting EBITDA — the earnings figure the loan would be sized and priced against. It opened at the sponsor's €17.5 million, above an audited base of €14.2 million. The second was leverage: the €45 million facility divided by that earnings figure, which came to 2.6 times on €17.5 million and 3.2 times on €14.2 million. The third was RAROC measured against the desk's 12.0% hurdle; it was blank, because no price had been set. The fourth was portfolio utilisation, the leveraged-finance book against its €1.2 billion limit. That stood at 84%, a little over €1 billion drawn, and a full €45 million hold would take it to roughly 88%. Beneath the four ran the internal rating and probability-of-default band the file carried on opening: B, with a probability of default of around 4%.

The €3.3 million bridge had two components (see **Exhibit 2**). €2.1 million was a management fee paid to a vehicle owned by the selling family; the sponsor's position was that the fee stopped at completion and had never been a cost of running the business. €1.2 million was restructuring, presented as a one-off item — an item that had also been charged, and described the same way, in each of the three audited years. The auditors had signed the statements. They had not signed the bridge.

The desk's financial-quality reviewer had raised a second line. Receivables days had moved from 52 to 71 across the same three years, while revenue had grown in single digits.

The desk's own pricing work pointed to about Euribor plus 475 basis points.

If the file went to committee as the sponsor had drafted it — the full €45 million held on BAN's books for five years at Euribor plus 350, repaid in a single bullet at maturity under one interest-cover test — the leveraged-finance book would stand at about 88% of its limit, and the loan would be sized against the earnings figure the auditors had not signed.

## What Carvalho Controlled

At the first stage she set the earnings figure she would lend against: the sponsor's full €17.5 million, a split at about €15.9 million, or the audited €14.2 million. She decided how to treat the receivables movement — as ordinary drift, as an amber note in the file with no change to the earnings figure, or as a working-capital haircut. She graded the file on revenue recognition, working capital and related-party items. And she recorded, as a percentage, how firmly she intended to hold her number when the sponsor pushed back on it.

At the second stage she mapped Duramax to an internal rating and a probability-of-default band: BB at around 1.5%, B at around 4%, or B− / CCC+ at around 7%. She chose how far to run the cyclical scenario — not at all, at a 15% EBITDA decline, or at the full 28% with a check that interest cover held above 1.5 times. And she named the single factor she would tell the committee drove the credit's risk.

At the third stage she priced and structured. She set the margin over Euribor in basis points, anywhere from 200 to 700 in steps of 25. She set arrangement and commitment fees at 0.5%, 1.5% or 2.5% of the €45 million. She chose the maintenance covenants: a single generous interest-cover test; one leverage covenant at 3.5 times tested annually; or a package with maximum leverage at 3.5 times stepping down to 3.0 times, minimum interest cover of 2.0 times, and a cash sweep, tested quarterly. And she chose what protection to require beyond price and covenants — a bullet with no equity condition, mandatory amortisation of 5% a year, or a minimum 40% equity contribution from the sponsor alongside that amortisation.

At the fourth stage she chose the recommendation: approve the full €45 million hold; decline; approve with conditions attached to price, covenants and the equity cushion; or approve at a reduced hold, keeping €25 million and syndicating €20 million. She chose what to lead with when the committee pushed on price, covenants and concentration. She wrote a closing line on the desk's risk appetite. And she recorded, again as a percentage, how firmly she would hold her price and covenants if the sponsor threatened to walk.

Each stage she committed added a line to the file's running record: the stage, a one-line note of the move she had made, the leverage the file then carried, the risk-adjusted return, a covenant-strength reading on a 0-to-100 scale, and portfolio utilisation as a percentage of the limit. Leverage, the risk-adjusted return and covenant strength also ran as a chart across the four stages.

## Closing

At 7:20 p.m. on Monday, Carvalho left both earnings figures open on the screen and wrote the first line of the file. The committee sat on Thursday. The sponsor's 72 hours ran out the same day.
