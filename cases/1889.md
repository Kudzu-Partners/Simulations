# Northline Executive Education: The Cascadia Brief

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1889 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1889 — *The Program Architect* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 8 June 2026, Naomi Okafor read Cascadia Energy's one-page brief, then reread the file behind it: the leadership program Northline delivered the year before, closed with a 4.6-out-of-5 satisfaction score and nothing else. Cascadia wanted leadership development for about 150 mid-level managers, a fixed CAD 750,000, launched inside the quarter, and — in the CHRO's words — a CEO who wanted to see results this time. A boutique consultancy had quoted the same scope at CAD 540,000.

Okafor had roughly ninety days to turn that brief into a program Cascadia would buy, launch and believe in — or confirm the CHRO's verdict on the last one: lovely, and we have no idea what it did for us.

## The Protagonist

Naomi Okafor, 41, had been Northline's newest Program Director since January 2026, promoted after four years designing its custom leadership curricula, and eleven years before that in learning and development at a Canadian bank. She took the role knowing Northline's renewal rate had slipped to 61 percent and that a recent survey found nearly half of buyers could not say what their last program produced. Her year-end review would weigh the margin Cascadia left against whether the account renewed at all.

## The Decision

Okafor had to decide what Cascadia's request meant, what program Northline could design and price for it inside the budget and calendar, how she would prove afterward it had worked, and what she would say when Cascadia's CEO and CHRO asked why Northline cost CAD 210,000 more.

## The Company and its Setting

Northline was the custom-programs arm of a major Toronto business school, running about 40 tailored engagements a year across Canada on CAD 14 million of annual revenue at a 22 percent contribution margin (see **Exhibit 1**). Its pitch rested on outcomes over content, but the slipping renewal rate and the survey said pitch and delivery had drifted apart.

Cascadia Energy was a CAD 4.2 billion utility mid-restructuring, the account worth roughly CAD 1.6 million over three years. A boutique competitor, quoting CAD 540,000 for comparable scope, was already in its CHRO's inbox.

## The Situation

The brief itself was the first problem. "Leadership development for 150 managers" described a course, not a business outcome, and Cascadia's restructuring pointed to something the brief never mentioned: line managers in the new structure were not making calls they already had authority to make, and the reorganisation was losing time as a result. Whether that was the real driver, or managers were simply unsettled, or lacked broader leadership skills, was unclear — Cascadia had offered a second discovery call nobody had used.

The arithmetic sat underneath any answer. CAD 750,000 across roughly 150 participants was CAD 5,000 a head, all-in, with no overage, and the program had to launch within about ninety days. The prior program set no baseline and measured nothing beyond an end-of-course rating — repeating that would leave Cascadia no better able to name a result. With the boutique's CAD 540,000 on the table, whatever Northline built had to be worth CAD 210,000 more.

Left alone, the brief would produce exactly the program Cascadia was already unhappy with: a well-reviewed course nobody could connect to anything that had changed.

## What Okafor Controlled

The first block was diagnosis. Cascadia's restructuring produced four readings of the brief: managers disengaged since the announcement, needing a program to lift morale and engagement; decision paralysis inside the new structure, needing a program that targeted that behaviour directly; a general shortage of modern leadership skills, needing a wider curriculum; or the brief taken at face value, needing a strong leadership-essentials course for all 150 managers. Whichever reading she took, she had to name the one measure the CEO would accept: a knowledge test proving how much content managers had retained, a completion count confirming all 150 managers had finished, the time managers took to act on the restructuring's own milestones, or an end-of-program satisfaction rating above the prior 4.6 out of 5. She also had to decide what the offered second discovery call was for: confirming the scope and dates Northline had already assumed, interviewing line managers and the CHRO on where decisions stalled, or skipping it outright to protect the ninety-day calendar. Beneath all three sat a plainer question: how completely to rule out simply repeating last year's course.

The second block was format. Northline's menu ran from a fully bespoke, faculty-led series of in-person cohorts — the richest option, near C$6,800 a head and about 120 days to build — to a blend of online modules, live workshops and exercises built around the restructuring, near C$4,600 a head and about 80 days; alongside those sat simulation-led decision labs on their own, near C$5,200 a head and about 70 days, engaging but apart from the rest of the design, and a cohort-based online program alone, the cheapest and fastest choice at roughly C$2,400 a head and about 45 days, though the lightest touch (see **Exhibit 2**). She could add a full manager-as-coach cadence for the rest of the quarter; a lighter package of a toolkit plus one follow-up; or no reinforcement, ending the program at its last session. She had to set, as a percentage, how much material came from Northline's catalogue rather than being built for Cascadia, and whether to serve the full 150 managers, as Cascadia had asked; run a pilot of 90, keeping a richer format affordable; or trim the cohort to 120, protecting the per-head budget.

The third block was proof. Okafor had to decide how far her definition of success reached: stopping at whether managers enjoyed the sessions, as before; extending to whether they had also learned the material, tested directly; extending further to whether their on-the-job decision-making behaviour changed; or reaching all the way to a named business result from the restructuring. She had to decide when to take a starting measurement — before day one, capturing decision-speed and behaviour data; not at all, leaving only a picture reconstructed after the fact; or built afterward from memory and Cascadia's existing reports — and how to frame the headline number: a specific decision-speed lift, thirty percent, credited entirely to Northline's workshops; a baselined figure with the reorg's and new incentives' effect separated out via a control group; or a vaguer promise of "improved leadership effectiveness" with no number or baseline attached. Last, she had to agree how often results were reported and who held the data: one read-out at the end, owned by Northline alone, or a baseline, mid-point and quarter-end read-out shared with Cascadia's HR-analytics team.

The fourth block was the room. Facing the boutique's quote, Okafor could split the difference at CAD 640,000, trimming the measurement plan to make the number work; match the boutique at CAD 540,000, to win the number and protect the relationship; or hold at CAD 750,000 and make the case on the result the design would prove. She had to choose what led her three minutes with the CEO and CHRO — the baselined decision-speed result the CEO would see within the quarter, price and design argued as one case; the breadth and quality of the curriculum itself, built specifically for Cascadia's own managers; or Northline's reputation and its faculty roster, the strength of the brand that stood behind the room — and name the single biggest risk to delivery: none, on the view that a confident pitch should not volunteer weaknesses; faculty availability against the ninety days, mitigated by reuse and the blended design; or attribution itself, mitigated by the baseline and the control group. Whatever she chose, she had to say, in a number she would defend in the room, how firmly she stood behind pricing on outcomes rather than on cost.

By the end of the quarter, Northline would judge the engagement on four things: the margin left after design and delivery cost, whether the impact case was provable rather than asserted, whether the design could actually be delivered inside the budget and calendar, and how confident Cascadia was in renewing the account.

## Closing

Okafor closed the client file and wrote the CHRO's line across a blank page: lovely, and we have no idea what it did for us. Ninety days remained to launch. The boutique's CAD 540,000 sat unanswered in Cascadia's inbox, and the second discovery call was still an offer on the table.
