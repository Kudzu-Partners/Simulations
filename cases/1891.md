# Tessellate Labs: The Focus Decision

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1891 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1891 — *Commercialize It* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, June 16, 2026, Priya Sandhu opened the memo her advisory board had sent overnight and found one sentence underlined: "Optionality is now a liability." Below it the board set out what it would and would not do. It would not release the remaining C$90,000 of Tessellate Labs' federal innovation grant on the schedule the company had planned. It would release the money against a single commercialization plan, backed by evidence, presented in ninety days. "Pick the customer you will starve to serve," the lead advisor had written.

Sandhu had until September 14 to choose which market Tessellate Labs Inc. would sell into, how the founding team would spend the ninety days testing the beliefs the plan rested on, and what she would tell the board the next C$90,000 was buying. The company held C$420,000 in the bank and was spending C$30,000 a month.

## The Protagonist

Priya Sandhu, 33, was Tessellate's product co-founder and the name on the board's memo. She had spent most of her career in product roles at Toronto software firms, and had left the last of them after a launch that shipped on schedule to customers who did not want it. She met Émile Doucet, Tessellate's technical co-founder, on a consulting engagement inside an Ontario extrusion plant, where they had both watched a quality lead sort finished profiles by eye under a work lamp. Sandhu owned the commercial side of the company — pricing, customers, the board relationship, and the plan the board had just frozen the money against.

## The Decision

Sandhu had to decide which problem Tessellate was solving and in whose words, how the ninety days and the cash inside them were spent gathering evidence, and — on day ninety — one route to market she would defend before the board while naming what she had killed to fund it.

## The Company and its Setting

Tessellate Labs Inc. was incorporated in Toronto in January 2026 out of a university engineering lab. Its single asset was MosaicQA, a machine-vision model that detected micro-defects in extruded aluminium and polymer profiles from ordinary low-cost camera feeds mounted above a production line. Doucet had built the core model in eleven weeks using AI-assisted coding tools. The company was three people: Sandhu, Doucet, and a part-time advisor. It had a working prototype, two unpaid pilot conversations, and no paying customers.

The C$420,000 came from a friends-and-family round and a federal innovation grant, C$90,000 of which remained undrawn. At C$30,000 a month, the balance bought roughly fourteen months (see **Exhibit 1**).

Six months of exploratory work had produced four candidate markets and no decision between them: aluminium extruders, automotive-parts quality assurance, food-packaging inspection, and a horizontal "defect API" any manufacturer could call. None had been tested with a buyer. Meanwhile a US-funded entrant had raised US$4 million to sell general-purpose visual inspection.

## The Situation

Engineering capacity was not the constraint. A model built in eleven weeks could be extended in weeks more. What Tessellate lacked was evidence about who would pay, for what, and why.

The board's complaint was arithmetic. Of roughly twenty beliefs the venture depended on — who had the pain, how acute it was, what they would pay, who signed the cheque, whether the model held its accuracy in a dusty plant at line speed — three had been tested with a real buyer. The board treated the other seventeen as unpriced risk and had begun scoring it. On the board's own index, where zero was a fully evidenced plan and one hundred was hope, Tessellate stood at 60.

The board had also formed a view about the ideas. Across two quarters the founders had circulated the same three feature concepts, and the board rated the shortlist 2 on a five-point scale of how non-obvious it would look to a well-informed rival. It said the bar was 4, and warned that generative tools used without a filter hand every founder running the same prompt the same confident, generic output.

Doucet wanted to spend C$120,000 — four months of runway — hardening MosaicQA into a finished product. Sandhu had carved out C$25,000 and four weeks of the ninety, to be spent in full on evidence, against the menu in **Exhibit 2**. Every week spent building rather than selling consumed about C$7,000 of cash.

If nothing changed, the outcome was not dramatic. Four markets would keep splitting three people's attention, seventeen assumptions would stay untested, the tranche would stay frozen, and the burn would run at C$30,000 a month against a rival with US$4 million.

## What Sandhu Controlled

She controlled the words first. Three versions of the problem statement sat in her notes. One described what the model did: MosaicQA detected micro-defects from low-cost camera feeds. One described what the team could do: ship an inspection product faster than anyone, because the model had taken eleven weeks. One described what a plant's quality lead was measured on: defect escape rates, with no affordable way of catching micro-defects at line speed.

She controlled the sequence. Doucet's C$120,000 build could go first; a smaller build could run alongside the assumption work; or the assumption ledger could be written first, ranked by impact-if-wrong against the evidence in hand, with no build money committed until it was. And she controlled how many of the four markets Tessellate carried forward — all four, or any fewer, down to one.

She controlled how the team generated its options. It could use the model to open the option space wide and then cut the list by hand; draw up a shortlist by hand and have the model attack it; have the model play the buyer and raise objections before any interview money was spent; or run one prompt for a large batch and take the model's own top five. Alongside that she had to state what the humans on the team contributed — nothing beyond speed; sharper prompts and cleaner packaging; or knowledge of the plant floor that told them which option every founder with the same prompt had also produced — and set the novelty bar, on the board's one-to-five scale, that the shortlist of three had to clear.

She controlled the C$25,000. It could be spread across the five lines in Exhibit 2 in any mix, provided it totalled exactly C$25,000, and it could be concentrated on the one market she would stake the company on, split across two, or divided evenly at C$6,250 across all four. She also had to decide how the team would treat evidence that contradicted the deck it had already built. The buyer's identity was itself one of the untested beliefs: both unpaid pilot conversations had been with plant owners, and nobody had established whether the owner or someone reporting to them held the inspection budget. Sandhu could hold the original assumption, log a contrary finding and revisit it after the first deal closed, or rewrite the value proposition around the new evidence and restate the ledger.

On day ninety she controlled the commitment. One route: direct paid pilots sold plant by plant to extruders; an embed into a camera or machine vendor's product, reaching many plants through someone else's sales force; or a self-serve defect API any manufacturer could buy without a conversation. She could also carry all three forward. She had to set a time to first shippable product between two and twenty weeks, name the milestone the next C$90,000 was buying — a shipped first product, ten thousand signups and a million API calls, or three paying pilots at C$5,000 monthly recurring revenue by a named date — and decide how hard to defend the two routes she was killing when the board asked why.

Four numbers would follow her into the room: months of runway remaining at the C$30,000 burn; how many of the twenty critical assumptions had been tested with a real buyer; the novelty score of the shortlist against the board's bar of 4; and the board's venture-risk index, where lower was better.

## Closing

At 9:15 that morning Sandhu wrote the four market names on the whiteboard in the company's one room and put a dollar figure beside each: the cost of proving it. Doucet came in at 9:40 with the build estimate reprinted and asked her which one she was erasing first.
