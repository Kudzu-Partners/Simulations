# Vértice Consultoria: Proving the Return on People

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1894 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1894 — *Analytics-Led HR* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, June 16, 2026, Marina Salgado came out of Vértice Consultoria's half-year review with one page of notes and a date. Beatriz Nakamura, the firm's chief financial officer, had spent forty minutes of the meeting on a single line of the accounts: BRL 22 million a year of human-resources programme spend — training, engagement initiatives, wellbeing benefits, retention payments — against which five years of records contained nothing that tied any of it to a business number the finance function recognised. Nakamura froze the line flat for the coming year.

The date was ninety days out. In that time Salgado had to show that one intervention, chosen from the firm's own workforce data, returned more in Brazilian reais than it cost. If she could not, the budget would be cut by 15% at the next cycle and the balance reallocated to the practices.

## The Protagonist

Marina Salgado, 43, had been HR director of Vértice Consultoria for eleven weeks. She had come from a Brazilian retail group, where she ran compensation and workforce planning through a two-year restructuring. Vértice's chief executive had recruited her on an explicit mandate: stop administering and start deciding. She reported to him. Her budget was signed by Nakamura.

## The Decision

Salgado had to settle four things in sequence: what question the firm's people data would be asked, which single programme would be funded to answer it, how the effect would be measured, and what she would say when finance tested the number in front of the executive committee.

## The Company and its Setting

Vértice Consultoria was founded in 2004 and headquartered in São Paulo, delivering technology and management consulting to large Brazilian corporates. It employed 4,200 people, most of them billable consultants, and booked BRL 980 million of revenue in 2025. The firm sold hours; its workforce was the product.

Human resources had run as an administrative function since the founding: payroll, labour compliance, an annual engagement survey presented to the board and filed. Five years of engagement responses, performance ratings, promotion histories, manager spans and project-staffing records sat in a warehouse that had never been queried to make a decision, and in that period the function had launched no programme designed from its own data.

## The Situation

Voluntary turnover among consultants in the firm's top two performance bands had reached 24%, against a Brazilian professional-services benchmark near 15%. That was roughly 400 departures a year the firm had not wanted. Finance costed each one at BRL 180,000 in recruitment, ramp-up and lost billings — about BRL 72 million a year, more than three times the entire frozen programme budget (see **Exhibit 1**).

The executive committee had an account of the cause: engagement. The last survey had come in below the prior year, and it was the only firm-wide measure the committee had; "we have an engagement problem" was how the board pack put it. Line managers had a different account, which they gave freely and often: HR did not understand billability, and whatever HR shipped would cost them chargeable hours.

Four programmes competed for the frozen envelope (see **Exhibit 2**). Together they came to BRL 19 million against the BRL 22 million line, so the envelope covered all four outright and still had room for a design that compared treated groups against untreated ones, which came out of the same money. What it could not buy was all four and an answer to which of them had worked.

Camila Duarte, the finance business partner assigned to HR, had done one calculation that Salgado kept in front of her. Spread across roughly 400 regretted departures a year, each percentage point taken off the segment's attrition rate was worth about BRL 3 million a year avoided.

Nakamura ran the review from a standing template. Four readings sat at the top of it, three scored from 0 to 100 by finance and the business-unit leaders jointly, and one in reais. *Insight quality* recorded how far HR's stated problem was a specific, decidable one rather than a screen of metrics; it stood at 12. *Causal confidence* recorded whether an effect HR claimed could be told apart from the market, the economy and luck; it stood at 8. *Manager adoption* recorded whether line managers actually used what HR shipped; it stood at 15. The fourth was the net annual return in reais — value of departures avoided, less what was spent — and it stood at zero, against BRL 0 committed of the BRL 22 million.

Nothing in the position was moving on its own. The 24% rate had risen in each of the last three years. On the current trajectory the BRL 72 million recurred, the four programmes carried on being funded in fractions as they had been since 2021, and at the next cycle the line came down 15% and went to the practices.

## What Salgado Controlled

She controlled the analysis first. Four readings of the same data were defensible: that the loss sat in a narrow group of roughly 600 top-rated consultants; that it sat everywhere, as a firm-wide engagement deficit across all 4,200 people; that it sat with first-year joiners, where churn was expected; or that it sat with anyone paid below the São Paulo market rate for their grade. She could name the factors that travelled with regretted exits as sustained over-utilisation combined with low manager review scores, as engagement scores, as tenure clustering around the third year, or as no consistent pattern at all. And she set how much of her ninety days went into separating what merely moved together from what made anything happen.

She then controlled the framing. The brief could lead with cutting regretted attrition among high performers on over-utilised projects under low-scoring managers; with raising engagement across the firm; with bringing the headline turnover number to the 15% benchmark; or with specifying the twelve metrics a new HR dashboard would display. Against it she would fund one of the four programmes, state the mechanism she was claiming — that removing the driver would stop the departures, that raising engagement would raise retention, or that money would hold people who would otherwise leave — and decide how early to put manager adoption risk on the table rather than in a footnote.

She controlled the money and the proof. She could stagger the rollout and compare treated business units against units not yet treated, compare the whole firm against last year, or run no comparison and report the attrition number as it came. She could fund the one chosen programme plus a measurement design and defund the rest, fund the two cheapest programmes plus a design, or split the envelope thinly across all four. She had to name a target reduction in the segment's attrition rate, in percentage points off 24%, and decide how much of a low-base-high range to show finance alongside it.

Last, she controlled the rollout. Adoption could be built by showing managers the programme protected their billable teams and their staffing, by a mandatory training session, by a mandate from the executive committee with an audit of managers who did not follow it, or by issuing the programme and letting the analysis carry itself. She would track either utilisation balance and manager-quality scores inside the target group, or the annual attrition figure alone twelve months out, or attendance and satisfaction from the programme itself. When Nakamura pressed, she would defend one intervention with a comparison group and a range around the return, or a spread of spending on the grounds that something in it would work, or the survey finding that engaged consultants stayed. And she would decide how much of the ninety days to spend building HR's own capacity to do this again.

## Closing

At 7:20 the following morning Salgado put the warehouse query log on one side of her desk and the four programme costs on the other. Duarte's number was in the middle: BRL 3 million a year for every point. Nakamura's template had four readings on it and three of them were under 20. Ninety days.
