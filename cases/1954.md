# Thames Leadership Studio: The Britannia Severn Brief

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1954 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1954 — *Design the Programme* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, May 12, 2026, Freya Bramwell read two documents about the same company and found that they did not name the same problem. The first was her interview transcript with Alistair Renshaw, the Britannia Severn plc board member sponsoring the work: a group that decided too slowly and did not talk across itself, eighteen months after a merger and eleven months after a regulatory investigation. The second was the 360-degree feedback and psychometric summary for the client's 120 senior leaders. It named three things — leaders rated high on technical judgement and low on awareness of their own impact; business units that ran their own numbers and did not lead together; and a senior group that either froze or took personal control when the picture was unclear.

Bramwell had ninety days. On Monday, August 10, 2026, she would put a costed nine-month programme for those 120 leaders in front of Renshaw and the Britannia Severn board, inside a delivery budget of GBP 600,000 the client had set as a ceiling, not a target. Renshaw had already told her how he intended to judge it. "At the nine-month review I will ask one question," he had said, in the meeting she had just transcribed. "Did these leaders lead differently?"

## The Protagonist

Freya Bramwell, 45, had been Executive Education Director of Thames Leadership Studio since 2021, after nine years in the Studio as a designer and client director. Two engagements sat behind her judgement. A 2023 design for a retail bank closed with the highest participant ratings the Studio had recorded; the client's people review, a year later, could not distinguish it from no programme at all. A 2024 design for an insurer, built entirely around real-work projects and coaching, the cohort called relentless and the client did not renew. Her name went on the Britannia Severn design either way, and the Studio's client directors had already put the follow-on work into the forecast.

## The Decision

Bramwell had to settle four things before the August gate, and each narrowed the next. Which capability gaps the programme would target. How the nine months would be weighted across the Studio's three development elements. How GBP 600,000 would be divided across delivery formats for 120 leaders. And what she would say to Renshaw, and commit to measure, when the board asked her to prove any of it.

## The Company and its Setting

Thames Leadership Studio was the custom executive-education practice of a London business school, founded in 2009. It built each programme with the client rather than selling from a catalogue: roughly 35 engagements a year, about GBP 9 million of revenue at a margin near 28%, and some 120 contracted faculty, facilitators and coaches (see **Exhibit 1**).

Its method rested on three development elements a design was expected to weight deliberately. Self-awareness — 360 feedback, psychometrics, reflective practice, individual coaching — built a leader's insight into their own impact. Collective leadership through teams — cross-business project teams, real-work assignments, peer challenge — built the capacity to lead across boundaries. Navigating complexity — simulations, scenario work, systems and pressure labs — built judgement under ambiguity.

Britannia Severn plc was a FTSE-250 infrastructure and utilities group. The merger that formed it had been difficult, and the regulatory investigation that followed had put its decision-making under scrutiny. Its 120 senior leaders sat across businesses that had been separate companies eighteen months earlier.

## The Situation

The brief's parameters were fixed. One hundred and twenty leaders. Nine months. GBP 600,000, signed off at a ninety-day gate and examined at a nine-month impact review.

The arithmetic of the formats made it hard (see **Exhibit 2**). One-to-one coaching ran at GBP 1,800 to 2,400 per leader for a full set of sessions, or roughly GBP 250,000 for all 120. Action-learning projects carried a high facilitation cost per team and ran only as fast as facilitators allowed. Immersive labs cost most of their money once, in design, then ran cheaply for each later cohort. The version the Studio's resourcing lead had costed with every deep format for every leader came to about GBP 840,000 — roughly 40% above the ceiling.

Three colleagues had taken positions. Dev Mistry, the lead programme designer, argued that nothing in the feedback data moved without individual coaching, and that a design spread across five formats would do all of them thinly. Cordelia Vance, the client relationship director, argued that Renshaw's words were the brief, and that real-work projects landed in a company mid-restructure as extra work on a day job. Ntombi Achebe, who ran the Studio's evaluation practice, argued that no measurement she could build would survive being added after the programme was sold.

Bramwell also knew the design the Studio submitted most often: masterclasses for all 120, an even spread across the three elements, an offsite, and a satisfaction survey on the last day. It passed gates. It was the retail bank design.

## What Bramwell Controlled

She controlled the diagnosis first. She could name as the primary gap collective leadership across the merged businesses, or self-awareness among leaders whose feedback showed little insight into their own impact, or the group's capacity to navigate complexity, or all three at once. She could trace each presenting symptom back through the 360 and psychometric evidence to an underlying gap before naming a single activity; or start from a menu of activities the client would recognise and select against the symptoms; or take Renshaw's own account of what the group needed and build to it. And she could name one lower-priority gap the programme would not close, or promise the sponsor all three.

She controlled the architecture: what share of the nine months went to each of the three elements, the shares summing to the whole. And the order they ran in — self-awareness, then collective work, then complexity integration, each building on the last; or everything front-loaded into one intensive offsite week with the remaining months left open; or no fixed order, sequencing each element as faculty and cohort diaries allowed.

She controlled the money: five format lines to fund out of GBP 600,000 — masterclasses, action-learning projects, one-to-one coaching, immersive labs, and peer coaching with digital reinforcement. Where a deep format would not stretch to 120, she could reserve it for where she judged the return greatest and scale the rest through cohorts, peer work and digital; or give every leader every deep format and go to the client for the difference; or fund the broad-reach formats for all 120 and return the balance of the budget.

And she controlled the room in August. She could open with the diagnosis and the logic tying each format to a gap, or with what the cohort would experience — the speakers, the venues and the nine months as the leaders would meet them. She could promise manager-rated behaviour change, a re-test 360 and project outcomes at nine months; or a knowledge test after each module, marked and reported; or a participant-satisfaction survey on the last day, reported against the Studio's benchmark. When Renshaw pressed her, as he had said he would, to "just run some inspiring keynotes for all 120," she could hold the design, rebuild it around keynotes, or fund the keynotes out of the project budget. And she chose how firmly, from nought to one hundred, to state her conviction.

The Studio's review board scored designs on four measures, each from 0 to 100. Capability uplift estimated how much of the diagnosed gap the programme would close. Transfer to practice estimated behaviour change back in the business rather than reaction in the room. Sponsor confidence recorded how far the board believed the design would work at nine months. Budget discipline fell with overspend, with money left unspent, and with spending that could not be tied to a gap. An unstarted design entered at 15, 15, 20 and 50.

## Closing

At 9:20 that morning Bramwell wrote three words on a sheet of paper — *self, together, ambiguity* — and put a blank column beside each. The gate was ninety days out; the review that Renshaw cared about was nine months after that. She had GBP 600,000, 120 leaders, and a sponsor who had told her in advance which question he would ask.
