# Constructora Andina Sanín: The Friday Recommendation

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-831 · **Companion simulation:** Express 831 — *Edificio Quindio: Critical Path and Fast-Tracking* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, April 21, 2026, at 07:10, Mariana Rocha Villamizar, project manager for Edificio Quindío, opened an email that the general manager of Constructora Andina Sanín had forwarded to her without comment. The building's owner had found a buyer for the entire fourth floor who wanted to move in by November. The owner now wanted substantial completion at week 32 instead of the contracted week 38, and wanted to know by Friday whether that could be done and what it would cost.

The project was in its twelfth week. Rocha had until the steering committee at 10:00 on Friday, April 24, to recommend whether Andina should accept the owner's date, make a counter-offer or decline and hold the contract date, and to put a number on what that recommendation would cost the company.

## The Protagonist

Rocha was thirty-six, a civil engineer with eleven years on Bogotá building sites, the last four at Andina. She had run three residential buildings. Edificio Quindío was the first project on which management had insisted on a fully baselined critical-path schedule and weekly variance reporting. She knew the scheduling software well. She had never had to defend a compressed schedule to an owner's finance director.

## The Decision

Before Friday Rocha had to work through four steps in order. She had to confirm that the baseline schedule was what the planning file said it was. She had to read the week-12 progress report, due that afternoon, and decide what it meant and who should hear about it. She had to choose a target week and a set of compression measures from the menu her site team had priced (see Exhibit 2). Then she had to lock the recommendation and the contractual terms that went with it. Anything said to the owner or committed to a subcontractor along the way could not be taken back.

## The Company and its Setting

Constructora Andina Sanín S.A.S. was a Bogotá general contractor founded in 2008, with fourteen active projects in Cundinamarca and Boyacá and revenue of about COP 86,000 million in 2025. It employed 38 staff and some 210 subcontracted workers, and it specialised in residential and institutional buildings of four to eight storeys. Four of its last seven projects had finished late, by 11.3 weeks on average, and that record had started to cost it places on public-tender shortlists.

Edificio Quindío was a six-storey building with 48 apartments and a contract value of COP 14,200 million (see Exhibit 1). Its 38-week baseline held 142 activities in four phases: foundation; structure; envelope and mechanical, electrical and plumbing work; and finishes. The critical path ran through structure, envelope and the wet finishes.

## The Situation

The contract set a penalty of COP 35 million for every week of delay beyond week 38. The week-32 date was not in the contract; accepting it would create an exposure that did not yet exist. To make it attractive, the owner had offered an early-completion bonus of COP 180 million, payable only at substantial completion by week 32 and nothing at week 33 or later. The construction loan that financed the works carried a covenant tying monthly draws to the baseline spending curve, and a sharp acceleration in spending could trip it. The contract also required every status report to the owner to include the schedule performance index.

The site superintendent, Wilson Patiño, had answered the email before Rocha finished reading it. "We can't do 32," he wrote. "Maybe 35. And only if we accept overlap in the wet trades." He had also warned her, the previous Thursday, that structural concrete on floors 2 and 3 was running behind after the rebar supplier short-delivered in week 9. The earned-value report for week 12 would show by how much.

There was a second clock. Another contractor was trying to hire Andina's formwork crew for a project starting in week 16, and subcontractors needed two weeks' notice to expand any crew.

The pressure ran both ways. The general manager, Hernán Sanín Duque, believed a flat refusal would cost the owner's next three referrals. The cost controller, Diana Castro, pointed out that a commitment that missed would cost the crash premium and the late penalty together. The owner's finance director, Paula Andrea Giraldo, read every status report herself.

If nothing changed, Andina would keep building to the 38-week baseline, the fourth-floor buyer would not move in by November, and, depending on what the progress report showed, the 38-week date might itself be at risk.

## What Rocha Controlled

The first step was the **baseline check**. Rocha had to sign off four figures: the total duration of the critical path, in weeks; the average total float on the non-critical chains, in days; how many activities were coded under the wrong phase of the work breakdown structure; and how many near-critical chains, with five days or less of total float, the network contained. The planning file's summary page carried figures for all of them, but nobody had rechecked them since January.

The second step was the **week-12 report**. She had to estimate the cost at completion, in millions of pesos, assuming the remaining work performed at the current cost performance index. She had to decide which chain had become critical after the slip: still structure, the wet finishes, the mechanical and electrical rough-in, or the foundation. She had to decide how the owner would hear about the slip: flagged separately before the acceleration plan, folded into the acceleration narrative, or left out, with only the recovery figures reported. And she had to settle the formwork crew: commit extra work now to keep it, let it go and find a new crew later, or negotiate a one-week retention bonus and decide on Friday.

The third step was **compression**. She had to pick a target completion week: 38, recovering only the slip; 35; 34; 33; or the owner's 32. She could then apply any combination of the nine measures on the menu. Crashing added crews, overtime or prefabrication at a direct cost. Fast-tracking overlapped activities that had been sequential, at no direct cost but with an estimated chance of rework.

The fourth step was **Friday**. She would recommend going ahead with week 32 as proposed, making a counter-offer at week 33 or 34 on re-priced terms, or declining and recovering the slip to hold week 38. On the penalty clause she could renegotiate, deferring the start of penalties to week 40 with a force-majeure carve-out for the rebar slip; accept the COP 35 million a week against the new date; or not raise it. On the bonus she could ask to pro-rate it, full at week 32 and partial at weeks 33 and 34; keep the all-or-nothing structure; or drop the bonus in exchange for a clean schedule with no acceleration premium. On the bank she could call pre-emptively with the revised spending curve and ask for covenant relief if a breach was forecast, wait until the covenant tripped, or say nothing.

Rocha's war-room board tracked the **forecast completion** in weeks against the 38-week baseline; the **acceleration spend**, direct crash cost plus expected rework, in millions of pesos; the **net profit-and-loss impact**, bonus minus penalty minus acceleration spend; the **owner's trust** in Andina, on a scale of 0 to 100; and a **risk index** for overlap and covenant exposure. The steering committee would score each of the four steps out of 100 and average the scores at the end.

## Closing

At 07:40 Rocha printed the Gantt chart and spread it across the site-office table. Patiño appeared at the door with the rebar delivery notes and said the formwork foreman had called him twice since six. She wrote "38?" at the top of the whiteboard and started the forward pass.
