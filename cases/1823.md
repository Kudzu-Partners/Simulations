# Northgate Systems: Recharging the Platform Engineering Division

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1823 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1823 — *Fully Charged* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 14 September 2026, Grace Fenwick walked out of Northgate Systems Ltd's quarterly board meeting with a single instruction from the chair ringing in her ears: fix the energy of her division within twelve weeks, or the board would take it apart and hand its work to outside providers. Northgate, a Reading, England-based enterprise-software company with 1,400 staff and £240 million in annual revenue, had been bought by a private-equity group the previous year, and its appointed board had just walked her through a dashboard she already knew by heart. Her 320-person Platform Engineering division — the team that built and ran the product every other part of Northgate depended on — was logging more hours than any other group in the company and reporting less energy for the work than any of them. It was, in the board's own words, spending more and delivering less.

Fenwick had twelve weeks, a one-time budget of £600,000, and roughly enough of her own attention, the board reckoned, to properly fund and personally stand behind two initiatives — not five. She had to decide, in stages, what she was looking at, where she wanted the division to end up, which two moves would carry her name, and how she would prove to the board that whatever she achieved by the twelve-week mark would last.

## The Protagonist

Grace Fenwick, 45, had led Platform Engineering for years before the acquisition and was one of the few senior leaders the new owners had asked to remain. She had led it through the period the rest of the company still called its best work, and had watched the mood curdle since into something she could measure but not yet name with confidence. Her own standing with the new board depended on what she did with the next twelve weeks.

## The Decision

Across four stages, Fenwick had to make a connected set of choices rather than one big one. First, she had to read the data and decide what kind of problem she actually had — not the volume of hours worked, but the character of the energy behind them. Second, she had to decide where she wanted the division to end up, which broad approach to lead with, whether she was, on balance, removing demands from the team's plate or adding to them, and how many initiatives she meant to personally sponsor. Third, she had to commit money and her own sponsorship to exactly two initiatives out of several available, choose the order she introduced them in, and decide how firmly to protect any reduction in workload if new demands later pushed against it. Finally, she had to decide how to make any improvement outlast her own attention, how closely to monitor for a recovery that looked real but was not, and how candidly to describe the result when the board asked her to defend it.

## The Company and its Setting

Northgate Systems Ltd made and sold enterprise software from its headquarters in Reading, England. Across the wider company, 1,400 people generated £240 million in annual revenue. Platform Engineering, at 320 people, built and maintained the core product that every other part of Northgate sold on top of — the division the rest of the business could least afford to lose. Since the private-equity acquisition, a reorganisation had left the division with friction it had not had before and a sense that autonomy it once had was gone. Cynicism and blame had followed, along with a marked drop in the trust the team had in its own leadership.

## The Situation

The numbers the board had shown Fenwick were specific (Exhibit 1). Engagement inside the division had fallen from 78% to 54%, against a company-wide average of about 72%. Forty-one percent of the division's staff now reported high exhaustion, compared with 19% company-wide, and average overtime stood at 9.2 hours a week. An internal diagnostic placed the division's productive-energy score — a 0–100 measure the board wanted to see climbing past 60 — at 38. Regretted attrition, the share of departures the company had not wanted, was running at 18% a year against a company average of 9%, and each departure cost Northgate roughly £95,000 to replace. On-time delivery had slipped from 94% to 81%. If none of this moved by the twelve-week mark, the board's own words left little room for interpretation: the division would be broken up and its work sent outside the company.

## What Fenwick Controlled

Fenwick's first decision was how to characterise what the data showed. It could support more than one reading: a workforce working at high intensity but carrying a negative undercurrent of politics, blame and internal friction; a team simply exhausted from giving too much of itself to work it still cared about; a quieter picture of low intensity and withdrawal, people disengaging rather than overworking; or a calm, largely content division that had merely grown a little complacent. She also had to read intensity and quality as two separate questions — was activity itself high or low, and was the feeling behind it positive or negative — and decide how strictly her team would judge the second, harder-to-observe question rather than simply counting hours.

Next she had to choose a destination and a route. The destination could be a state combining high intensity with a positive, joint-effort quality; a calmer state reached by deliberately lowering intensity so people could recover; or simply more output, with energy following results. The route could lead with uniting the group against a shared external threat, with a new vision for the platform communicated through town halls, or with both at once and at full intensity. She also had to set, on a sliding scale, whether her plan was a net reduction or addition to the team's workload, and decide how many initiatives — from one to eight — she would shortlist for her own sponsorship.

The initiatives themselves numbered seven, and she could commit to exactly two (Exhibit 2): rebuilding the leadership team and resolving the politics left from the reorganisation, at £60,000; removing layers of meetings and restoring the team's autonomy, at £40,000; targeted coaching for the division's top eight leaders, at £80,000; a new platform vision carried through town halls, at £60,000; hiring two additional engineers to relieve the workload directly, at £190,000; a package of wellbeing measures — a meditation app and a wellness week — at £20,000; and a further reorganisation of the division's structure, at £120,000. Beyond choosing the pair, she had to decide which one she introduced first, how much of the £600,000 to commit to the two together, and how firmly to protect any workload reduction if pressure later mounted to reverse it.

Finally, she had to decide how to make any gain last beyond her own twelve weeks of attention: by embedding it through protected autonomy, lighter meetings and named owners; by continuing to rally the team with more town halls and urgency; or by accepting sustained overtime, with high morale carrying it. She had to decide how closely to watch for the possibility that a rising energy score was really strain in disguise — by tracking energy together with overtime and attrition, by watching the energy score alone, or without ongoing monitoring between reports. And when the board chair questioned her at the twelve-week mark, she had to decide whether to answer with a full, evidenced account, to point to the single strongest reading, or to ask for a much longer timeframe instead — and how firmly to hold that answer under questioning.

## Closing

Fenwick closed the board deck and opened the division's latest pulse survey instead. She had twelve weeks, £600,000, and enough of her own attention to carry two initiatives properly. She had not yet decided what she was looking at, where she wanted the division to end up, which two initiatives would carry her name, or what she would tell the chair when the twelve weeks were over.
