# Meridian Cloud Systems: The Character Audit

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1834 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1834 — *The Character Audit* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 3 February, Meredith Osei, People Director at Meridian Cloud Systems, read two files left on her desk overnight: a skip-level complaint against a member of Daniel Vos's sales team, and a compliance note flagging a client discount that had bypassed required sign-off. Vos, the SVP of Enterprise Sales, had beaten his quota for eleven consecutive quarters and personally held the client relationships behind 38% of the company's new annual recurring revenue — roughly CA$26 million. On the same desk, in the same week, he was also the most serious governance problem in the building.

The Chief Executive gave Osei three weeks to turn the complaint and the compliance flag into a formal leadership review and a recommendation to the board's People and Compensation Committee. Vos's promotion to Chief Revenue Officer — a role that would put all of Meridian's revenue under one person — sat on the same agenda. Whatever Osei recommended, the board would treat it as a considered answer; recommending nothing would read as one too.

## The Protagonist

Osei, 44, had joined Meridian as People Director in 2023 to build a talent function for a company that had scaled faster than its people practices. Her career had run through fast-growing technology companies, mostly in roles built around measuring things — engagement, retention, leadership pipelines — that others treated as intuition, not data. She had never had to build a case that could cost the company its largest revenue relationship, or one that could just as easily hand that relationship more authority.

## The Decision

Before recommending anything, Osei had to decide what she was looking at. The evidence pack — 360-degree verbatims, exit-interview notes, peer and skip-level comments, the discount file, and Vos's commercial record — supported more than one reading. It could describe a character imbalance: real drive and real courage, running with no brake because temperance, humility, justice and collaboration weren't holding them in check. It could describe a flat character deficit: a leader simply low across the board. It could describe a competence gap: someone lacking the skills a bigger role would need. Or it could describe a motivation problem: a leader who would behave differently under different incentives.

Within whichever reading she chose, she then had to name which traits were over-played — drive and appetite for risk, running unchecked; or, on the opposite reading, an excess of deference and fairness blocking results; or the possibility that nothing about him was over-played and he was simply a strong, well-rounded leader. She also had to say which traits the evidence might show were deficient: temperance, humility, collaboration and justice together, read as recklessness, arrogance and harm to colleagues; or integrity alone, treating the discount deal as outright dishonesty; or transcendence and humanity, framing the problem as a leader who simply lacked vision and warmth. Underneath those calls sat a fourth: how tightly each one tied to a cited behaviour rather than a general impression — rangeable from a loose read to a fully documented, dimension-by-dimension case.

## The Company and Its Setting

Meridian Cloud Systems Inc., headquartered in Waterloo, Ontario, sold a data and analytics platform to mid-market and large business customers across North America: CA$240 million in annual recurring revenue, 1,650 employees, privately held and roughly two years from an initial public offering — a horizon on which its governance of senior leaders would soon face outside scrutiny. Vos ran the Enterprise Sales division and had, for almost three years, been its most commercially successful executive.

## The Situation

Osei's scorecard for the review started in a bad place (see Exhibit 1): character-gap closure — how far the case had moved from a vague complaint toward a diagnosis specific enough to act on — stood at 12 out of 100; intervention fit — whether any plan would target the right traits, at the right scale, inside budget — stood at zero; retention risk — the chance that Vos, and the revenue behind him, left the company — stood at 70 out of 100, where lower was safer; and projected effectiveness lift — the expected gain in his leadership performance if development worked — stood at 15.

The numbers behind those measures were not encouraging: a voluntary regretted-attrition rate of 19% in Vos's division against a company average of 9%, an engagement score 22 points below average, and a discount file that was the first formally flagged breach on his record, not the first thing anyone had heard about his conduct. Osei had a single, fixed CA$120,000 to spend on whatever development she designed.

If Osei changed nothing — if the review produced no clearer diagnosis than "he has a character problem," and the promotion went forward on the strength of his number — the attrition and engagement gaps would stay exactly where they were, the discount breach would stand as the only precedent on record, and the 38% of new revenue Vos controlled personally would remain concentrated in one relationship, unexamined, three weeks before the board expected an answer.

## What the People Director Controlled

Osei could not develop ten traits in three weeks on one budget, so the review turned on prioritisation: temperance and humility together, as the brake and self-awareness that might curb the recklessness; temperance, humility and collaboration together, adding peer relationships as a third lever; all ten traits at once; or drive and courage themselves, doubling down on the qualities already driving his numbers. Alongside that came a headline recommendation: develop Vos but hold the CRO promotion pending evidence of change; develop and retain him with a concrete plan; manage him out now, on the view that his character would not change in time; or promote him on the strength of his commercial record. Regardless of that people decision, she had to set how hard to de-risk the 38% revenue concentration itself — succession planning, handing accounts to a team rather than one person, spreading relationships more broadly — a dial rangeable from token to thorough.

The intervention itself was one choice among several, each costed and aimed differently (see Exhibit 2): coaching, a structured 360-degree feedback and accountability cycle with a follow-up assessment, a stretch assignment with explicit conduct guardrails, a peer-shadowing and reverse-mentoring arrangement, or a combined package of coaching, a full 360, an offsite, a mentor and a leadership programme together. Whatever she picked, she had to commit a specific spend against the CA$120,000 cap, and decide how change would be measured: baseline indicators, a target, a six-month re-assessment and a defined consequence; a baseline and a review date without a stated consequence; or a general instruction to work on the trait, without a specified baseline, horizon or consequence. Partway through, Vos reacted defensively and mentioned, without quite saying it outright, that a competitor had approached him — and Osei had to decide whether to hold the plan as it stood, soften it and accelerate his promotion to keep him, or issue an ultimatum and accept that he might leave.

The review closed with the board. Osei had to frame the business case — as performance and governance data, citing attrition, engagement and compliance costs directly; as a values-and-culture argument; or by leading with the quota record and treating the character read as a footnote — and decide whether to build character assessment into Meridian's promotion process, keep an informal watch-list instead, or treat the review as a one-off with no process change. When the chair pushed back — "he hits his number, isn't this just HR softness?" — she had to defend the assessment as measurable and defensible, hedge and let the chair's judgment carry the room, or concede that the results outweighed the concerns. Throughout, she set her own conviction, from a light touch to full advocacy that character was a performance investment rather than a courtesy.

## Closing

At 8:10 a.m., with the complaint and the compliance flag still open on her screen, Osei opened a blank page and wrote Daniel Vos's name at the top, followed by the ten traits she would have to score against it. She had three weeks, CA$120,000, and a board that already knew what his number was.
