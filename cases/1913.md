# Daalhof Food Group: The Fourth Attempt

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1913 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1913 — *Appreciative Change* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Friday, 12 June 2026, Marieke van Rooij sat in a third-floor meeting room at Daalhof Food Group's Nijmegen headquarters and listened to Youssef Belkacem, chair of the works council, read out a sentence he had written down beforehand. The council would not endorse a fourth company-wide sustainability programme. It had voted for the 2030 targets and it stood by them. What it would not do again was assemble in a canteen and be shown a deck.

By 10 September van Rooij had to present a restart of that programme to Belkacem and to the chief executive, Bram Oosterhuis, who had appointed her to run it that same morning. She had ninety days, a change budget of EUR 6.0 million, and a limit — set by Oosterhuis in front of the council — of three company-wide engagement events for the whole period.

## The Protagonist

Marieke van Rooij, 44, had led organisational development at Daalhof since 2019. She joined the company in 2013 from a Rotterdam consultancy, first running leadership development across the four production sites, then the graduate scheme. She was not a sustainability specialist; the 2030 commitments belonged to Ingrid Sleutjes, the chief sustainability officer, and Sleutjes kept them. Van Rooij's own history with the file was more particular. She had sat on the design team of the second programme, the one wound down without announcement in 2023, and had written its engagement plan. For the ninety days she reported to Oosterhuis directly; her permanent line ran through human resources.

## The Decision

Van Rooij had to decide how the restart would be conducted. The targets were fixed, the evidence behind them was not disputed inside the company, and Sleutjes' technical roadmap had been reviewed twice by external auditors. What was open was method: how Daalhof would open the conversation, who would be in the room when it happened, who would choose which initiatives the company backed, and what would hold the effort together once van Rooij handed it back to the line.

She had to commit to that method in four steps between June and September (see **Exhibit 2**), and defend it on 10 September to Oosterhuis and Belkacem together. If they were not persuaded, the board's stated alternative was to hand the entire 2030 programme to an external firm.

## The Company and its Setting

Daalhof Food Group N.V., founded in 1958 as a farmers' cooperative and headquartered in Nijmegen, was a dairy and plant-based foods business with EUR 1.3 billion of revenue, 6,200 employees, four production sites, and a supplier network of roughly 1,900 dairy farms.

Its public 2030 commitments covered emissions across its own operations and its supply chain, food waste, and the share of the portfolio held by lower-impact plant-based lines. At the half-year review the programme was behind on all three interim milestones: emissions down 9% against an 18% target, food waste down 12% against 25%, and plant-based lines at 11% of the portfolio against a 20% gate (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two consequences were already quantified. Daalhof's syndicated debt carried margin ratchets tied to the emissions trajectory, worth EUR 28 million if the trajectory was missed. The largest retail customer's "net-zero supplier" listing, worth EUR 95 million of annual revenue, turned on the same numbers.

## The Situation

Three programmes had run in five years. Each opened the same way — an external deck, a new dashboard, and a full catalogue of what the company was doing wrong. Two were abandoned. The third was the one now stalling.

Four measures went to the executive committee each quarter, and van Rooij took all four as hers (see **Exhibit 1**). *Change engagement* was the share of staff answering favourably to the pulse survey's questions about change; it stood at 41% and had fallen in each of the last three quarters. Oosterhuis had named 60% as the level at which he would call the restart credible. *Adoption* was a 0-to-100 index of how far the new working practices had actually been taken up on the shop floor; it stood at 25 and had not been above 30 since the third programme launched. *Sustainability impact* was a 0-to-100 index of progress toward the 2030 targets, counted only where a practice was in live use rather than where it had been announced; it stood at 10. *Generativity* was the people-analytics team's 0-to-100 count of activity nobody had been told to do — ideas logged, pilots begun without a mandate, staff volunteering to run something. It stood at 12.

Two places in the company did not fit the picture. The Veghel site had cut its own food waste by 30% over two years, without a programme, using a scheme its shift leaders designed. And about forty farmer-suppliers had been running regenerative grazing trials with the agronomy team since 2024, comparing results among themselves. Neither had been written up.

If van Rooij changed nothing, the arithmetic was not in doubt. Engagement would continue its decline. Adoption would stay below 30, which meant the three 2030 measures would stay near 9%, 12% and 11%. The EUR 28 million and the EUR 95 million would both remain live, and on 10 September the board would take the decision she had been appointed to avoid.

## What van Rooij Controlled

Her first commitment, in June, was the opening: which of four ways of starting the conversation Daalhof would use, which of the successes already inside the company she would build on, and how plainly her team would say — to the executive committee, in writing — that what had failed was the method rather than the targets or the workforce's motivation. She recorded that last judgement as a percentage.

In July she designed the engagement itself: how far to carry the conversation before decisions were taken, who would be in the room, whether to run the events inside a single week or spread them across the remaining time, and how many of her three company-wide events to commit to it. Each event cost EUR 0.8 million and could not be recovered.

In August she chose the initiatives. Three questions: who selected them, on what basis they were ranked, and whether to spend any remaining events on further momentum sessions during rollout. She also set, again as a percentage, how much of the workforce's working time she would ring-fence against the demands of the rollout.

In September she committed to what would keep the effort running afterwards, chose what evidence to bring, decided how to answer Belkacem and Oosterhuis when they asked whether ownership had genuinely moved, and set — on the same percentage scale she had used in June — how firmly she would defend her own contention that work people chose for themselves would outlast work handed to them. The options open to her at each step are set out in **Exhibit 2**.

## Closing

At six in the evening on 12 June, van Rooij wrote four dates on a single sheet: the opening, the engagement design, the initiative selection, and the September presentation. Under them she drew three empty boxes for the three events. Ninety days, EUR 6.0 million, three convenings, four numbers that had to move, and a hearing on 10 September in front of Oosterhuis and Belkacem.
