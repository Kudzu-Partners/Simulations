# Lusitânia Logística: Earning Trust Through Change

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1790 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1790 — *Earning Trust Through Change* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 5 March 2026, Mariana Teixeira walked out of a board meeting in Porto with a mandate she had known was coming and a deadline that tightened her stomach anyway. Lusitânia Logística's two largest e-commerce clients, together worth 40% of the group's revenue, had tied a further EUR 30 million of business to a new shift system and a warehouse automation line being fully operational by 30 September 2026 — seven months out. As General Manager, Teixeira would have to decide, in stages across those seven months, how to diagnose why her workforce no longer believed what leadership told it, how to announce a change that would touch roughly 180 of the group's 1,900 operational roles, what she was willing to stake her own credibility on, and how to hold her ground if the announcement blew up in public.

## The Protagonist

Teixeira, 44, had run operations at Lusitânia for six years before the board named her General Manager in January 2026, three months after her predecessor left. She had not made the promise that broke her workforce's trust — a profit-share scheme that a previous management team announced and then, eight months before this story opens, quietly cancelled — but she now occupied the chair that promise had emptied, and the board expected her to answer for it regardless of who had made it. Engagement scores at Lusitânia had been flat or falling for two years, and warehouse staff cited the cancelled profit-share, unprompted, as proof that "management says one thing and does another." Teixeira's own bonus, like every senior manager's, was tied to the automation rollout landing on schedule.

## The Decision

Over the months ahead, Teixeira would have to work through the problem in stages. First, before communicating anything, she needed to work out which part of her workforce's trust the broken promise had actually damaged — whether staff no longer believed leadership kept its word, doubted leadership's capability to pull off a hard change, or simply doubted that leadership cared about the people living through it — and how much time to spend investigating before acting on her answer. Second, she needed to choose how to build the announcement itself, whether to address the cancelled profit-share directly, and exactly what she was willing to promise. Third, she needed to settle on one concrete, costly gesture to back her words, and how much of the company's thin margin to commit to it. Fourth, when pressure peaked around the deadline, she needed to decide how to respond in real time to a workforce watching for the first sign that history was repeating itself.

## The Company and its Setting

Lusitânia Logística SA was a Portuguese third-party logistics operator headquartered in Porto, running warehousing and last-mile delivery for retail and e-commerce clients out of six sites across the country. It employed 2,600 people, of whom about 1,900 worked directly in warehouse and driving roles, and generated EUR 240 million in annual revenue at a 6% EBIT margin — EUR 14.4 million a year, thin even by logistics standards. Four of the six sites were unionized, and the works council carried real leverage: a walkout during peak e-commerce season could breach the service-level agreements that the two large clients were already watching closely.

## The Situation

The trust-in-leadership index — a 0–100 score built from a regular staff survey — stood at 38, well under the roughly 65 that Lusitânia's own historical data treated as healthy. Change adoption, the share of teams that had actually switched onto the new shift system on schedule, stood at 20%. Perceived ethicality, a 0–100 read on how consistent staff judged leadership's words to be against its verifiable actions, sat at 50. Turnover risk, a 0–100 measure of the danger of losing the people Lusitânia most needed to keep — where a lower score was better — stood at 55. The works council's strike ballot was already described internally as live, with the client service-level agreements flagged as at risk (see Exhibit 1).

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was not complicated: the shift system and the automation line would miss the 30 September date, the two clients holding 40% of revenue would move their volume elsewhere, and there was no reason for a trust score sitting at 38 to climb on its own. Teixeira did have a budget set aside for a credibility-repairing gesture — a funding line that opened, by default, at a suggested EUR 2,600 thousand, adjustable up to the company's entire EUR 14.4 million of annual EBIT, with an on-screen note flagging anything above roughly EUR 5,760 thousand — 40% of that EBIT — as pushing past a prudent band. Committing nothing to it was also an option.

## What Teixeira Controlled

Across the rollout, Teixeira's decisions fell into four groups. In the first, she named which single part of trust — capability, goodwill, or keeping one's word — she believed the broken promise had damaged most, and set how deeply she investigated the cause and the mood of different groups before acting, anywhere from a same-day judgment to a multi-week study.

In the second, she decided how the announcement itself would be built: one option led with the competitive case for the change and introduced the staffing impact later in the message; a second named the affected roles at the very outset, on the reasoning that the people whose jobs would move deserved the hardest facts first; a third brought the works council into shaping the announcement before any of it went out to the wider company. Alongside that choice, she decided whether to name the cancelled profit-share directly or leave it unmentioned, and she decided what to promise — committing only to what she was confident the business could deliver, which limited how much immediate relief the announcement offered and left her with no reassuring line for anyone who asked whether their own job was safe, or telling the workforce outright that nobody would lose a job, which offered relief at once even though the business case still called for roughly 180 roles to move.

In the third, she picked one gesture to back her words and set its funding: a binding commitment against compulsory layoffs, enforced by a financial penalty if broken; a visible sacrifice by the leadership team itself, such as a freeze on their own pay; a committee elected by the workforce to oversee how redeployment actually happened; a revived version of the cancelled profit-share, this time tied to the change's own success; or the strength of the communication alone, with no further resource behind it.

In the fourth, when a crisis broke around the deadline, she decided how to respond: stand by the plan and point back to whatever gesture she had already funded; offer the works council further concessions to defuse the situation; hold the line on the 30 September deadline itself, on the view that slipping it would cost the client relationships the change was meant to protect; or commit, on the spot, to a new gesture she had not previously planned for (see Exhibit 2).

## Closing

By the afternoon of 5 March, Teixeira had blocked two hours to draft the diagnosis her leadership team would work from the next morning. She had a survey reading 38 out of 100, a workforce that had heard a version of this promise before, a union with a ballot already in hand, and seven months to move 1,900 people onto a new way of working without losing the clients paying for it. She opened a blank page and wrote down three words: capability, goodwill, integrity. She had to choose one before she could choose anything else.
