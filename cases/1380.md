# Helio Verde Beverages: Fifteen Decisions Before the Contract

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1380 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1380 — *ERP Architecture Walkthrough — Order-to-Cash & Procure-to-Pay* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, May 11, 2026, at 07:40, Marisol Achával-Rendón connected her laptop to the wall screen in the third-floor meeting room of Helio Verde Beverages, S.A., and opened a demonstration tenant of SAP S/4HANA Cloud, the enterprise resource planning (ERP) suite in the system integrator's bid. Nine tiles across the top stood for the parts of the planned system, from sales to analytics; each decision would light the ones it touched.

The chief operating officer, Gonzalo Iturbe-Saldías, had given her five days. By 18:00 on Friday, May 15, he wanted a written blueprint: one sale and one purchase walked end to end through the new system, fifteen design decisions taken along the way, and an implementation recommendation. On Thursday, May 21, he would ask the board to sign a CO$ 8 million implementation contract on the strength of that document.

## The Protagonist

Achával-Rendón was forty-one, an industrial engineer, and twelve years into Helio Verde. She had run the sales-order desk and then the month-end close, and she knew every workaround between the company's four systems because she had built several of them. In April Iturbe-Saldías named her head of business processes and lead of the design team. She had never implemented an enterprise system. Her team had a process owner per function, an IT architect and a challenger: the integrator's engagement partner, Óscar Lindqvist-Paredes.

## The Decision

Achával-Rendón had to decide, fifteen times, how the integrated system would behave, and defend each answer to the people who would live with it. Three decisions laid the foundations. Six followed a customer order from entry to cash. Five followed a purchase from requisition to payment. The last settled who could do what in purchasing and how the programme would roll out. Each went into a design log in turn (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde Beverages was the soft-drinks division of the Helio Verde group in Costaria, where it held about 44% of carbonated soft-drink volume and employed 1,600 people. It was a single legal entity with one sales organisation and two bottling plants, Plant 1000 at Costaria Norte and a second plant at Costaria Sur. Its brands were Helio Cola, three citrus lines and a family of tonics. About 60% of sales went through supermarket and convenience chains, known as modern trade, and 40% through independent neighbourhood shops.

It ran four core systems: an in-house sales and order application from the late 2000s, a plant manufacturing and inventory package from 2014, a finance and general-ledger package from 2016, and a payroll system from 2019. They exchanged data through nightly batch files, spreadsheet exports and re-keying by hand.

## The Situation

Iturbe-Saldías's April memo had been blunt: *"We are operating four disconnected systems. Our month-end close is nine days. Our inventory is wrong by 8%. Our salespeople sell on credit terms our finance team cannot enforce in real time."* The commercial team's view of customer credit could be 48 hours old. Three finance analysts spent most of each week reconciling the systems. When Iturbe-Saldías asked for profitability by product, customer, channel and week, the answer took a fortnight of spreadsheet work (see Exhibit 1).

The board had approved CO$ 8 million for the programme. The integrator had priced two timetables: a single cutover after 14 months, and a 24-month roll-out in phases. The share for training, process redesign and adoption support had not been set.

The design team scored the blueprint on five readings, updated after every logged decision. ERP maturity, an overall index out of 100, stood at 25. Master-data quality, the degree to which customer, vendor and material records were clean and held once, stood at 50%. Process integration, how much of a transaction moved between functions without re-keying, stood at 20%. Audit compliance, how much of the control set the system itself would enforce, stood at 30%. Change management, how far adoption had been funded and planned, stood at 0%. The log recorded each step, the modules it touched and the maturity reading after it. At the end the blueprint would be graded Beginner, Solid, Good or Excellent.

If nothing changed, the four systems would keep running, the close would stay at nine days, the scorecard would stay where it stood, and the contract would reach the board with no blueprint behind it.

Facundo Rivadeneira-Holm, the commercial director, did not want a system holding an order from Mercado Sur, a long-standing modern-trade customer. He would rather promise delivery now and let the plant catch up, saw the routine freight deduction as not worth a dispute, and wanted the single cutover so the system went live sooner. Ximena Lauría-Breto, the controller, trusted checks her team could see: a morning journal reviewed by a person, value-added tax (VAT) keyed by a clerk who knew the customer, a profitability workbook built over years, and a nightly credit run. The two plant managers wanted a company code each. The procurement lead asked to keep the four purchasing rights he held in the old system and for small requisitions to clear without waiting. Lindqvist-Paredes argued for staying close to the standard; his firm would bill ten more months under the phased plan.

## What Achával-Rendón Controlled

For the foundations she set the **master-data budget**, starting three months before the programme: 2% of the CO$ 8 million (CO$ 160,000), 8% (CO$ 640,000) or 20% (CO$ 1.6 million). The **deployment doctrine**: an on-premise installation adapted to Helio Verde's own processes; specialised applications for each function joined through interfaces; or a cloud tenant kept close to the standard, with extensions built alongside it. The **organisational structure**: a company code for each plant; one company code with two plants and two distribution channels; or one flat structure with no plant or sales split.

For the sale, she decided what happened when a salesperson saved the Mercado Sur order: run the **credit check** overnight as before, let the order through, or check it live against the open balance. Which **delivery date** to confirm: immediate delivery, a refusal because stock was short, or a date that netted incoming production. What the **goods issue** posted: the inventory, cost-of-sales and controlling entries at once; inventory only, with cost of sales at month-end; or nothing until a clerk keyed the journal next morning. How the invoice **VAT** was determined: keyed by a clerk, set to zero as if the sale were an export, or derived by the system from the customer and material records. What happened to the **CO$ 50,000 short payment**: rejected; written off automatically as a sales allowance; or matched, with the CO$ 50,000 parked for review. And how the **month-end profitability** question would be answered: by finance in a spreadsheet, by closing the books and deferring analytics to a later phase, or by an analysis running over the postings.

For the purchase, she decided what the nightly planning run did about the **sugar shortfall**: e-mail the buyer to investigate, wait for a planner to notice the gap, or raise a 7,500 kg requisition automatically. Who **approved** the CO$ 35,000 requisition: the plant manager, an automatic release, or the board. What the **purchase order** did in finance: reserve the budget against the cost centre, record a payable at once, or nothing until the goods arrived. Which account the **goods receipt** credited: accounts payable, cash on delivery, or a clearing account until the invoice arrived. And what happened to an **invoice for 7,800 kg** against 7,500 kg ordered and received: pay it as billed, correct it to the received quantity automatically, or block it for payment.

The last decision was a **package**: keep the procurement lead's four rights with a 14-month single cutover; split the four duties across four roles, with a 24-month phased roll-out and 15% of the programme for change management; or a manager override on the combined role, with specialised applications joined through interfaces in place of the suite.

## Closing

At 07:55 Achával-Rendón typed the first line of the design log: Step 1, master data. Her phone lit up. Rivadeneira-Holm: Mercado Sur had confirmed the 5,000 cases. On the wall screen, Iturbe-Saldías's first question was waiting: how much of the CO$ 8 million would go to the data before a single module went live.
