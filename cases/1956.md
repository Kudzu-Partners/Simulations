# Calderwood Building Society: Announcing Project Horizon

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1956 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1956 — *Framing the Change* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the evening of Monday, 9 March 2026, Ruth Naylor read the board minute a second time and wrote one word on the pad in front of her: *Horizon*. The minute approved the consolidation of Calderwood Building Society's 92 branches into 64 hub-and-spoke sites, the transfer of 480 of its 1,850 front-line roles into a centralised digital and telephone service centre, and the retraining or exit of the affected staff over six months. It did not say what any of it should be called.

Naylor had until 31 March to announce Project Horizon and launch the first wave. The date was fixed to the financial-year cost programme; missing it would push GBP 6 million of planned savings into the following year. A pulse survey two weeks earlier had found that 34% of Calderwood's staff believed leadership was honest about why change happened. The announcement would reach 1,850 front-line people through whatever account of it had got to them first.

## The Protagonist

Naylor, 46, had been Calderwood's Director of Transformation since 2023. She had joined at 22 as a cashier in Oldham, run the branches in Bolton and Stockport, and moved into change delivery in 2019. She was one of three executives who had worked behind a counter — the fact colleagues cited to trust her, and the one union officials cited to say she should know better. Neither of her two earlier programmes had been contested in public. Her 2026 objectives carried the cost-to-income target and the retention of the staff the society meant to retrain.

## The Decision

Naylor had to decide what Project Horizon would be called, in the widest sense: which single governing image would explain the restructuring, how much of the loss that image would carry, what each of three audiences would be told, and whether the people most likely to object would hear it from her before they read it elsewhere. Three weeks, one announcement, no second first day.

## The Company and its Setting

Calderwood Building Society was a mutual, owned by its 740,000 members and headquartered in Manchester. It held a mortgage-and-savings book of GBP 14.2 billion and employed 3,100 people, 1,850 of them facing members directly. Its cost-to-income ratio stood at 62%; the board's plan required 54%. Digital-only lenders had spent years undercutting its margins while carrying no branches at all; the society's answer had been to hold the network and absorb the cost.

What Calderwood had instead of scale was a century-old promise that its service was local, human and delivered by someone a member could stand in front of. It was why every one of the 28 branches on the closure list mattered more than its balances suggested: in each of those towns, Calderwood's was the last branch of any bank or society left. The regional press had begun asking which towns were on the list.

## The Situation

The plan was settled; the three weeks would decide how it arrived. Naylor's team had agreed four measures with the board's people committee, tracked from announcement day (see **Exhibit 1**). Staff buy-in ran on a 0–100 scale of front-line acceptance, set at 34 to match the pulse survey, against a committee target of 60. Message clarity, also 0–100, measured how consistently staff, members and the union could state unprompted what Horizon was and why; it stood at 25. A resistance index counted grievances, press flare-ups and competing versions of the plan circulating internally, at 45, and better the lower it went. Rollout speed was the share of the 31 March schedule still protected, at 100%.

The costs were known. Retraining ran at roughly GBP 9,000 per affected employee. The union had requested consultation. The 480 affected roles were 26% of the front line, and no individual yet knew which of them was theirs.

If Horizon did not launch on 31 March, GBP 6 million moved into the next financial year and the ratio stayed at 62%. If it launched into 34% trust with no account of itself, Naylor's own view, which she had put to the board, was that an account would be supplied by someone else.

## What Naylor Controlled

Before choosing any words she had to settle what Calderwood's people heard when the society talked about change. Her communications lead had set out four positions: the century-old people-first promise, against which Horizon would be measured; defence of the branch network against the digital entrants; cost and process, the ratio visibly running the society; or nothing at all, no story doing any work and the plan read as stated. She had to fix in parallel what the 34% meant. A credibility problem, in which no account without named losses would be believed. A communications problem, the cost case never properly explained. A morale dip that would recover once the change bedded in. Or a low number that would not shape how Horizon was received. She also had to set what share of the three weeks went into that work before anything was written.

Then the governing image, one only. A journey: Horizon as the next chapter of the mutual, with a destination held in common. Renewal: Horizon as pruning, cutting back so the society could grow again. An engine: the streamlining of a machine grown too expensive to run, in the vocabulary the board already used for the 62%. Adaptation: a society evolving to survive a market that had changed around it. Within whichever she chose, she had to fix how squarely the 480 roles were named — from an account that led with them to one that left them to the individual consultations. She had then to decide whether to rule an image out in public: the engine, as addressing 1,850 people whose work was human service as parts of a mechanism; the family language, as unable to carry 480 lost roles without reading as evasion; or neither, keeping the vocabulary open.

Three audiences would receive the announcement, and each had asked for something different (see **Exhibit 2**). Naylor could write three messages inside one governing image, the wording built for each audience; write two and reuse one, sending the staff message to the union as well; or issue a single announcement to all three. For members in the 28 towns she could lead with the closures named, the support specified and the dates given; with the case for the renewed society, what it could then do and what it would cost to get there; or with continuity — that what Calderwood was for its members was not changing, with the branch detail to follow. For the union she could bring its officials into the design of the rollout, so that they signed off the sequence before it was announced, at a cost in days she did not have; meet the consultation duty and keep authorship of the message; or announce first and consult after, on the argument that negotiating beforehand would leak and consume the window. She also had to set how much of the window went to consultation before the announcement.

Her team wanted a response protocol fixed in advance, for the case where a competing version of the plan took hold; there would be no time to agree one afterwards. She could absorb it, folding the concern inside it into the society's own account; hold, repeating the original account unchanged; or correct it, treating a distorted version as a factual error, with a notice to its author and a request to the platform carrying it. She had to name who spoke in the first 48 hours, before the pulse survey re-ran: herself, in person and in the channels staff and members used; a single approved statement through official channels; or nobody, leaving the launch materials and branch managers to carry it. And she had to fix the date. Hold 31 March. Hold it, but stagger the hardest closures across a longer first wave. Or take two more weeks and accept the GBP 6 million.

## Closing

At 9:15 p.m. Naylor wrote four words down the left-hand margin of the pad — *journey, renewal, engine, adaptation* — and left the right-hand side empty. The announcement was set for 31 March. She had three weeks, 1,850 people who did not believe leadership told them the truth about change, and one word to choose.
