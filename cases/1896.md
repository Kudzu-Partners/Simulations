# Hub Tecnológico Paulista: Ten Seats, Two Numbers

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1896 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1896 — *Inside the Incubator* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 10 February 2026, Beatriz Furtado had two documents open on the same desk. The first was the applicant list for Hub Tecnológico Paulista's next cohort: 38 qualified ventures for 10 seats. The second was the countersigned renewal letter from the São Paulo state innovation agency, which extended HTP's funding for three years and reduced the condition of that funding to two numbers.

The cohort's two-year survival rate had to reach at least 60%, and the same cohort had to raise at least R$25 million in follow-on funding. If either number came in short, the agency would withdraw R$3.5 million — 55% of HTP's income — and HTP would halve its 14 staff and close one of its two cohort tracks within twelve months. Furtado had to fill the ten seats, decide how the incubator's mentor-hours and grant money would be split among the ventures that took them, and set the criteria those ventures would have to meet to graduate. The cohort closed on intake; the two numbers would be audited two years later.

## The Protagonist

Beatriz Furtado, 44, had been HTP's Managing Director since 2019. She had come to the job from the state agency's own innovation directorate, where she had spent six years writing the grant instruments she now applied for, and before that from a decade in project finance at a Paulista bank. She had run six cohorts. Her habit, formed on the funding side of the table, was to ask what a programme could actually be held to in writing, and she had told her board more than once that HTP had never been held to anything.

## The Decision

Furtado had to state, in advance and in writing, what kind of cohort HTP was assembling and why; then choose the ten ventures; then decide how the incubator's scarce support would reach them; then set the bar they had to clear to be counted as graduates — and defend all four decisions to the agency board.

## The Company and its Setting

Hub Tecnológico Paulista, founded in 2014, was a university-linked technology incubator in São Paulo, run as a not-for-profit by a partnership between a business school and the state innovation agency. It occupied 1,800 m² of co-working and laboratory space, ran two 18-month cohorts a year, and drew on a network of 120 volunteer mentors, three resident venture funds and a group of corporate sponsors.

In ten years HTP had incubated 96 ventures. Forty-one per cent were still operating, 12 had raised institutional rounds, and two had been acquired. Its annual operating budget was R$6.4 million: 55% from the state agency, 30% from corporate sponsorship, 15% earned through equity returns and rent. Of that budget, R$2.2 million was fixed overhead — staff and the facility — leaving up to R$4.2 million that could be spent directly on a cohort.

Historically HTP had selected on judgement and supported on request. The agency had never asked for more, and its programme officers had spoken about pipeline and ecosystem rather than about rates.

## The Situation

Against a required 60%, the survival rate HTP could show was 41% — the ten-year figure across all 96 ventures (see **Exhibit 1**). Against a required R$25 million, a cohort not yet chosen had raised nothing. The agency had also asked HTP to report, alongside the two headline numbers, an index from 0 to 100 of how much of its support had reached the ventures that moved them; on the agency's first assessment, using the last cohort's records, HTP scored 20.

The support available to the 2026 cohort was fixed and smaller than the cohort. HTP could fund roughly 2,400 mentor-hours, a micro-grant pool of R$1.8 million, and ten desk and laboratory allocations. Ten ventures could not each be fully served. Separately, HTP could run a fundraising-readiness programme — demo days, investor matching and legal clinics — at a cost of R$700,000, money that would have to come out of the micro-grant pool or be raised from sponsors.

The applicant pool divided into four families, summarised in **Exhibit 2**. Deep-tech ventures took longest to reach revenue and needed the most capital; HTP's own record put their two-year survival near 30%, and its two acquisitions had both come from that family. Business-to-business software ventures reached revenue fastest in a crowded market; their two-year survival ran near 58% and the rounds they raised were the smallest HTP saw. Marketplaces depended on assembling both sides of a market before either would pay, and raised middling rounds. Social ventures were closest to the university's mission and the hardest to place with investors. HTP had never incubated enough marketplaces or social ventures to publish a survival rate for either.

If HTP repeated what it had done for a decade — select on judgement, support on request, graduate whoever was still trading — the arithmetic was not in dispute. A cohort resembling HTP's ten-year history would come in near 41% survival, well under the mandate, and the agency would withdraw R$3.5 million.

## What Furtado Controlled

Before opening the applications, Furtado had to commit to a thesis: which venture family, if any, the cohort would lean on to reach both numbers at once; whether the two numbers pulled against each other and where; which observable signal in an application she would weight most heavily — coachability on its own, on the view that the programme could supply the rest; the quality of the pitch and the size of the market it claimed; founder track record together with capital efficiency and traction; or none of them, on the view that nothing in an application predicted much; and how much of her team's February to spend translating "60% and R$25 million" into a target mix before the first file was read.

Then the seats. Ten of them, divided across the four families in whatever proportion she chose, and a decision on how far to spread them: to take the highest-scoring applications regardless of the resulting mix, to load the cohort onto a single family, or to hold the seats deliberately apart so that no one sector's year could take the cohort with it.

Then the support. The 2,400 mentor-hours and the R$1.8 million could go out in equal shares — 240 hours and R$180,000 per venture — or weighted from the start toward the ventures she judged most likely to survive and to raise, or released in tranches as ventures cleared agreed checkpoints. She had to decide how much of the grant pool to concentrate on a few ventures rather than spread across ten, and how many of the 2,400 hours to steer at the two ventures closest to closing a round rather than leave in the common pool. And she had to fund the R$700,000 readiness programme out of the micro-grants, fund it with money raised from sponsors, or not fund it.

Then the gate. Graduation could mean still being incorporated and trading at the end of the programme; or revenue traction, validated unit economics, a committed lead investor and mentor sign-off; or a closed Series A round and nothing less, whatever else a venture had achieved. Midway through the cohort she would review two ventures that had missed every milestone and decide whether to keep supporting both, halve their support and keep them under review, or end both and move what they were consuming to a venture close to a round. And when the agency board asked whether her survival figure was real or padded with companies that existed only on paper, she would answer with the graduate count, with a request to wait for the audit, or with per-graduate evidence — and would decide how hard to hold that answer against a board that wanted one clean headline number.

## Closing

Furtado turned the renewal letter face down. The applicant list stayed where it was: thirty-eight names for ten seats, and two numbers she could not negotiate — 60 and 25. Everything she settled from here — who came in, what they were given, what counted as finishing — would be read back to her at the audit.
