# Verveine & Co.: Decode the Brand

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1781 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1781 — *Decode the Brand* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Thursday, 12 November 2026, Camille Dubreuil read an outside study of what her company's best-known product actually told a shopper, commissioned after a run of rejected grocery listings, and understood within two pages why. Verveine, the herbal-infusion brand she managed, still led every pharmacy in France; in grocery stores and online it was close to invisible. The study's conclusion was direct: the tea's colour, lettering, ingredient copy and sachet told a shopper who wanted a ritual to choose that she was instead holding a remedy to be prescribed.

Dubreuil had sixteen weeks before the spring wellness season opened the next window to get Verveine onto grocery shelves, and a launch budget of EUR 2.4 million that her parent group had already fixed and would not extend. Before she could decide what the brand should look like, she had to decide, with her team, what it currently said — and what, instead, it should say.

## The Protagonist

Camille Dubreuil, 38, had managed the Verveine brand for four years inside the mid-sized French food group that had owned it since its 1998 launch. She had come up through category management in grocery retail before moving client-side, and her standing in the business rested on a run of small, disciplined pharmacy-channel launches that had protected Verveine's leadership there without a single price war. This was the first time she had been asked to move the brand into a channel it had never held, against a deadline the group would not move and a budget it would not extend.

## The Decision

Dubreuil had to decide, across four sessions with her brand team, which single element of Verveine's current design was doing the most work to make it look like medicine rather than a pleasure; how much of the team's limited time to spend mapping the brand's existing colours, words and materials against what they actually communicated before changing anything; which new story the brand would tell about itself; how far to carry that story toward a lifestyle register without losing the pharmacists who had built the brand's trust; how to split a fixed sum of money across changing the product's look, paying for retail shelf space, and telling shoppers the story had changed; and how to pitch the result to two audiences wanting opposite reassurances.

## The Company and its Setting

Verveine & Co. had launched in 1998 as a pharmacy-channel infusion sold on digestive and calming claims, built around a muted green-and-white logo, a serif wordmark, dense ingredient copy on the box, and individually wrapped sachets styled like a blister pack of tablets. Twenty-eight years later, the identity that had built the brand was also what was now holding it back. Verveine held 38% of the French pharmacy category, and pharmacies still supplied most of its EUR 96 million in annual revenue, at an 11% operating margin, but that channel was flat. Meanwhile the broader "wellness ritual" segment — premium, sensorial infusions bought as a daily pleasure rather than a treatment — had tripled in three years to reach EUR 340 million in grocery and e-commerce, a segment where Verveine barely registered. Three attempts to place Verveine on supermarket shelves that year, including with each of the two major grocery chains now back at the table, had already failed (see **Exhibit 1**).

## The Situation

The study's central finding was a gap between what Verveine's own team believed the brand meant and what a shopper under thirty-five actually read from the shelf: not "a calm I choose" but "medicine for someone older than me." The stakes attached to closing that gap were specific. The two grocery chains had told Verveine's trade team they would grant shelf space only if the repositioning looked credible to their category buyers; failing to secure that space would forfeit an estimated EUR 6 million of addressable sales in the first year alone. Separately, an association representing pharmacists had warned that a relaunch read as abandoning clinical credibility could cost meaningful ground within the EUR 26 million tied to that 38% pharmacy share — still the channel funding most of the business. And a venture-backed challenger, marketed under the name Aube, was rumoured to be preparing its own entry into pharmacies within nine months, arriving with exactly the sensorial, ritual-coded design Verveine had never had. If Dubreuil changed nothing — kept the sachet, the wordmark, the colour and the copy exactly as they were — the pattern of the last three years would simply continue: pharmacy leadership intact, most of its revenue flat with no growth left in it, and almost no share of the EUR 340 million segment the relaunch existed to win.

## What Dubreuil Controlled

Dubreuil's first decision was diagnostic: which single element of the current design — the sachet's blister-pack material, shape, and individually wrapped format; the apothecary-style wordmark combined with its dense ingredient-copy text block; the muted green colour palette in isolation from every other cue; or no single element alone, but the overall look reading as dated — was doing the most work to signal medicine rather than ritual. Tied to it was how much of the team's scarce time to spend, before touching the design, building a full inventory of colour, word and material against what each actually told a shopper — from a fast, confident call to a complete audit first.

Her second decision was which new story Verveine would tell about itself. The candidates ran from a narrative built on clinical calm and evidence, with a layer of everyday ritual, to one built on the brand's own French herbal heritage, reframed as an asset, to one centred on the daily ritual of slowing down, echoing established grocery wellness brands, to one built on broad wellness themes already proven to work with grocery buyers and shoppers. Alongside that choice sat a single dial running between two ends of one spectrum: how far to keep the brand's tone anchored in clinical, pharmacist-facing credibility versus how far to carry it toward a fully lifestyle-facing register — pharmacists had already warned against drifting too far one way, and the grocery buyers she needed were unmoved by anything that still read as clinical.

Her third decision was arithmetic. She had to divide the fixed EUR 2.4 million among changing the physical product and its packaging, paying the two grocery chains' trade listing fees — estimated at roughly EUR 700,000 to secure both — and funding the marketing that would tell shoppers the story had changed (see **Exhibit 2**). Money committed to one line was not available to the others, and the three had to add up to no more than the fixed total.

Her final decision came at the pitch itself, presented separately to a grocery category buyer and to a senior pharmacist. She could divide her emphasis evenly between the two audiences, lead with the lifestyle story aimed at the buyer, lead with clinical reassurance aimed at the pharmacist, or lead with a direct claim of higher value for the relaunched product.

Four measures tracked progress along the way: how strongly the brand's codes read as a chosen ritual rather than a medicine (24 out of 100 the morning the study arrived); how much clinical trust the brand still held with pharmacists (88 out of 100); how distinctly ownable its new meaning was against a fast-following competitor (35 out of 100); and how much value shoppers perceived from the physical cues themselves, apart from anything claimed in a pitch (42 out of 100). Alongside those sat the EUR 2.4 million budget, at its full balance; the grocery listing itself, pending with EUR 6 million riding on it; and a year-one purchase-intent estimate, not yet calculated.

## Closing

By the end of that Thursday, Dubreuil had called her semiotics lead, her design lead, and her trade lead into a room with the study, a whiteboard, and a calendar showing sixteen weeks to the spring window. Across the top of the board she wrote the four sessions ahead of them — decode, reposition, cost, defend — and under the first one, a single open question: which part of Verveine was actually saying "medicine," and was she prepared to change it.
