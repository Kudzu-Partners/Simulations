# Aurelis Mobility Systems: Reshaping the Knowledge Base

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1804 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1804 — *Building the Knowledge Base* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of March 16, 2026, Camille Vasseur, Chief Technology Officer of Aurelis Mobility Systems, read the board's strategy review for the third time that week, marking the same sentence again: "Aurelis's competitive advantage is migrating to technologies we do not own." The board had approved a capability-investment budget of €220 million to be spent over three years and had given Vasseur, alongside her new R&D strategy mandate, one instruction: reshape the firm's technological knowledge base before the market reshaped it for her. Halberg Automotive Group, Aurelis's largest customer, would decide within nine months who would supply the integrated power-electronics-and-software module for its next vehicle platform, and Aurelis could not yet build the module its own engineers would sign off on.

## The Protagonist

Vasseur, 49, had joined Aurelis in 2001 as a motor-design engineer straight out of a Grenoble engineering school, risen to head the electric-machines division in 2014, and been named Chief Technology Officer in 2020 when her predecessor retired. The stronghold the board's review now described as commoditizing — electric machines, alongside thermal management — was the one she had spent two decades building. She had not been in the room when the board decided the firm needed a reset; she had been asked to run it. Her standing with the board, and with the engineers she had promoted out of the division she used to lead, rode on what she did with the next three years, and with the nine months in particular.

## The Decision

Vasseur had to decide, for each technological competence Aurelis lacked, whether to build it inside the company's own R&D organization, acquire it by buying a company that already had it, or access it through a partner, by licensing or shared research, without ever fully owning it. Whichever mode she chose for a given competence, she also had to decide how much to spend on the people and integration work needed to absorb what Aurelis bought or partnered for; a capability was only real once someone inside the firm could extend it, not just hold the paperwork for it. Separately, she had to decide how Aurelis positioned itself toward the institutes, universities and start-ups around it in Grenoble: closed, owning and controlling every domain itself; open, trading some control for broader access; or a hybrid that drew a line between the two.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurelis Mobility Systems, founded in 1998 as a precision-motors spin-out from a regional engineering group, had grown into a mid-cap, Tier-1 supplier of electric-drivetrain and power-electronics components to European automakers. Headquartered in Grenoble, with research centers in Stuttgart and Eindhoven and a manufacturing plant in Pilsen, the firm employed 6,400 people and had posted €1.9 billion in revenue and a 9% EBIT margin the previous year (see Exhibit 1). Research and development ran at €135 million, 7.1% of revenue, across roughly 900 engineers and 480 active patent families.

Two domains, electric machines and thermal management, accounted for 71% of that patent portfolio, and were also the domains whose price per unit was falling 6% to 8% a year. In power semiconductors and embedded control software, where the industry's margin and differentiation were shifting, Aurelis held under 9% of its patents and licensed most of its core intellectual property (IP) from three outside suppliers. The firm sat inside the Grenoble innovation cluster alongside the Vercors Microelectronics Institute, two universities, and a group of battery and chip start-ups it had mostly left unused.

## The Situation

The board's review, delivered March 12, 2026, had been blunt about what the patent concentration meant for the coming decade. Halberg Automotive Group, 22% of Aurelis's revenue, had told Aurelis its next platform required an integrated power-electronics-and-software module, that the sourcing decision was nine months away, and that Aurelis could not yet deliver it. Building the capability inside Aurelis's own R&D organization was estimated at four to five years — longer than the platform would wait. If the sourcing decision arrived with no credible module, Aurelis would lose a platform worth an estimated €410 million in lifetime revenue, and would have signaled to customers that it was becoming a commodity supplier — a shift its own equity analysts had already flagged.

Three specific moves were competing for the same €220 million (see Exhibit 2). A power-semiconductor start-up in Eindhoven could be acquired outright for €140 million — a deep capability, available quickly, concentrated in a small founding team whose departure would take the knowledge with it. A controls-software scale-up was offering a €35 million licensing-and-co-development deal — faster than building and cheaper than acquiring, with the resulting IP shared, not owned outright. The Vercors Microelectronics Institute was offering a three-year joint-lab arrangement for €18 million — the cheapest of the three, open to more than one domain, slower to produce a proprietary result, and not exclusive to Aurelis. A fourth path existed on paper for the software gap: a second acquisition beyond Eindhoven, for a target not yet identified or costed. None of the three funded options alone closed both gaps, and €220 million would not cover all of them at once.

## What Vasseur Controlled

In an opening round, Vasseur would name which two domains most needed to close a gap — the emerging power-semiconductor and embedded-software domains, the existing electric-machines and thermal-management strongholds, or an adjacent area in battery cells and charging — and would characterize the knowledge base itself as narrow but deep, strong in the two established domains and thin in the ones now gaining value; already broad enough across its 480 patent families to cover what mattered; or deep enough in every domain that no material gap existed. She would also decide how candidly to size the cost of waiting against the nine-month clock.

Next, for power semiconductors and embedded control software separately, she would choose a sourcing mode — build, buy, or partner — funded from the €220 million envelope, alongside how much to invest in absorbing whatever was bought or partnered for, and how much to keep spending to defend the existing strongholds while their prices eroded.

She would then set Aurelis's posture toward the Grenoble ecosystem — closed integrator, open orchestrator, or a hybrid — and decide, domain by domain, whether the thermal-management stronghold stayed closed while the newer growth domains opened to partners, whether it opened to the joint-lab relationship as well, or whether every domain stayed closed, discontinuing its existing partnerships. That choice carried its own spending on governance and integration, and a separate decision on how hard to push internal teams to internalize outside knowledge rather than simply hold title to it.

In the final round, at the sourcing milestone and the three-year review together, Vasseur would decide what led the board's scorecard: the commercial traction the technology had gained, the patent families filed, or the partnerships signed. She would decide how to position the firm for whatever shifted next — staged bets that kept options open, full commitment to the one domain just built up, or a wait to see where value moved before acting. And when the board pressed on whether the €220 million had bought output or merely activity, she would decide whether to answer with the module and its efficiency numbers, with the patent count across the full portfolio, or with the fact that the full budget had been spent — and how forcefully to press the case that the knowledge base's architecture mattered more than its cost.

Across all four rounds, Vasseur was tracked on four scores running 0 to 100: breadth, the number of technology domains Aurelis meaningfully covered, which started at 42; depth, its mastery within the two priority domains, which started at 22; innovation output, how close the integrated module stood to being ready for Halberg, which started at 18; and investment efficiency, how much of that progress the €220 million had bought, not shown until money was spent.

## Closing

Vasseur closed the strategy review and opened a blank slide for the R&D committee. Halberg's sourcing decision was nine months out. The €220 million was hers to commit over three years, in pieces, starting now. She had not yet decided which two domains to name first.
