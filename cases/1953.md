# Venturely Urban: Eight Weeks to a Model

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1953 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1953 — *Model the Future* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 5 May 2026, Marta Bragança laid two documents side by side on the table Venturely Urban used for board meetings. The first was a term sheet: a lead investor would anchor a €6 million Series A. The second was a one-page cash summary her finance lead had left overnight — €220,000 a month of burn against €1.4 million of annualised recurring revenue, and cash into February.

The term sheet carried a condition. Within eight weeks, one fundraising cycle, Bragança had to present a single coherent account of what kind of business Venturely Urban was, of what generative artificial intelligence (AI) would be paid to do inside it, and of where the company would stand in a smart-city market that a global mobility super-app had just entered. If the presentation did not hold together, the term sheet lapsed and the cash summary was the whole story.

## The Protagonist

Marta Bragança, 41, chief executive and co-founder, started Venturely Urban in November 2024. She had spent the previous nine years at a transport-ticketing supplier, the last three of them running its municipal accounts, where she had learned that a city procured a reported outcome rather than a piece of software. She left to build something citizens would use directly. The consumer app was hers: she had written the first specification and had argued down two board members who wanted to sell to city halls from the start. She held the largest founder stake. The €6 million would price both it and that argument.

## The Decision

Bragança had to answer four questions in one document by the end of June: why the company's present model consumed cash, what kind of business Venturely Urban would become with the €6 million, what generative AI would be paid to do, and what position the company would hold as the super-app arrived. The investors would read the four answers together, in the order she put them.

## The Company and its Setting

Venturely Urban, Lda., of Lisbon, was 18 months old and employed 28 people. Its app aggregated public-transport timetables, shared bicycles and scooters, and electric-vehicle charging points into one journey planner. Alongside it, the company measured the transport emissions its routing avoided and sold anonymised mobility insights to municipalities. One mid-sized Portuguese city ran a live pilot.

Venturely Urban's environmental figures were externally verified, and the pilot city quoted them in its own reporting. What the company had not settled was what it sold. Three descriptions of the business existed inside the same building, and the board had split along them (see **Exhibit 2**).

A global mobility super-app had begun entering Iberia and was courting the same municipalities and the same charging networks. From its expansion pattern in two other markets, Bragança estimated roughly two quarters before it signed a flagship Portuguese city.

## The Situation

The consumer numbers were the ones the investors had asked about first. Blended across all channels, acquiring a customer cost €34; the revenue that customer produced over the whole relationship came to €41. The ratio of the two — what the customer was worth divided by what it cost to obtain — stood at 1.2 times. The benchmark the lead investor had quoted was at least 3.0 times (see **Exhibit 1**).

Against €220,000 of monthly burn, the company held about nine months of cash. The Series A had to close by month six; after that, Venturely Urban would be cutting staff during a fundraising round. Bragança's board chair maintained two further readings on the same sheet, each on a hundred-point scale: how far the syndicate believed the company had a fundable story, which stood at 25 after the last round of meetings, and how defensible the company's position was against a larger entrant, which stood at 20. The chair also tracked how much of the €6 million had been committed to a named use: none of it.

Generative AI sat unused. A full deployment across the app, the municipal dashboard and the support desk would cost about €45,000 a month in inference and engineering — roughly a fifth of the monthly burn.

If Bragança changed nothing, the arithmetic was plain: the company would keep spending €34 to acquire customers worth €41, the ratio would stay at 1.2 times, and the cash would run to February while the super-app worked through the municipalities.

## What Bragança Controlled

Four explanations for the burn circulated inside the company, and one of them would open the presentation. The first was that the economics were broken and the position undefended, every additional user consuming cash and faster growth consuming it faster. The second was that the company under-marketed, and that more paid acquisition and a stronger brand would move the averages. The third was that the product was too thin, and that more features would raise retention until the economics followed. The fourth was that nothing structural was wrong, and that the company was simply early and needed the €6 million.

She also had to say where the environmental value the company produced went unpaid. One reading was that verified emissions reductions and mobility outcomes passed to municipalities free while the company paid to acquire consumers. A second was that they went nowhere, environmental performance being a reputational asset no buyer would pay a line item for. A third was that they went unpaid by consumers, who could be charged a premium for low-emission routing. A fourth was that nothing was unpaid, the company already collecting on everything it produced. And she had to commit publicly to a target for the value-to-acquisition-cost ratio, a figure between 1.0 and 5.0 that the plan would then have to deliver.

The second question was what the €6 million built (see **Exhibit 2**). She could build the consumer app, taking the largest addressable market and scaling users first. She could build a subscription and data business selling verified emissions reductions and mobility insights to municipalities, with higher margins, longer retention and a slower sales cycle. She could open the interfaces and let transit and charging operators build on Venturely Urban, taking network effects that required scale on both sides. Or she could carry all three forward and decide later. Alongside it she had to reset how revenue was earned: charge municipalities for measured outcomes, reporting and insight; keep the app free and charge consumers for premium environmental features; keep everything free and sell attention; or leave pricing where it was and revisit it after the round. Then she had to say what share of the €6 million went to the chosen route to market, from none of it to all of it.

The third question was generative AI. It could go into operations, automating the municipal emissions reporting and analytics that cities procured. It could go into the consumer product, as journey planning and personalised prompts. It could go across the app, the municipal dashboard and the support desk at once. Or it could wait. Whichever, the capability could be rented from a foundation-model provider, adapted on the company's own municipal data, or trained in-house for control of the whole stack — and she had to name a monthly budget between nothing and €45,000.

The fourth was position. Venturely Urban could stand as the preferred data and insight partner to cities, holding the municipal relationship and the outcome reporting; as the integration layer that transit and charging operators connected through; or as the aggregator on the citizen's home screen, in the same place as the super-app. To reach scale first she could turn the pilot city into a reference and sell its evidence to neighbouring municipalities, meet the super-app on price and outspend it on consumer acquisition, or hold the company's present breadth until the market showed which position paid. Last, she had to state how tightly the three answers fitted as one argument.

She would be judged on four numbers: the months of cash remaining, the value-to-acquisition-cost ratio, the syndicate's confidence reading, and the defensibility reading.

## Closing

At 9:15 a.m. Bragança wrote four headings on the whiteboard — the burn, the model, the AI, the position — and left them blank. The pitch was in eight weeks. The cash ran to February, and the super-app was already in Iberia.
