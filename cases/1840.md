# Bosphorus Executive Academy: Scoping the Marmara Brief

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1840 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1840 — *Building the Exec Program* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 9 February 2026, Elif Sarıoğlu, Executive Development Director at Bosphorus Executive Academy in Istanbul, opened an email from Marmara Holding's people office and read a brief that would decide the shape of her next four months. Marmara Holding, a nine-billion-euro Turkish-Gulf conglomerate and BEA's largest single client, wanted a leadership-transformation program for 120 senior managers, launched in fourteen weeks, worth 1.6 million euros. The proposal was due in ten working days.

The brief read like a wish list rather than a specification. It asked for world-class faculty at a price the procurement team had all but capped, a program that was digital-first and high-touch at once, and "measurable behaviour change" that the client would judge for itself six months after the program ended. Two rival providers had already been invited to bid the same brief. Sarıoğlu had ten days to turn the wish list into something BEA could design, staff and deliver — and four months after that, to show it had worked.

## The Protagonist

Sarıoğlu, 44, had led BEA's custom-program practice since 2022, after nine years building the same kind of bespoke corporate programs at a rival Istanbul institution. She had joined BEA specifically to grow its custom book beyond its open-enrolment core, and the Marmara relationship — cultivated before her arrival and expanded since — was the proof that the strategy had worked. Her own review, like the unit's annual numbers, moved with two things: the operating margin her programs delivered, and the strength of BEA's relationship with the clients who kept coming back. Marmara Holding was not just BEA's largest custom account; it was the account every partner meeting pointed to when explaining why custom education mattered more than open enrolment.

## The Decision

Her work would come in four stages, each locking in choices the next stage would have to live with.

In the first stage she had to decide how to turn the brief into a program at all: starting from the behaviour Marmara wanted to see at month six and designing modules and faculty backward from it; writing outcomes but still leading with a syllabus of expert topics; building the program around a dozen highly credentialed lectures organised by subject rather than by outcome; or adapting one of BEA's existing open-enrolment leadership courses. Alongside that, she had to choose which of the brief's built-in tensions to raise with the client before designing anything — the gap between "world-class faculty" and an unstated price ceiling, the gap between "digital-first" and "high-touch," or neither — and how rigorously to write the learning outcomes the client would actually be able to evaluate.

In the second stage she had to architect the program inside the contract: how many of BEA's seven marquee professors, only three of whom were free in the fourteen-week window, to put into the proposal; which format to build for a client that wanted both digital reach and high-touch delivery, from a residential-online hybrid design, to a fully residential design, to a fully online design; and how much of the design to spend on applied work — projects, line-manager involvement — rather than classroom time.

In the third stage she had to scope, price and defend the proposal against the two rivals: setting a price, then responding when procurement asked mid-process for a twelve-percent cut and a third cohort at the same rate, by trading the cut for an equivalent reduction in scope, holding the price and scope as quoted, or granting the cut and the free cohort while keeping every faculty day and module in the plan — and choosing how tightly to commit the fourteen-week timeline: staged, with cohort two built in parallel behind a real critical path; a lean commitment to the full scope with no slack; or a timeline pulled in ahead of the rivals.

In the fourth stage, with cohort one already running, she had to answer three shocks at once: a marquee professor's cancellation two weeks before a module carrying two of the program's learning outcomes, to be met by remapping those outcomes onto other faculty, by filling the slot with whoever was free, or by dropping the module from the program; a scheduling clash between cohort two and Marmara's fiscal close, to be resequenced or pushed through on the original dates; and a request from the client's sponsor for evidence that behaviour was changing, to be answered with a plan built on application projects, manager check-ins and business metrics, with the program's already-strong satisfaction scores, or with no formal plan at all.

## The Company and its Setting

Bosphorus Executive Academy was the open-and-custom executive-development unit of a leading Istanbul business school, generating fourteen million euros a year at a twenty-two-percent target operating margin, run by a core team of eighteen program staff drawing on a pool of seventy affiliated faculty, academic and practitioner. The unit's standing rested on a single claim: that its programs were built to a client's own strategy, not assembled from a catalogue. Marmara Holding was the proof of that claim at scale — a relationship worth nineteen percent of BEA's revenue, roughly 2.66 million euros a year, and the account every partner meeting pointed to when explaining why custom work mattered. Two rival providers, both hungry for exactly this kind of flagship account, had been invited to bid alongside BEA.

## The Situation

By the morning the brief landed, BEA's starting position was already on the scoreboard Sarıoğlu would carry through the months ahead (Exhibit 2). Operating margin stood at the twenty-two-percent target, with anything below roughly ten percent treated internally as value-destroying. Outcome fit — a zero-to-hundred measure of how far the design sat from producing behaviour the client would recognise at month six — stood at a bare 20. Client trust, tracking the health of the Marmara relationship on the same zero-to-hundred scale, stood at 45. The faculty plan showed three marquee professors proposed against three actually free in the window, and not a euro of the 1.6-million-euro contract had yet been committed to cost (Exhibit 1).

None of those numbers would move on their own. If Sarıoğlu answered the wish list exactly as written, without resolving either of its built-in tensions, matched whatever price the rivals set without regard to her own cost base, and treated the marquee roster and the delivery format as defaults rather than decisions, the proposal would carry an outcome-fit score barely above the 20 it opened at, a client relationship no more secure than the 45 it started from, and a margin exposed to whatever concession procurement asked for next — while two rival providers, holding the same brief, waited for exactly that outcome.

## What Sarıoğlu Controlled

Across the four stages, Sarıoğlu's decisions fed a single scoreboard: the operating margin the program delivered against its 1.6-million-euro contract and BEA's 22% target, the outcome-fit score the client would judge at month six, the trust score tracking the Marmara relationship, and whether the faculty plan she proposed matched the roster BEA could actually field. Every design choice, every response to procurement, and every response to a delivery shock moved some combination of the four — and once cohort one was mid-delivery, there would be no stage left in which to fix a number she had let slide earlier.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 9 February, Sarıoğlu printed the brief and drew four columns across the top of a blank page, one for each stage still ahead of her. The proposal was due in ten working days. Two rival providers already had the same brief in hand.
