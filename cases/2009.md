# Aldgate Advisory: Eight Weeks In

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-2009 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2009 — *Crossroads Leader* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

At 07:52 on Monday 9 March 2026, Callum Reid opened a message from Priya Raghunathan that was ten words long. "I've been thinking about whether this is really for me." No greeting, no sign-off. It had been sent at 23:40 the night before, three days after the promotion list went out without her name on it.

Reid was eight weeks into his first people-management job. By Thursday he owed a client the regulatory gap analysis his team had been building since January. Inside the same five working days, according to a colleague who passed it on half as gossip, Priya had a call booked with a recruiter. Whatever Reid decided about the ten words, he had to decide it that week.

## The Protagonist

Callum Reid, 31, had joined Aldgate Advisory as a graduate analyst seven years earlier and spent most of that time on financial-crime remediation for retail banks. In January the firm gave him its nine-person risk and regulatory advisory team on the strength of a client-facing record rather than any management experience. Aldgate had been moving high performers into people-management roles 18 to 24 months earlier in their careers than a decade before, and Reid knew he belonged to that cohort. His skip-level director sat two floors up; the People Partner covering his practice had introduced herself by email and not since. Before January he had never had a direct report, or sat on the manager's side of a career conversation.

## The Decision

Reid had to decide what Priya's message actually meant, how and when to answer it, what he could put on the table in response, and what he would still be doing about it in ninety days — with a client deliverable due Thursday sitting on top of all of it.

## The Company and its Setting

Aldgate Advisory was a London-headquartered firm providing risk and regulatory advisory to financial-services clients across the UK and continental Europe. It employed 1,350 people and generated GBP 198 million of revenue in 2025. It ran a hybrid model: a three-day-a-week in-office anchor, a Manchester hub alongside its London floors, and roughly 30% of staff fully distributed across Europe.

Its exit interviews had settled into a pattern the people team repeated at every leadership session: the people Aldgate most wanted to keep did not leave over pay, but over a quiet sense that they were on the wrong path. Few of the firm's new managers had been taught what to do with it.

## The Situation

Priya Raghunathan was 28, and by Reid's assessment and his predecessor's one of the two strongest analysts on the team. She was staffed on a client account worth GBP 1.2 million a year and had been the continuity on it through two renewals. Aldgate's people team costed a senior analyst's replacement at roughly GBP 95,000 once recruitment and lost continuity were counted. She was also one of two women in the nine-person team, at a firm that had spent three years telling clients and graduates it was building a leadership pipeline that would not look like its current partnership.

Four things sat behind the message. Priya had gone quiet in the mostly-virtual standups, contributing when asked and not otherwise. She had declined the last two in-office days. Eighteen months earlier she had moved sideways into the team rather than follow her cohort onto the promotion track. And on Thursday the promotion list had gone out without her (see **Exhibit 2**).

Reid had read that evidence four ways over the weekend. She had been passed over for the promotion, and wanted more money or a faster title. She had gone quiet eight weeks into working for a manager who was new, and the new manager was him. She had taken a missed cycle hard, and would be over it once the deadline cleared. She had moved sideways, gone quiet and sent ten words, and those three were one question about whether the work still fitted her.

His instrumentation was thin. Aldgate's people team kept an index called regret-signal resolution: a 0-to-100 reading of how far a career-regret signal had been surfaced and acted on rather than left to run, higher meaning safer. Priya's stood at 28. It also kept a leader-credibility score out of 10, built from what a manager's whole team read about whether their own careers were safe with that manager; eight weeks in, Reid's was 5.0, the figure given to anyone who had not yet moved it either way. A third number, the odds that Priya stayed and re-engaged, was blank on Monday; nobody would estimate it until Reid had done something. Against the GBP 95,000 that losing her would cost, he had committed nothing: GBP 0 of spend, GBP 0 of promises (see **Exhibit 1**).

The arithmetic of waiting was simple. If Reid held off until Thursday's deliverable cleared, the recruiter call happened on Friday with the ten words unanswered — read, as Priya could see, and not replied to. Replacing her cost about GBP 95,000 and put the GBP 1.2 million renewal into play. And the other eight, who had watched their new manager receive a resignation signal from one of his two best people, would have their answer about how careers were handled here.

## What Reid Controlled

First, what he decided the message meant, and which single unknown was worth testing before he acted: how far along the recruiter conversation actually was, or what "this" referred to and whether the work still fitted who Priya wanted to become, or nothing at all — the message spoke for itself and the week was short. He also had to place his own standing: established by the promotion, irrelevant to a conversation about Priya rather than him, or thin and still forming, with the team reading this episode as evidence. And he had to say how sure he was that he had the cause rather than the symptom.

Second, how he showed up. Fifteen minutes on video between client tasks respected her flexibility; a written message was the lightest touch available; a day at the Manchester hub put him across a table from her. He could hold it this week inside the five days, hold it after Thursday cleared, or say they would find time soon without fixing a slot. He could build it around persuading her that Aldgate was worth staying at, around direct questions about the recruiter and her plans, or around open questions about fit and meaning with nothing promised. And he could decide what the other eight saw: nothing, a visible reallocation of his week to one person, or a quieter signal that careers got real attention here.

Third, the response. He could refer Priya to the firm's career coach to work it through outside the team; accelerate her pay and promotion timeline, moving money and title forward now; re-craft the role around her strengths, changing its tasks and its meaning; or commit to talking again in a few weeks and decide then. Whatever he chose, he had to split it between what an eight-week team leader could authorise himself and what went up to the skip-level director and the People Partner. He could promise Priya a named sponsor and a seat at the client steering committee, broker something smaller through his director, or leave visibility out of it. And he could commit money against the GBP 95,000: nothing, GBP 8,000 for a course, a coaching package or a stretch project, or GBP 40,000 as a retention package and an off-cycle rise.

Fourth, what happened after the week. In ninety days he would face his skip-level director with a plan, with a habit of watching Priya's standup participation, or with the memory of one good conversation. He would have said nothing broadly to the team, announced what he had done for Priya, or made career conversations a normal rhythm. And he would have connected the episode to Aldgate's pipeline goal, led with it, or left it alone.

## Closing

At 08:20 the reply box was open and empty. Thursday's deliverable had three days of work left in it and three days to do it. Priya's calendar showed her at home for the rest of the week. Reid counted five working days to Friday, and started typing.
