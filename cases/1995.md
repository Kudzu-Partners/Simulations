# Atlantique Business School: The Boundary in Singapore

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1995 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1995 — *Cross-Border Program* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 14 September 2026, Élodie Marchand left the board room of Atlantique Business School with two numbers written on the back of the agenda: €1.2 million and 45. The board had approved Programme Atlantique Global — a fourteen-month Executive Master in International Management, taught across more than one country, opening September 2027 — and had fixed the design-and-launch budget at €1.2 million, a hard cap. The programme was to cover its costs with 45 paying executives at €32,000 each by its second cohort.

Marchand had until December to lodge the accreditation documentation for every partner school she intended to use, nine months before the first class. Between September and December she had to name the foreign schools that would co-deliver the programme, settle the terms, and decide what ABS would share with Lumière École de Management — the school in Lyon that competed with ABS for the same French candidates and co-owned its recruitment office in Singapore.

## The Protagonist

Élodie Marchand, 44, had been Director of Executive Programmes at ABS since 2021. She had joined the school in 2013 from a Paris consultancy and had built her standing on portfolio work: retiring three open-enrolment programmes that had not filled, and taking the executive line from break-even to a 24% contribution margin in four years. She had never launched a programme outside France. The Dean had given her the mandate and the budget in the same sentence. Her own bonus, like that of every programme director at ABS, moved with the contribution margin of the portfolio she ran.

## The Decision

Marchand had to decide, in four steps between September and December, what Programme Atlantique Global would be, who would deliver it with ABS, where the line with Lumière fell, and whether to tell the Dean in December that the programme should open at all.

Four schools sat on the table (see **Exhibit 2**). Keystone University in the United States was the most widely recognised name in the segment and asked €9,000 per enrolled student as its share; its academic calendar did not line up with the French one. Marina Institute in Singapore held term dates and delivery costs that matched ABS's own and was already a co-tenant of the Singapore office; French and Latin American candidates knew it less well. Cordilheira EduBrasil brought the largest regional applicant pool of the three; its local accreditation had not yet been recognised under the framework ABS's own accreditors applied, and €740,000 of committed spend would ride on the December decision. Lumière had proposed something different: co-owning the programme outright with ABS, pooling the Singapore office and splitting cohorts and costs between Lyon and Nantes.

## The Company and its Setting

Atlantique Business School, founded in Nantes in 1908, enrolled roughly 6,200 students across its bachelor, master and executive lines and held all three of the international accreditations French grandes écoles compete for. Its 2025 operating budget was €92 million. Executive education contributed €18 million of that at a 24% contribution margin, and it was the line the Dean had told the board would grow.

ABS held 41 active exchange agreements and three dual-degree partnerships. The exchanges moved students; the dual degrees were the only arrangements in which another school had ever helped design an ABS award.

The Singapore office was the school's one shared asset. ABS and Lumière had opened it jointly in 2022, split its costs, and used it to reach executives across South-East Asia — the same executives both schools then tried to convert into their own French programmes. Nothing in the arrangement said which school's recruiters saw which enquiry first.

## The Situation

The €1.2 million had to cover partner contracting, faculty buy-out, curriculum build, accreditation mapping and the launch campaign. Nothing had been committed on 14 September (see **Exhibit 1**). Théo Bassin, the school's recruitment analyst, put projected enrolment for the first cohort at 38 paying executives, inside a range he would not narrow further than 30 to 60; the width, he said, depended on which name went on the diploma beside ABS's and on how the programme read to candidates outside France. Bassin could not tell her what the revenue-share guarantees would cost until an architecture was chosen.

Two internal indices sat beside the enrolment number in the Dean's monthly pack. The first, which the academic office called curriculum coherence, scored how far the cases, teaching methods, assessment and cohort experience of a programme held together across the countries it ran in, out of 100; ABS's records tied low scores to low completion in partner cohorts. The draft syllabus scored 52. The second scored how well the school's pricing, its candidate list and its faculty materials were held inside ABS rather than visible to a partner, also out of 100. With nothing signed, it stood at 80.

At Bassin's 38 executives, the first cohort came in seven short of the 45 the board's break-even rested on, and the €1.2 million spread across that cohort ran to roughly €31,600 a head against the €26,667 the break-even assumed. Nothing in the plan closed that gap by itself.

## What Marchand Controlled

In September she had to write down what the programme was: a respected French master delivered abroad with the international element handled as logistics, a programme built for several countries from the outset with calendars, campuses and content designed together, or a programme whose design followed the cheapest partner combination the budget allowed. She had to name what ABS would keep rather than source — pricing, the candidate list and faculty materials; back-office logistics only, with partners owning the academic and market-facing work; or nothing in particular, with the most recognised partner leading. She had to say which line of Bassin's fit sheet would drive the shortlist: the recognition a partner's name carried, the cost of the terms it asked, or an explicit weighting of all six. And she had to set how much of the autumn's analyst hours went into a cohort profit-and-loss before any partner was chosen, from none of it to all of it.

In October she had to choose one of five architectures — Keystone alone, Marina alone, Marina with Keystone, Marina with Cordilheira, or Cordilheira alone — and answer Lumière: co-own the programme outright with them, pooling costs, cohorts, the Singapore office and the candidate flow; pool the office and the logistics but keep pricing, candidates and faculty materials inside ABS; or withdraw from the shared arrangements altogether, Singapore included, and carry the Asian recruitment cost alone. If Cordilheira was in, she had to say whether the accreditation gap was assumed to clear before the December filing or funded with a contingency review and a fallback. Then she had to commit a revenue-share guarantee in euros against the envelope.

In November she had to answer a partner's demand for calendar dates that collided with French faculty availability — hold the French calendar, rebuild the calendar across all three countries at extra coordination cost, or take the partner's dates. She had to fix how far cross-cultural teaching went: no change, translated materials and visiting speakers, or cases, assessment and cohort design rebuilt region by region. She had to state the enrolment number the cohort profit-and-loss would be built to break even on — 58, 45 or 32 — and commit a launch marketing budget.

In December she had to submit the accreditation package: complete and on time, thin and on time, or complete and late. She had to give the Dean a recommendation — open, open only once the outstanding risk was closed, or open regardless — defend the Lumière arrangement in one line, and state a confidence percentage that the cohort broke even at the number she had chosen.

## Closing

On the evening of 14 September, Marchand pinned Bassin's fit sheet above her desk and wrote the two numbers from the board agenda across the top of it. Lumière's director had asked for an answer on the co-ownership proposal by the end of October. The accreditation file was due in December, the first cohort in September 2027, and the projection on the sheet still read 38.
