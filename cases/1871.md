# SaharaFresh Distribution: Optimizing the Chain

On the morning of July 14, 2026, Ifeoma Balogun, Head of Operations at SaharaFresh Distribution Ltd., opened a joint letter from the company's two largest principals. NorthStar Foods and AquaClean, together worth 41% of SaharaFresh's revenue — ₦39.4 billion — wrote that on-time-in-full delivery, the single service measure named in both supply contracts, had fallen to 78% against a contractual target of 95%. Each contract carried a clause letting the principal appoint a second, parallel distributor if on-time-in-full stayed below 90% for two consecutive quarters. One of the two quarters was already gone.

Balogun had thirty days to bring the principals a credible recovery plan, and one quarter from that letter to lift on-time-in-full back above 90%. If she did not, SaharaFresh would lose both accounts and, with them, more than two-fifths of its revenue.

## The Protagonist

Balogun had joined SaharaFresh in 2016 as a regional distribution manager, after eight years in FMCG supply-chain roles in Lagos and Accra, and was promoted to Head of Operations in 2023. She had watched the company absorb three acquisitions from the inside, first inheriting one acquired distributor's warehouse, later becoming the executive responsible for making all four work as one network. Her standing with the board moved with the same two numbers the principals now cared about: service and cost. She had spent six years arguing that SaharaFresh's four centres, four inventory rulebooks and one shared fleet had never merged into one operation. The letter on her desk was the first time anyone above her had agreed it mattered.

## The Decision

Balogun had to run a structured recovery in four stages inside the deadline: establish where the service failure actually lived before spending anything on it; choose how many distribution centres SaharaFresh should run and where; set how much stock to hold and how tightly to control it across 1,400 SKUs; and decide who should physically move the goods — SaharaFresh's own fleet, a contracted logistics partner, or a mix of the two — under whatever service commitment she was prepared to put her name to.

## The Company and its Setting

SaharaFresh Distribution Ltd., founded in Lagos in 2009, was a third-party distributor carrying 1,400 SKUs for eight FMCG principals into 22,000 retail outlets — kiosks, neighbourhood shops and modern-trade supermarkets — across Lagos, Ibadan, Abuja and Port Harcourt. Annual revenue was ₦96 billion, and the EBIT margin was a thin 4.5%, or ₦4.32 billion, typical of thin-margin African distribution economics. SaharaFresh ran four regional distribution centres, a fleet of 140 owned and hired vehicles, and carried ₦11.5 billion in inventory.

The company's growth had come from acquiring three regional distributors between 2018 and 2023. Their four networks had never been integrated: catchment areas around the distribution centres overlapped, each carried its own inherited inventory rules, and the combined transport cost per case ran 35% above the best-in-class Nigerian benchmark — diesel, bad-road detours and security escorts ate the difference.

## The Situation

Fill rate, the share of order lines shipped complete, stood at 88% against a target of 97% — roughly one line in eight arrived short. Inventory turns had fallen to 8.3 times a year against a target of 12, meaning the ₦11.5 billion tied up in stock was both larger than it needed to be and sitting in the wrong places. Total logistics cost had climbed to 11.8% of revenue, roughly ₦11.3 billion a year, against an 8.5% benchmark — an excess of about ₦3.2 billion, larger than the company's entire EBIT (see **Exhibit 1**). Truck fill sat at 61% against 85% for good practice, and emergency transfers between distribution centres had tripled to ₦480 million a quarter — pure waste, in the operations team's own words. Left alone, the current run rate carried SaharaFresh into a second quarter below the 90% trip line, and the parallel-distributor clause would activate on its own.

## What Balogun Controlled

The first stage was diagnosis, and Balogun's team was already split on where the ₦3.2 billion excess actually lived. One view wanted to act directly on the network — close one distribution centre or build a new one, on the view that the footprint itself was the problem. A second argued transport was already the dominant cost — costly backhauls and ad-hoc hired vans were burning cash every week the analysis ran. A third held that nothing should be decided until cost-to-serve was quantified lane by lane and SKU class by SKU class, pointing to the 61% truck-fill figure already in hand. A fourth argued the lever was inventory: safety stock had been set by feel for years, and fixing the formulas would let service follow. Layered on top was a second question: whether demand had outgrown capacity, whether the four un-integrated networks were the structural cause, whether diesel and roads alone explained the transport premium, or whether warehouse execution was the weak link. A third question asked which lane and SKU class drove most of the miss — Abuja's mid-velocity range, no lane in particular, Lagos's slow-moving tail, or Port Harcourt's fast movers.

The second stage was the network itself (see **Exhibit 2**). Balogun could consolidate to three centres, closing one for about ₦900 million a year in fixed-cost savings against roughly ₦620 million in added transport cost and a longer Port Harcourt lane. She could keep all four distribution centres instead and re-cut their catchment boundaries to remove the overlap, at no change in fixed cost — though it meant renegotiating delivery routes and account assignments with every regional sales team, and left total network capacity unchanged. She could add a fifth cross-dock in the weak region for about ₦1.1 billion, in exchange for an estimated nine-point lift in that region's on-time-in-full. Or she could commit to all three moves together — consolidating, cross-docking and re-cutting catchments at once — across the full capital envelope. Alongside the footprint, she could push truck fill from 61% toward the 85% good-practice mark through routing and backhaul discipline, and decide whether to stop, halve or continue the ₦480-million-a-quarter emergency transfers.

The third stage was inventory policy. Balogun could review all 1,400 SKUs the same way — continuously, which reacted fastest but was heavy to administer across so many lines, or periodically, which was simpler to run but slower to react on fast-moving items — or match the review to the item, running fast-movers continuously and slow items periodically, at the cost of classifying — and periodically re-classifying — all 1,400 SKUs into the right cadence. She then set a target stock-availability level for the fast-moving A-items, among roughly 95%, 97% or 99% cover, and a separate target for the slow-moving, long-tail C-items, among roughly 85%, 92% or 99% cover, aware that cover and safety stock rose together. She could also raise or lower total safety stock as much as 30% below or 40% above current levels.

The fourth stage was the carrier decision, bound to a proposed 95% on-time-in-full service agreement. Balogun could keep the fleet in-house, invest roughly ₦1.4 billion in newer trucks and routing software, and carry the service risk herself. She could hand secondary distribution to a national partner at about 12% above her in-house cost per case, for a contractual 96% service commitment and no new capital spend. She could split the two, contracting the partner into the two weakest regions while keeping the fleet where it already performed — running two operating models, two cost bases and two service scorecards side by side. Or she could choose the partner with the lowest quoted rate per case — the cleanest number to compare across bids — and finalise the contract terms afterward. Whichever model she chose, she still had to set the penalty per point of on-time-in-full missed — a token amount, a material amount tied to a 90-day review, or none at all — decide how much of the first quarter went to immediate fixes rather than the longer redesign, and commit up to ₦2.5 billion of capital across the plan.

## Closing

By the evening of July 14, Balogun had the four distribution centres' cost data, the SKU-level inventory records and the principals' letter open on three separate screens, and thirty days to turn them into one plan.
