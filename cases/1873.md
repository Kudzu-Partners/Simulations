# Brücke Mobility: Pricing the Tier After the Backlash

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1873 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1873 — Price the Tier · v1.0 · August 2026

---

On 8 June 2026, a journalist published screenshots showing that Brücke Mobility GmbH had quietly tested individually adjusted prices on its mobility-subscription app: two customers buying the identical monthly plan had been shown €39 and €51, the difference generated from their device, location and browsing history. A consumer-rights group filed a complaint within hours, the hashtag #BrückeAbzocke — "Brücke rip-off" — began trending across the Rhine-Ruhr region, and a German consumer-protection authority opened an inquiry into whether the practice was lawful. The board suspended the pilot immediately and ordered a replacement: a pricing structure the company could defend in public, built and approved within three weeks.

Meike Brandt, Head of Pricing at Brücke Mobility, would design it. She had three weeks to move the company from its single flat price to a tiered structure that met the board's new margin and revenue targets while pulling the fairness score back into positive territory — before the authority's inquiry concluded and a low-price national rival's campaign peaked.

## The Protagonist

Brandt, 41, had led pricing at Brücke Mobility since 2022, after eight years setting subscription prices at a regional telecommunications operator. She reported to the Chief Financial Officer and had sat outside the small executive group that approved the personalised-pricing pilot the previous winter; she had inherited the fallout rather than caused it. Her mandate from the CFO was numeric and unambiguous: lift the margin, lift the revenue, and do it without another headline.

## The Decision

Brandt's task had four parts, each depending on the last. She first had to work out which of Brücke's customers the flat plan had mispriced. She then had to design a structure that priced different levels of service differently without repeating the personalisation that had caused the scandal. She then had to decide what would stop a valuable customer from simply claiming the cheapest option once that structure existed. And she had to decide how to explain all of it — to customers, to the press, and to the authority now examining her company's pricing.

## The Company and its Setting

Brücke Mobility GmbH, founded in Düsseldorf in 2017, sold a single monthly membership bundling unlimited regional public transport, e-bike and e-scooter minutes, and a discount on car-sharing into one app. It held the leading position in the Rhine-Ruhr metropolitan region: 640,000 active subscribers and €312 million in annual revenue, at a blended contribution margin of 34%. Every subscriber paid the same €42 a month, whether they rode twice a week or twice a day.

The company's own usage data told a simple story about that single price: a light, price-sensitive rider and a heavy, multimodal commuter paid identically for very different amounts of value. A national competitor had just launched a flat plan of its own at €35 a month, aimed at the price-sensitive end of Brücke's base, and had already begun courting students.

## The Situation

The damage from the leak was already measured. Brücke's internal fairness index — a 0-100 score built from customer surveys — had fallen from 72 to 49 since the screenshots surfaced, and subscriber churn had spiked to an annualised 19%. The CFO's brief to Brandt set three numeric targets for whatever replaced the flat plan: raise the blended contribution margin from 34% to at least 39%, lift revenue by at least 8%, and recover the fairness index above 65. A launch and communications budget of €2.4 million was available to fund whatever combination of research, tier-building, fencing and campaign she needed, and no more (see **Exhibit 1**).

If Brandt changed nothing, none of those three numbers had any reason to move. Margin stayed at 34%, the fairness index had no reason to climb back from 49, and the €35 entrant would keep pulling price-sensitive riders out of the base while the most valuable multimodal users kept paying what everyone else paid.

## What Brandt Controlled

In the first stage, Brandt had to name which customers the flat plan most failed to price correctly: the heavy multimodal users paying the same €42 as everyone despite using the service far more; the price-sensitive students and occasional riders drifting toward the €35 rival; the daily commuters, generally considered close to fairly priced already; or the multi-rider families, whose bundling potential nobody had monetised yet (see **Exhibit 2**). She then had to decide what logic the new structure would rest on: pricing that let customers sort themselves into the plan matching the value and features they wanted; an approach built on the same device, location and browsing signals the leaked pilot had used; a single across-the-board discount applied regardless of segment; or a price set mechanically from cost, without reference to what any customer would pay. Alongside both calls, she chose how much analytical effort to put into measuring each segment's willingness to pay and price sensitivity before committing to any numbers.

The second stage was the architecture itself. Brandt could build a ladder of three tiers, priced and featured so customers sorted themselves into the one matching their needs; leave the flat plan in place and simply adjust its price; cut a cheap entry-level plan to undercut the €35 rival across the whole base; or split the base into seven narrow tiers for maximum granularity. Within whichever architecture she chose, she had to set an entry-level monthly price — anywhere from €28 to €42, against a rival selling at €35 — and a top-tier price for the heaviest users, anywhere from €45 to €80. She also had to decide how sharply value stepped up from one tier to the next: a gap narrow enough that a heavy user might as well buy the cheaper tier, or wide enough that each tier only made sense for the customer it was built for.

The third stage was about what would keep a customer in the tier intended for them. Brandt had to choose the main test a customer would pass to unlock a lower price: a controllable, visible criterion such as verified student status, a committed term or off-peak access; the same device, location and browsing signals the leaked pilot had used; no test at all, trusting customers to pick honestly for themselves; or a blend of visible criteria with a few quieter data-based ones. Separately, she had to decide whether the cheaper tiers also carried a transactional condition — an annual commitment paired with off-peak access, a harder 24-month lock-in with steep exit fees, or none at all, leaving every tier available month to month. And she decided how much effort to spend trying to break her own rules before a customer did.

The fourth and final stage was the launch itself, conducted while the authority's inquiry was still open. Brandt had to decide what Brücke would say publicly: an explanation that retired personalised pricing openly and stated that every customer on a given tier now paid the same price for it; silence, launching the new tiers without addressing the scandal that caused them; or a statement framing the leak as a misunderstanding. She had to decide how to move the existing 640,000 subscribers onto the new structure — gradually, with loyal customers protected near their old price for a period; immediately, with every account switched overnight; or with no transition plan, leaving customers to pick a new tier in their own time. She had to decide how to answer the €35 entrant — with a fenced, value-differentiated entry offer, by matching its price across the base, or by holding her prices and betting loyalty would keep customers in place. And she set how much to invest in transparent communication to support whichever choice she made.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 8 June, Brandt opened a blank spreadsheet with four tabs, one per stage of the redesign, the board's three-week clock running in the corner of her screen. The consumer authority had not yet asked for a formal response. The €35 rival had not yet launched its next campaign. She had a segmentation study to run, a ladder to price, fences to design and test, and a public explanation to write — and every one of the four choices in front of her fed into the same fairness index that had just fallen twenty-three points in a matter of weeks.
