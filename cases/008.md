## Abstract

FinSalud, a Latin American health-microinsurance startup, closed a USD 400,000 seed round in November 2025 at a USD 2.8M post-money valuation with two angel investors and a small seed fund. By January 2026 the company had USD 380,000 remaining, a USD 25,000 monthly burn, and a 16-month runway if the team held. The CEO and co-founder, Valentina Aranda, faced the Round 1 decision that would commit the company's early identity: how much to spend on customer discovery; which features to build in the MVP; whom to hire first; whether to price per-member, per-claim, or as a flat subscription; and how to reach customers — direct digital, partner pharmacy, or employer channel. Eight scenario-variants of the simulation existed; FinSalud was the health-microinsurance draw. The same decision architecture would govern the three rounds that followed — Round 2 persevere-or-pivot, Round 3 Series A, Round 4 Series B or cash-out. The case ends with Aranda at her kitchen table the Sunday night before the first operating week.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate a Round 1 resource-allocation decision under strict runway constraint, with full-cycle downstream implications.
2. Analyze the trade-off between discovery spend and build spend and the conditions under which each dominates.
3. Design an MVP feature set against customer-discovery evidence rather than founder intuition.
4. Diagnose cap-table consequences of early hiring and pricing choices and model their Series-A impact.
5. Recommend a Round 1 package and defend it under a hostile Round-2 / Round-3 projection.

On the night of **January 11, 2026**, **Valentina Aranda** sat at the kitchen table of her one-bedroom apartment in Bogotá with four documents open: the FinSalud bank statement (USD 378,412 of cash), the draft Round 1 operating plan, a list of six candidate hires in a spreadsheet, and a one-page letter from her seed investor, Matías Kessler, dated December 28. The letter read, in part: *"You have one shot to show me a wedge by quarter end. Don't boil the ocean."* FinSalud's first full operating week would begin on Monday. Aranda had twelve hours to commit a Round 1 plan.

---

## The Protagonist

Valentina Aranda, 32, had co-founded FinSalud in September 2025 with Lucas Duarte, 31. Aranda had spent six years as a product manager at a regional digital-bank and eighteen months at a global reinsurance firm; Duarte had been a data-science lead at a health-tech startup. The two had met at a regional fintech conference in 2023 and had spent fourteen months researching the underinsured-middle-income segment in three Andean countries before incorporating. Aranda held 42% of the common stock; Duarte held 38%; the seed round had taken 12% (18% fully diluted including the 6% option pool). Aranda's husband held a full-time salary; Duarte had taken a three-month leave from his previous employer, expiring April 1.

---

## The Decision

Aranda had to finalize, by 8:00 a.m. on January 12, the FinSalud Round 1 plan — covering January through March 2026 (Q1, approximately USD 75K of expected spend). The plan had five dimensions:

1. **Customer-discovery budget:** USD 5K to USD 30K.
2. **MVP feature set:** which two or three features to build in Q1 from a ten-feature backlog.
3. **First hires:** two hires from a six-candidate shortlist (senior backend engineer, product designer, growth marketer, senior actuary, claims operations lead, clinical advisor part-time).
4. **Pricing model:** per-member-per-month, per-claim-capitated, flat-subscription, or hybrid.
5. **Channel:** direct digital (paid + organic), partner pharmacy (Farmacia del Ahorro test already informally offered), or employer (three SME pilots offered).

The plan would commit roughly USD 75K of the USD 378K remaining and define the identity of the company through the first external investor update on April 15.

---

## Company Background

FinSalud, incorporated September 2025 in Bogotá, proposed a microinsurance product targeted at Latin American middle-income households whose employers did not offer health coverage and whose out-of-pocket exposure — through informal consultations, pharmacy-based medication, and occasional urgent-care visits — averaged between USD 40 and USD 110 per household per month. The founding thesis, developed through 84 customer interviews during 2025, held that this segment would accept a USD 18–28 per-month coverage product if it was (a) digital-first, (b) paid straightforward caps on three defined use-cases (pharmacy, consultation, urgent-care), and (c) avoided the paperwork and denial-rate of traditional Colombian private insurance.

The November 2025 seed round had closed at a USD 2.8M post-money valuation on a convertible note with a USD 2.4M valuation cap and a 20% discount at the next priced round. The lead investor was Kessler Ventures (USD 250K); two angels (a former fintech CEO and a regional health-tech founder) had contributed USD 100K and USD 50K respectively. A 6% option pool had been carved at closing.

## Industry and Market Context

The Latin American microinsurance market, sized at approximately USD 2.4B in 2025 across six countries, had grown at 18% annually since 2020 and was projected to grow at 14% through 2028. The principal drivers were a growing middle-income segment uncovered by employer plans, the regional proliferation of digital wallets and mobile-money rails, and regulatory reforms in Colombia, Mexico, and Peru that had, between 2021 and 2024, created new product categories for digital-first insurers.

Competition had intensified. Two regional insurtechs had raised Series B rounds in 2024 at valuations implying USD 180–260M enterprise value; three early-stage entrants had raised Series A between USD 8M and USD 18M. The capital environment for 2026 had tightened: Latin American VC deployments in Q3 2025 had fallen 34% year-over-year from the 2022 peak, and median Series A post-money valuations for fintech startups had compressed from USD 42M to USD 28M.

Regulatory context, specifically in Colombia, required microinsurance products to be underwritten by a licensed insurer. FinSalud had, in October 2025, signed a letter of intent with a mid-sized domestic insurer to act as the underwriter for its first product, conditional on product sign-off by April 30, 2026.

---

## The Situation

### How we got here

The Aranda/Duarte founder pair had spent fourteen months in discovery before incorporation. Their 2025 customer research had yielded a clear wedge — the "first bad month" problem, in which a mid-income household absorbed a USD 120–250 unplanned medical cost and missed a utility payment or a loan instalment — and a pricing anchor around USD 22 per month. What the research had not yielded was a clear channel. The pharmacy-partner test (informal) had shown enthusiasm but no price-to-enroll conversion. The employer pilot offers had been warm but slow — HR leads at SMEs had a four-to-six month buying cycle. Direct digital had not been tested at all.

### What Aranda knew

The cap table permitted one further seed-extension before the priced Series A, but only if the extension closed before April 1 and only if the note-cap did not reset. Kessler Ventures had committed pro-rata rights but would not lead a Series A. The underwriter LOI expired April 30. The option pool allowed two senior hires at the current grants.

### What Aranda did not know

Whether the pricing wedge of USD 22 per month would hold once paid-acquisition was added to the equation. Whether the pharmacy test could be converted to a commercial arrangement. Whether Duarte's leave would, in fact, expire April 1 and what FinSalud's position would be if he returned to salaried employment before product launch. Whether Kessler's "one shot" language was a coaching posture or a real deadline.

### What others were saying

**Lucas Duarte** (co-founder, CTO), pressed for a tight-feature MVP and one senior-engineer hire. "Two features: pharmacy cap, consultation cap. One engineer. Paid ads. Fast loop."

**Matías Kessler** (lead investor), in the December 28 letter, pressed for pharmacy-channel focus. "Pharmacy Del Ahorro said yes. Go yes. A wedge, not a platform."

**María Elena Cárdenas** (advisor, former insurance executive), pressed for actuarial discipline. "Claims-operations lead in Round 1. Senior actuary part-time. Don't hit April 30 without an underwriter-ready loss model."

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — Wedge-and-Ship (the Duarte plan):** Discovery USD 8K, MVP features = Pharmacy Cap + Consultation Cap; hires = senior backend engineer + growth marketer; pricing = PMPM USD 22; channel = direct digital. Q1 spend USD 72K. Burn rate shifts to USD 28K/month. Fast iteration; weakest actuarial rigor; hardest to underwrite at April 30.

- **Option B — Kessler Wedge (the investor plan):** Discovery USD 12K, MVP features = Pharmacy Cap + Consultation Cap + Enrollment Kiosk; hires = senior backend engineer + product designer; pricing = PMPM USD 20; channel = partner pharmacy. Q1 spend USD 78K. Burn rate USD 29K/month. Narrowest wedge; fastest validation; highest partner-dependency risk.

- **Option C — Underwriter-Ready (the Cárdenas plan):** Discovery USD 5K, MVP features = Pharmacy Cap + Consultation Cap + Claims Dashboard; hires = senior actuary part-time + claims-operations lead; pricing = hybrid USD 18 PMPM + per-claim co-pay; channel = employer SME (three pilots). Q1 spend USD 74K. Burn rate USD 26K/month. Slowest top-of-funnel; highest probability of an April 30 underwriter sign-off; highest cap-table efficiency into Series A.

(See **Exhibit 3** for the modeled four-round trajectories under each option, including the April 30 underwriter-sign-off probability and the Series A close probability.)

Duarte's leave expired in eighty days. The Kessler letter sat in her drafts folder with a reply she had not yet written. The first full operating week began in thirty-two hours.
