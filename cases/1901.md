# Massif Business School: The Dean's Bet

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1901 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1901 — *Dean's Bet* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Wednesday, 30 September 2026, Marianne Delcourt read two documents that had reached her four hours apart and saw that they were one problem. The first was the European Master-in-Management ranking, published at seven o'clock: Massif Business School had fallen from 32nd to 41st, its sharpest single-year drop in a decade, with almost all of the loss in two component scores, career progression and international mobility. The second was the minute of the previous evening's board meeting, which approved a one-off strategic investment envelope of EUR 14 million over three years, financed from reserves and a development loan, and instructed the dean to answer the ranking and sharpen what the school stood for, inside that number.

The board would reconvene for its strategy session on 2 December 2026, nine weeks later. By then Delcourt had to bring an allocation of the EUR 14 million, a statement of what Massif Business School was for, and a plan for the ranking — and defend the three together before a board chair and a trustee who had already asked, in writing, what the school intended to stop doing. The investment cases sitting in her inbox came to about EUR 31 million.

## The Protagonist

Marianne Delcourt, 51, had been Dean and Director General since March 2022. She joined the faculty in 2008 as a lecturer in operations, took over the specialised master's portfolio in 2014, and served four years as associate dean for programmes before the board appointed her. Her mandate had been to grow executive education without diluting what made the school recognisable. She had spent the first three years of it on an accreditation renewal and a campus refinancing. Her contract ran to 2029, against indicators the board set itself and would set again on 2 December.

## The Decision

Delcourt had to decide what the EUR 14 million would buy, what the school would claim to be while spending it, and what it would stop competing on — and hold those three answers together tightly enough that the board saw one plan rather than three.

## The Company and its Setting

Massif Business School was a grande école founded in Grenoble in 1968. It held the three accreditations European schools competed for — AACSB, EQUIS and AMBA. It enrolled about 4,800 students across degree programmes from bachelor's to executive MBA, and ran a custom and open executive-education unit. It employed 165 permanent faculty and about 620 staff on an annual budget of EUR 92 million, and had closed the previous year with an operating surplus of EUR 3.6 million, or 3.9% (see **Exhibit 1**). Degree tuition supplied 48% of revenue, executive education 27%, public grants and apprenticeship-tax funding 15%, and corporate partnerships and philanthropy the remaining 10%.

For twenty years the school had sold itself on responsible innovation and entrepreneurship. The claim was credible and thinly funded. In the spring of 2026 two larger competitors, each able to put roughly three times the MBS budget behind a launch, had opened flagship programmes on sustainable business and on entrepreneurship.

The development loan behind the envelope carried one financial condition: the school had to hold an operating surplus of at least 2.0%.

## The Situation

The school's own analysis put a price on the ranking. Each place lost correlated with about EUR 350,000 of degree tuition at the following intake, through a weaker applicant pool, a lower acceptance yield and heavier discounting. Nine places, unanswered, put roughly EUR 3.0 million of annual tuition at risk within two intakes (see **Exhibit 1**). Neither component that had moved was a marketing line: career progression came from alumni salary and seniority three years out, mobility from exchange places and terms abroad actually taken.

Four investment cases had been prepared for the board (see **Exhibit 2**): EUR 8 million for a flagship online executive-education portfolio; EUR 11 million for careers services, exchange partnerships and scholarships on the degree side; EUR 9 million for twelve research-active hires and the chairs to hold them; and EUR 3 million to reposition the brand. Together they came to EUR 31 million, and the envelope covered about 45% of that.

Two star professors, each anchoring a chair worth about EUR 600,000 a year in grants and in the research record on which EQUIS re-accreditation rested, were being courted by rivals; both had asked to see the faculty plan before the December board.

Delcourt's scorecard held four numbers, and the board would judge her on them 18 to 36 months out. A differentiation score, maintained by the marketing office on a nought-to-one-hundred scale from applicant and recruiter surveys, stood at 30. A reputation index built from the school's ranking positions, on the same scale, stood at 35 — the value corresponding to 41st. The operating surplus stood at 3.9% against the 2.0% floor. A portfolio-balance measure, which the finance committee used to test whether a year's spending added up to one identifiable commitment or to a share for each claimant, stood at 25.

If the envelope went unallocated, the board would hold it, the tuition erosion would compound toward the EUR 3.0 million figure, and the chair had said in September that a dean who could not choose would be asked why the board needed one.

## What Delcourt Controlled

She had four moves and one meeting.

She had to put a diagnosis in writing, choosing among four readings of the fall: mostly structural with parts money could reach; purely money-fixable, so careers services and exchanges would return the places at the next intake; a presentation problem in which the method under-counted the school; or ordinary year-to-year movement needing no action. She had to state what the distance between EUR 31 million and EUR 14 million meant — that a few things be funded completely and the rest not at all, that each pillar take a proportional share, or that more be borrowed against reserves and all four funded in full. And she had to record, as a percentage, how far the responsible-innovation ground was already occupied.

She had to set the position. She could deepen the existing one into a narrower space, pivot to an adjacent claim such as the management of artificial intelligence or career outcomes, broaden toward a generalist top-40 school competing on every dimension, or spend against the two rivals directly on the ground they had entered. She had to say, as a percentage, how much the school would actually drop. And she had to choose how to speak about the ranking: to name which drivers the position moved and which it accepted losing, to promise both the position and a return to 32nd, or to keep the two subjects apart.

She had to divide the EUR 14 million across executive education, degrees, faculty and brand, in figures that summed to 14.0. She had to set the share of the envelope spent in Year 1, anywhere between 20% and 90%. And she had to decide whether to retain both professors, one, or neither.

Then, on 2 December, she had to present it: as a concentrated commitment naming the ranking trade-off she accepted, as a recovery plan returning to 32nd within two intakes, or as four funded pillars leaving no department out. She had to answer the charge of spreading EUR 14 million too thin — by naming the commitments and what they let the school do that it could not do before, by arguing that 45% of each ask still advanced all four plans, or by asking for a larger envelope. She had to answer the charge of abandoning the school's identity — that the funded assets deepened the position, that rank mattered more than a distinctive claim, or that the claim was already established and the envelope belonged where the ranking was decided. And she had to say how firmly, from nought to one hundred, she would commit to the four numbers.

## Closing

At half past eight Delcourt laid the four investment cases side by side and wrote EUR 14.0 million at the top of a blank sheet. The board would sit on 2 December. She had nine weeks, four asks worth EUR 31 million, and one envelope.
