# The Ferrer Siblings: The Sleep-at-Night Test

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1252 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1252 — *Risk & Return — The Sleep-at-Night Test* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Saturday, April 18, 2026, at 15:40, Nuria Albarén laid three letters from the notary on Carmen Ferrer's kitchen table in Zaragoza. Each confirmed that €10,000 from the estate of Ramón Ferrer had been paid into an account in the name of one of his three children: Sara, Mateo and Carmen. The money had sat in those accounts, in cash, for six weeks. The three wanted it invested, they wanted Albarén to do it, and Mateo's train back to Madrid left at 19:30.

Albarén had until then to propose a portfolio for each account and see it signed. Her practice moved no client money without a rehearsal first. Its planning software would run each proposed portfolio through a replay of a 2008-scale crash, stop at the deepest point and ask her, account by account, whether to hold, sell to cash or add €2,000. Only after that would the siblings sign their final allocations. Three portfolios, three calls at the bottom and three signatures, all before the train.

## The Protagonist

Albarén was forty-four. She had spent eleven years on the private-client desk of a Spanish savings bank before opening Albarén Planificación, a two-person, fee-only practice, in 2019. She charged by the hour, sold no products and had looked after Ramón Ferrer's modest savings for the last six years of his life. She knew his children mostly through his descriptions of them. She had built hundreds of portfolios. She had never built three on one afternoon, for one family, with the same amount of money and three different lives attached.

## The Decision

For each sibling, Albarén had to settle how the €10,000 would be split between equity funds, bonds and cash, in whole percentages summing to 100. At the bottom of the replayed crash she then had to make one call per account: hold, sell to cash, or put in another €2,000. Finally she had to look at both sets of choices together and settle the allocations the siblings would actually sign.

The difficulty was not arithmetic. Each sibling had told her something about the money that pulled against something else they had told her (see Exhibit 1).

## The Company and its Setting

The Ferrer siblings had been born forty-three years apart, across their father's two marriages, and met as a family perhaps twice a year. Ramón Ferrer, a retired surveyor, died in February 2026. His will left each child the same €10,000.

Sara, seventeen, was in her final year of school. Her university fees were covered by a separate fund her mother had set up, so this money had no job for decades; Albarén's file gave it a fifty-year horizon. "I've never had any money," Sara said, "so I don't know what losing it feels like."

Mateo, thirty-five, worked in IT in Madrid, rented with his partner and paid into a workplace pension. He thought of the money as retirement savings, twenty-five years away, but the couple had not ruled out buying a flat in seven to ten years. "About twenty-five years if it's retirement," he said, "about seven if it's a flat."

Carmen, sixty, was widowed, translated part-time and planned to retire at sixty-five. She owned her flat outright. For her the €10,000 was a cushion she might draw on in any year between sixty-five and seventy-five: five years to the first possible withdrawal, fifteen to the last. "I don't need it to grow," she said. "I need it to be there."

## The Situation

Albarén Planificación built every portfolio from three building blocks (see Exhibit 2). Global equity index funds owned slices of about 1,400 companies. Over the long run they had returned roughly 6% a year after inflation, with a typical swing of about 18% in a single year. The bond sleeve was always held half in government bonds and half in investment-grade corporate bonds. It had returned roughly 2% a year after inflation, with a swing of about 6%. Cash and money-market funds had roughly kept pace with inflation and almost never moved. The software stated every figure in today's purchasing power, as a long-run assumption and not a promise.

The practice's planning sheet printed a rule of thumb: a hundred minus the client's age in equities. That gave 83% for Sara, 65% for Mateo and 40% for Carmen. A footnote added that a hundred and ten minus age reflected longer life expectancy. Neither line mentioned Sara's nerves, Mateo's possible flat or Carmen's cushion.

If nothing changed, the money would stay where it was. Each account held €10,000 in cash. In six weeks it had earned nothing in real terms, and five more years in cash would most likely end at about the same €10,000 in today's money.

The rehearsal was fixed in advance. It replayed a crash on the scale of 2008–09 as if it began in October 2026, six months out. Over twelve months equity funds fell about 40% from their peak. Government bonds rallied as money looked for safety, corporate bonds wobbled, and cash did not move. At that trough the software paused for her three calls. It then played four more years of recovery and showed each account's value at the end of a five-year window. Adding €2,000 meant new money from the sibling, invested in equity funds at the bottom. All three had confirmed they could find it if she asked.

## What Albarén Controlled

In the first session she set three splits, one per account: the percentage in equity funds, in the bond sleeve and in cash. Each split had to sum to 100, and an empty slice had to be written down as zero. From then on the file tracked each account in euros: at the start, at the trough and at the end of the five-year window.

At the trough she made one call per sibling. To hold meant leaving the portfolio as it stood and waiting for the recovery. To sell meant moving the whole account into cash at its trough value. To add meant putting another €2,000 into equity funds at the bottom.

In the last session, with the crash behind her, she reset all three splits. These were the allocations the siblings would sign. The practice's file review would then mark the afternoon out of 100 and place it in a band. The review looked at how well each allocation suited its owner, at the three calls made at the trough and at what the three accounts were worth at the end of the window.

## Closing

At 15:52 Carmen put the coffee on. Sara read a headline from her phone aloud: "Bull market enters its fourth year." Mateo asked whether the flat changed anything. Albarén opened the planning sheet on her laptop and looked at the three empty rows.
