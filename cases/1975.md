# Mekhala Asset Management: Three Points Above the Floor

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1975 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1975 — *ESG Investor* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Saturday, 14 March 2026, with the Thai market shut for the weekend, Sirikan Charoensuk read two numbers that described the same eleven trading days. The SET ESG Index, the benchmark of the fund she ran, had fallen 9.0%. The Mekhala ESG Leaders Fund had fallen 12.4%. The gap was 340 basis points, and it had not come from names picked badly. It had come from names picked deliberately: the shock hit renewables, consumer staples and healthcare, which the fund overweighted, and spared the energy and materials companies it held less of because of how they scored.

Charoensuk had seventeen days. On 31 March the fund's quarter-end net asset value would be struck, and with it the quarterly audit of the weighted environmental, social and governance (ESG) score that ฿12,000,000,000 of the fund's capital was contractually attached to. She had to decide what the ฿18,000,000,000 portfolio was allowed to own, in what size, and how fast to change it — against four limits she could not all satisfy at once.

## The Protagonist

Sirikan Charoensuk, 43, had run the ESG Leaders Fund since Mekhala launched it, after seven years as a Thai equity analyst at a regional broker. She had written the fund's eligibility rules herself and had defended the sector tilt through two earlier drawdowns, both shallower and recovered within a quarter. The European investors had subscribed partly on the strength of her published rating discipline. The book that had lost 340 basis points in eleven days was the book she had designed.

## The Decision

Charoensuk had to set what the fund could own and in what size, and how fast to trade into that shape.

The first setting was the minimum ESG rating eligible for the portfolio. At **≥CCC**, the entire rebounding energy and materials complex became investable, the fastest route back toward the benchmark, and the fund would hold names at the bottom of the rating scale it was sold on. At **≥BB**, most of the rebounding names became available while the lowest band stayed out; the return gap closed more slowly than at ≥CCC and low-rated weight still entered the book. At **≥BBB**, the investable universe stayed at the standard the fund had been marketed on, and the gap closed more slowly still. At **≥A**, everything the fund could buy carried a high rating, and the eligible list shrank, concentrating the portfolio in fewer positions and sectors.

The second setting was the active weight permitted in holdings with open corporate social responsibility (CSR) controversies — in practice, two positions (see **Exhibit 2**). Holding them at **full weight** paid no execution cost, kept exposure she considered mispriced and left the fund's weighted score dependent on what the rating agencies decided. **Trimming to a capped exposure budget** paid part of the execution cost, removed part of the exposure and left the rest in the book. **Capping near zero** paid the full execution cost, crystallised the loss at current prices and removed the exposure before the agencies acted.

The third was quantity: how much of the book, from 0% to 30%, to rotate into the rebounding low-rated energy and materials names. Every point rotated moved volatility and the return gap in one direction and the fund's weighted rating in the other.

## The Company and its Setting

Mekhala Asset Management was a Bangkok equity boutique. Its flagship, the Mekhala ESG Leaders Fund, was a ฿18,000,000,000 long-only Thai and ASEAN equity strategy benchmarked to the SET ESG Index and sold on one promise: index-beating returns from a portfolio whose weighted ESG rating sat near the top of the Thai sustainable-equity universe.

Two-thirds of the fund — ฿12,000,000,000 — came from European institutional investors whose subscription documents carried a covenant: the portfolio's weighted ESG score could never fall below the 70th percentile of the SET ESG universe, audited each quarter. Two consecutive audits below the floor released that ฿12,000,000,000 to redeem at no penalty, which would leave the fund below the size at which it worked. Alongside it sat a risk budget written by Mekhala's own board: annualised volatility no higher than 18%, and a maximum drawdown of 15% at which the board's standing rule liquidated the book.

## The Situation

On 14 March the fund stood against those limits as set out in **Exhibit 1**. The weighted ESG score was at the 73rd percentile — three points of cover above the covenant floor. Thirty-day annualised volatility had gone from 14% before the shock to 22%, against the 18% ceiling, so the fund was out of compliance. The drawdown was 12.4% against the 15% stop, roughly 260 basis points of room. The return gap was 340 basis points. The firm's risk committee had already moved the fund's covenant status flag to "at risk".

The complication was not in the aggregates but in two positions, at 4.2% and 3.1% of the fund, both with open CSR controversies — a labour-rights dispute at a supplier of the first, a community water complaint against the second — and both flagged by rating agencies for possible downgrade within thirty days. Mekhala's ESG analyst put it in one line: a downgrade on either name could take the whole weighted score through the floor on its own. Liquidity had tightened with the market: the desk put clearing the 4.2% position in one session at about 150 basis points of market impact, roughly ฿11,000,000 of net asset value.

Inside the firm the argument had settled into two positions. The trading and client-relations side wanted the rotation done in size and done quickly: a fund lagging its benchmark by 340 basis points would lose the capital either way. The research side wanted the threshold held and the two flagged positions closed: the covenant, not the benchmark, was what could take ฿12,000,000,000 out of the door.

If Charoensuk changed nothing before 31 March, the arithmetic was not dramatic. Volatility would remain above its ceiling. The return gap would stay near 340 basis points. And the two flagged holdings would sit in the book through the agencies' whole thirty-day window, with three percentile points standing between the fund and the first of the two audits that could release ฿12,000,000,000.

## What Charoensuk Controlled

She was measured on four numbers, recorded side by side after every move. The weighted ESG score, expressed as a percentile of the SET ESG universe, was the covenant number. Thirty-day annualised volatility was the mandate number. Drawdown, peak to trough, was the board's number. The return gap against the SET ESG Index, in basis points, was the clients' number, negative when the fund lagged.

Against them she held the rating threshold, the permitted CSR weight and the size of the rotation. She held execution: whether to keep a flagged position, clear it in a single session at the impact the desk had quoted, or work it out over several sessions at a lower cost and a longer exposure. She held what any proceeds bought — higher-rated names that lifted the weighted score, the rebounding low-rated names that closed the gap, or nothing. She could add a hedging sleeve against the index to pull volatility down without changing what the fund owned, add leverage to close the gap faster, or do neither. She could decide how much distance above the floor to carry — none, landing on the floor and spending nothing on cushion; three points; or six points, enough to absorb a downgrade or an index reconstitution, paid for in return foregone.

She would also have to give an account of it. The lead European investor would want to know what she led with — the recovered return, a commitment to dilute the tilt from here, or the case that it had been managed through the drawdown rather than abandoned — how she explained what the fund's rating discipline was worth to a client, and what standing policy she proposed for the next disruption. Neither the covenant nor the board's risk budget could be reopened inside seventeen days.

## Closing

By mid-morning Charoensuk had the four limits written across the top of a single sheet and the book listed beneath them. Seventeen days to the audit, three points of cover, two flagged positions and a thirty-day window belonging to somebody else. The market opened on Monday.
