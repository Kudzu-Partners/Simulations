# Calderwood Logistics: The Resilience Portfolio

On the morning of Monday, 9 March 2026, Miriam Okafor read the incident report and the wish list side by side and realised they described the same problem from opposite ends. The incident report totalled the cost of a 48-hour failure of Calderwood Logistics' core warehouse-management system five days earlier: three of the company's eight distribution centres halted by a failed supplier update with no tested fallback, GBP 1.1 million in penalties and recovery already booked. The wish list was longer and stranger — fourteen proposals from across the business, each pitched as the fix that would stop it happening again, adding up to GBP 14 million against a budget the board had just capped at GBP 6.0 million.

Okafor was Calderwood's Strategic Change Director. The board had given her 120 days to turn those two documents into a single board-ready plan, and to defend it in person to a chair who had already said, in the room, that Calderwood had been managing risk on luck.

## The Protagonist

Miriam Okafor, 44, had spent eleven years at Calderwood, the last three as Strategic Change Director. She had joined in 2016 — the year the company listed — as a continuous-improvement manager, and had built her name finding cost out of the distribution network: fewer buffer stocks, fewer redundant systems, fewer people covering for absent colleagues. It worked; Calderwood's margins were the envy of the sector. It was also, she now had to acknowledge to the board that had promoted her for it, part of why one failed update had stopped three sites at once. The programme was hers partly because nobody understood the network's dependencies as well as she did, and partly because she had helped build them.

## The Decision

Across the 120 days, Okafor had to produce one plan with four linked parts: diagnose where Calderwood was genuinely exposed, from a register with more red flags than the budget could address; test the evidence behind every proposed fix rather than fund whatever sounded current; split a fixed GBP 6.0 million between protecting against a failure that had already happened and building capacity for one nobody had named yet; and defend the result to a board that wanted to know whether any of it actually differed from what it had always funded.

## The Company and its Setting

Calderwood Logistics plc, headquartered in Warrington and listed on the London market since 2016, ran eight regional distribution centres and a transport fleet serving retail, healthcare and industrial clients across Britain. Founded in 1991, it employed 4,300 people, mostly in warehouses and on the road, and reported 2025 revenue of GBP 620 million at a 6% operating margin — a return that made it one of the most efficient operators in third-party logistics, and, as the board was now discovering, one of the most exposed. A decade of stripping out buffer stock, backup systems and spare management capacity had produced excellent numbers in calm years and a string of near-misses in difficult ones. Weeks earlier, a competitor had been forced offline for days by a ransomware attack, a fact the board had registered with more anxiety than sympathy. Then the outage had happened to Calderwood itself.

## The Situation

The outage had cost more than the GBP 1.1 million in direct penalties and recovery. Calderwood's largest healthcare client, a contract worth GBP 48 million a year, had put the relationship under review pending proof the failure would not recur. The board's response was to charter Okafor's programme, cap its two-year budget at GBP 6.0 million with no top-up, and set two numeric bars (Exhibit 1): a Resilience Score of at least 65 out of 100 to count as board-ready, and at least 70 to be called, in the board's own words, genuinely resilient. Calderwood was starting a long way back: existential-vulnerability coverage — how many of the could-kill-us gaps were actually closed — stood at 10 out of 100 that morning, and adaptive capacity — the ability to sense and adjust to a shock nobody had yet named — at 12 out of 100, with none of it committed. Left alone, the healthcare contract would stay under review, the dependency that had just failed would stay unpatched, and the network would still be one bad update from halting again.

## What Okafor Controlled

Her team's diagnostic pack pointed four ways at once. One reading named the thin management bench and an already-fatigued workforce as the real danger; a second pointed at cyber exposure, given that a rival had just been forced offline by ransomware; a third pointed at the warehouse-management dependency that had already halted three sites with no tested fallback; a fourth named the 6% margin itself, which left almost no cushion against any shock. Beyond naming one, Okafor had to decide how the other thirteen register items got worked: rank everything by likely damage and fund the worst two or three, treat all fourteen items as equally urgent at once, chase the cheapest fixes regardless of what they actually protected, or tackle whichever caused the most recent incident and move to the next. She also had to set, in blunt percentage terms, how openly her team's report would name the cultural habit of prizing efficiency over adaptability, rather than stopping at symptoms.

The wish list then needed testing for evidence, not enthusiasm. Proposals could be judged by taking vendor pilot data at face value, since the vendors ran the trials themselves; by rating each proposal on the quality and source of its evidence before funding it; by matching whatever peer firms in the sector were reportedly buying this year; or by funding whatever the leadership team in the room felt most strongly about. The sharpest test was an AI "resilience platform," pushed hard by one board member on a single vendor case study and priced at GBP 2.4 million — more than a third of the whole budget: commit the full amount and back the board member's enthusiasm, commission a smaller GBP 0.4 million pilot to gather evidence first, or decline it for now as one case study short of evidence. Separately, she had to rate how strong the evidence really was that a tested failover for the warehouse-management system would work — strong, moderate, or weak.

With priorities and evidence ratings set, Okafor had to build the portfolio inside the fixed GBP 6.0 million, split across three purposes (Exhibit 2): defensive spending on a tested failover, backups, continuity and insurance; progressive spending on sensing, adaptive capacity, learning loops and slack; and a cultural share embedding adaptability over pure efficiency, measured and owned by someone. A pound committed to one purpose could not serve another. Whatever the split, she had to say plainly whether the tested failover was funded first inside the defensive share, or left for a later budget while the rest of the portfolio went ahead.

Finally, presenting to the chair, Okafor had to say why any of this differed from every risk programme Calderwood had funded before: because the portfolio was rated on evidence and balanced between defence and adaptability; because it was a larger version of the same approach, funded more generously; or because more insurance and backups meant the company was now covered. She had to say how resilience would survive the programme's own end: as an eighteen-month project that gets delivered and formally closed; as an ongoing capability with named ownership, protected when cost pressure returned; or as something re-audited yearly and then re-chartered only after the next break. And she had to commit, for the board's tracking, to recovery time alone, reported after the next incident; to showing that the GBP 6.0 million had been spent on schedule; or to measuring both the resilience score and the disruption-recovery time together — and to state, in one number, how hard she would defend that answer.

## Closing

By nine that morning, Okafor had pinned the incident report to one side of her office wall and the wish list to the other, a hundred and twenty days marked between them. The chair wanted a plan, not good intentions. The healthcare client wanted proof before its next review. And the network was still running on the same thin margins, the same single points of failure, and the same decade-old instinct to cut first, ask later.
