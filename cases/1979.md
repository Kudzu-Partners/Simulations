# Escuela de Alta Gerencia del Pacífico: The Margin Floor

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1979 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1979 — *Fund the Program* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 2 March 2026, Marisol Cavero left the rector's office with two sentences on the back of the meeting agenda. Central funding for her unit would not grow next year. The contribution margin the unit had to return to the university had risen from 32% to 35%.

Cavero directed the Escuela de Alta Gerencia del Pacífico (EAGP), the executive-education arm of a Lima business school. EAGP had closed the previous year at 31%. On revenue of S/ 24,000,000, the four points between where the unit stood and where the rector now required it to stand were worth about S/ 1,000,000 of contribution a year, none of it coming from the university. She had 120 days to decide where it would come from — the term left on the lease of the floor above her classrooms, and the last chance to add rooms in San Isidro this decade.

## The Protagonist

Marisol Cavero, 44, had directed EAGP since 2019. She joined in 2012 as a programme director on the corporate side and built the in-company line from a handful of workshops into nearly half the unit's revenue. Her finance controller had written, in a review she had read, that she had never run the unit against a hard number. Until March 2026 she had not; the central subsidy had absorbed the difference. The rector had told her that if the unit finished below the floor two years running it would be folded into the main MBA office, and her post with it.

## The Decision

Cavero had to close a four-point gap in blended contribution margin inside one budget cycle, committing to the answer in parts rather than as one plan: how to read the unit's own accounts, then prices for the coming catalogue, then how teaching capacity divided between the two revenue lines and what to say to the client that had reopened its contract, and last the lease. Each commitment narrowed the ones after it.

## The Company and its Setting

EAGP had operated since 2009 from a campus in San Isidro, Lima, with a satellite classroom in Surco. Eighteen full-time staff ran it; sixty adjuncts taught for it, paid by the session. It sold two things (see Exhibit 2). The open-enrollment portfolio was fourteen short certificate programmes in finance, leadership, supply chain and digital subjects, bought by individuals or expensed to employers; it produced about 55% of revenue. The custom line designed bespoke programmes for corporate clients, carried the unit's best margins, arrived in irregular blocks tied to particular teachers, and produced the other 45%.

What EAGP could sell was bounded by where and by whom it taught. The six San Isidro classrooms ran at an average fill rate of 62% across the year — the share of available seats actually occupied — against the 75% the sector treated as a practical ceiling. The two most-requested faculty were at 95% of teaching capacity, booked nine months ahead. A seat unsold in a cohort could not be sold afterwards. What the unit returned to the university was contribution — revenue less the direct cost of running programmes, before allocated central overhead — and it funded faculty research across the school.

## The Situation

At 31%, EAGP had produced S/ 7,440,000 of contribution in the prior year. The floor required S/ 8,400,000 on the same revenue (see Exhibit 1).

Three items carried most of the gap. The Programa de Alta Dirección (PAD), an eleven-week general-management programme, enrolled forty participants at S/ 18,000 each: S/ 720,000 of revenue against S/ 720,000 of fully loaded cost, a contribution of about zero. Its alumni network appeared in the school's brochures; it held the two scarcest teachers for most of a term. A mining group buying 18% of the custom line — about S/ 1,940,000 a year — had reopened its master agreement and asked for a 12% cut in rates, putting roughly S/ 470,000 of contribution in play. And the floor above the classrooms had come free: two rooms, a third more capacity than EAGP held, offered for 120 days at S/ 1,400,000 a year on a lease or S/ 5,200,000 outright with the fit-out.

Cavero would be judged on four numbers: blended contribution margin against the 35% floor; the fill rate, at 62%; annual contribution in soles, what that margin produced on the revenue the unit could sustain; and a strategic-health index on a 0–100 scale combining brand standing, the alumni pipeline into the custom book, faculty capacity and participant satisfaction, which stood at 70 with the learner satisfaction score inside it at 72.

If nothing moved — prices as printed, the PAD as it stood, the mining rate settled on instinct, the floor above let go — the arithmetic gave a second year near 31%, about S/ 1,000,000 short, and then the merger the rector had described.

## What Cavero Controlled

With the programme accounts in front of her, Cavero had to say how to read them: work from the blended 31% and act on the average; treat every programme returning under 35% as loss-making and mark it for closure; or rank the fourteen on contribution and fill together, then decide programme by programme. She owed a one-line verdict on the PAD: close it; leave it in the catalogue as it stood; or keep it and book the subsidy as a deliberate charge on the higher-margin programmes. She had to settle what the allocated central overhead on each account meant: a real cost each programme should be judged after, a shared cost that stayed behind when a programme closed, or a distraction from a ranking on revenue. And she had to fix how much of the first month went into building that ranking at all, from none to all of it.

Pricing came next. A 10% rise in list price had historically cost EAGP between 6% and 8% of enrollment, and the ratio differed by programme. She could raise all fourteen list prices 12%; cut prices and discount to fill the rooms; or move each price separately by what she judged it could carry. Discounting was a separate instrument: let list price be an opening position everyone negotiated from, hold it as printed with no exceptions, or discount by a written rule reserved for sponsored learners. Per-cohort variable spend — catering, materials, adjunct fees — could be cut about 20%, left untouched, or reduced only where she judged it invisible to participants. On the programmes she believed carried a premium she had to name a figure, from a 10% cut to a 20% rise.

Then capacity and the client. She could book 40% more custom work next quarter; pull back to open enrollment and let the custom book shrink; or grow custom only as far as the roster could teach it. The increment would go to the two faculty clients asked for by name; to adjuncts from the sixty already on the roster; or to a new bench recruited and vetted before it taught. To the mining group she could concede the 12%, refuse it and let the agreement lapse, or counter with a smaller cut against a multi-year term and a volume commitment. Building and testing the bench would cost S/ 0, S/ 150,000 or S/ 350,000.

Last, the floor above: buy it for S/ 5,200,000, lease it at S/ 1,400,000 a year, or let the option lapse and stay in six rooms. Whichever she chose, the paper had to rest on a stated first-year fill rate for the new space — 75%, 50% or 40%. She could derive the rate at which the rooms covered their own annual cost before deciding, note it alongside the other figures, or leave it out. And she had to record, from 0 to 100, her confidence that the rooms would reach the rate she named.

## Closing

On the evening of 2 March, Cavero laid the fourteen programme accounts across the meeting-room table in the controller's printed order and turned the top one face down before reading it. Upstairs the floor was empty. The lease ran out in 120 days. The rector's number was 35.
