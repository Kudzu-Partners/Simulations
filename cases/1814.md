# Voltaris Systèmes: Earning Authority

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1814 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1814 — *Earning Authority* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday morning, 15 June 2026, Camille Roche led her first stand-up as the new head of the Helios engineering squad at Voltaris Systèmes SAS, four days after a promotion that had, until the previous Friday, made her a peer to everyone in the room. Within the first hour the squad's most outspoken engineer challenged her in front of the other ten: her call on a live architecture question was worth no more than anyone else's, he said, and he wanted to know why the room should treat it otherwise.

She had until Friday, 19 June — five working days — to close that question ahead of a steering-committee review, with her own skip-level director in the room. Helios generated 40% of Voltaris's revenue, and a slipped design freeze put an estimated €3.2 million of revenue at risk.

## The Protagonist

Camille had spent her career on Helios as an individual contributor, becoming, by common agreement, its strongest engineer — the reputation that earned her the promotion when the previous lead moved into a director role. She now led the same eleven people she had been sitting beside the week before, including the colleague also considered for the role, who had not gotten it. Voltaris's leadership knew this kind of promotion did not usually go well: four of the last six engineers promoted to lead a squad from within had, within a year, stepped back into an individual-contributor role or left the firm. Camille knew that pattern, and knew the room did too.

## The Decision

Camille's week reduced to four connected choices, each made in front of the same eleven engineers: how to answer a public challenge to her legitimacy; what kind of leader to be for the rest of the week; how to bring back a colleague who had been passed over for her job and now held back legacy-code knowledge the Friday deadline depended on; and how to close the architecture question itself, and whether to say aloud how she meant to make the next hundred decisions like it.

## The Company and its Setting

Voltaris Systèmes SAS designed and manufactured energy-management hardware and software for industrial buildings, headquartered in Lyon, France, with 520 employees and €110 million in 2025 revenue at an 11% operating margin (Exhibit 1). Its engineering culture ran on one belief: authority was earned through demonstrated expertise and the trust of the people led, not granted by a title. Teams argued openly and expected a lead to win on the merits, not by outranking anyone. Inside the firm, the four-of-six failure rate among internally promoted squad leads was an open, uncomfortable fact.

## The Situation

Helios had spent the prior week debating a contested architecture question that now had to be settled before Friday's steering-committee review. In the stand-up, the skeptic put his challenge to her directly, in front of the room: why was her judgment on that question worth more than anyone else's, today, when nothing but a promotion letter had changed since the previous Friday? Camille had roughly a minute to answer, watched by ten engineers deciding how much of the room to extend her. Separately, the veteran passed over for the lead role — who held knowledge of the squad's legacy codebase that no one else had — had been visibly doing less since the promotion was announced. On Friday, the director would be in the room; he had already told her, supportively, that he expected her to "establish herself this week."

Four separate measures mattered from day one (Exhibit 2): how much authority the squad actually granted her, how openly reasoning and dissent got aired, how many of the eleven had genuinely signed on to follow her, and how honestly she reflected on her own instinct to seize control rather than earn it. She started the week close to zero on all four, alongside one further reserve — a credibility account, full for now, that only ever spent down.

If none of the week's choices resolved in her favor, the arithmetic was plain: the freeze would slip or be forced through without real support, the internal-promotion failure count would extend to a fifth case in six, and Helios would keep losing the momentum past transitions like this one had already cost it.

## What Camille Controlled

Each choice had its own menu, and Camille controlled all of it directly. Before the stand-up she had to settle where her authority would actually come from that week: the promotion itself, already hers the moment it took effect; being, by common agreement, the strongest engineer in the room; the expertise and consent the squad chose to extend her; or the backing her director had made clear she could count on. She also had to decide what she most needed to learn before speaking: only the technical facts of the architecture question, leaving people matters to settle themselves; what each of the eleven — including the skeptic and veteran — needed in order to follow her; a first-week plan detailed enough to look decisive from hour one; or a private read on who would back her and who would not. On a separate, sliding scale, she also had to decide how openly to admit what she did not yet know about this particular team.

When the skeptic's challenge came, she had four ways to answer it: point to the title itself, on the logic that the job was now hers and so was the call; hand the question back to the group and let the team decide; change the subject and move the meeting on without answering directly; or lay out her reasoning and invite the room to test it. She also had to choose a stance for the week — set the direction firmly and expect the squad to follow; question and learn first, then direct and decide, signalling when she switched; or keep every decision open so no one felt overruled — and, on a sliding scale, how much dissent to actively invite rather than let go quiet.

For the veteran, Camille could frame the withdrawal as underperformance, to be logged like any decline in output; as separate from her job as manager, with the legacy-code knowledge sourced from someone else instead; or as a decision still being made, hers to follow or not. She then had to choose how to open the conversation: reassure her in general terms that she was still valued, without naming what had happened; hint that cooperation now would put her first in line for whatever came next; or name the disappointment directly and offer a real piece of work built on the veteran's legacy knowledge — and, separately, how concrete a role to put behind whichever opening she chose.

Finally, ahead of Friday's steering committee, Camille had to decide how to run the architecture decision itself: keep the discussion open for another round rather than close it; put the skeptic's case and the veteran's knowledge forward, then decide and stand behind it; or settle on her own preferred answer and shut the debate down. When her director pressed her on whether this was real authority or a performance, she could meet him head-on with total certainty and concede not one doubt; quietly shift her position toward whichever answer she believed he privately preferred; or tell him what she had learned, what she had decided, and what she did not know. And she had to decide whether to declare that every decision affecting the squad would run through her alone from here on; put a leadership contract in writing, naming when she would open a question and when she would decide; or say nothing further and let the freeze decision alone stand as her message. On a further scale of her own, she had to decide how firmly to hold that durable authority mattered more than one good day's performance of confidence.

## Closing

At a few minutes past nine that Monday, Camille closed her notebook, looked at the ten faces still watching her after the skeptic's question, and turned to the whiteboard to start the stand-up properly. Friday was five working days away. She had a title, a room that had not yet decided whether to grant her anything more, and four choices still to make.
