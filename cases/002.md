## Abstract

BancaVerde, a forty-year-old universal bank headquartered in the Republic of Arvelia, carried in early 2026 a tech-debt burden of AV$800 million, a digital Net Promoter Score of 28, a cost-to-income ratio of 68%, and a 17% share of the retail banking market. A five-year-old neobank, NeoBanca Verde, had grown to a digital NPS of 71 and was adding roughly 40,000 clients per month on a zero-fee account. An impending regulatory decree — *Decreto Fintech 2024* — would activate Open Banking within 24 months and levy a AV$45 million fine on any incumbent whose API readiness stood below 50% at activation. The CEO, Andrés Villalobos, had to commit the first of three two-year rounds of strategic investment: how to divide a AV$280 million technology envelope across six competing programmes, a partner choice (FinPago, AgriCredit, TechBank, or none), a single product launch (Digital Account, Buy-Now-Pay-Later, SME Suite, Remittances, or none at all), and how far to push branch closures and engineering hiring. The case ends with Villalobos the evening before the board commits Round 1.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate incumbent-versus-disruptor dynamics when a regulatory clock forces platform rather than product investment.
2. Analyze the trade-off between short-cycle product launches and medium-cycle infrastructure build-out in a resource-constrained incumbent.
3. Design a partner strategy that uses a third party's distribution, technology, or segment access to compensate for internal gaps.
4. Diagnose when an acquisition premium is about to appear, and what earlier decisions create it.
5. Recommend a Round 1 strategy and articulate the signalling effect of that strategy on Rounds 2 and 3.

On the night of **March 3, 2026**, **Andrés Villalobos** sat in his office on the twenty-second floor of the BancaVerde tower on Avenida Central, Ciudad Arvelia, with the lights turned off, the printed board pack on his lap, and the quarterly NPS chart projected on the far wall. The chart showed a single intersection in Q2 2024: the line where NeoBanca Verde's digital NPS had crossed his own and kept climbing. The board would meet at 8:00 a.m. the next morning to ratify the Round 1 strategy. Villalobos had drafted three versions. He had not yet chosen one.

---

## The Protagonist

Andrés Villalobos, 52, had joined BancaVerde in 2004 as a corporate-banking relationship manager and had risen through commercial, SME, and finally the digital function before being named CEO in 2023. His predecessor, a third-generation member of the founding Villalobos-Ortiz family, had stepped down after a shareholder revolt over five flat years of revenue. The board had given Villalobos a five-year mandate: "Defend the franchise, or hand it to someone who can." His compensation vested against three metrics: ROE (weight 40%), digital market share (weight 40%), and a regulatory-readiness index the board had defined specifically for this strategy (weight 20%). He held no family equity.

---

## The Decision

Villalobos had to present, by 8:00 a.m. on March 4, a single Round 1 strategy covering the period 2026–2027. The board had approved a technology envelope of **AV$280 million per two-year round**, held equal across the three rounds of the five-year plan; there was no mechanism to pull Round 2 or Round 3 money forward. Four levers were on the table, each with distinct costs and second-order effects:

1. **Technology allocation.** Divide the AV$280M across six programmes, the shares totalling exactly 100%, each capped by delivery capacity: **Core Banking modernization** (up to 60%), **Mobile & UX** (60%), **Data & AI** (60%), **Cybersecurity** (30%), the **Open Banking API layer** (50%), and **Branch Technology** (30%). Reinoso's engineering estimates put the effects at roughly: core banking, 0.4 points of API readiness and AV$4.2 million of tech-debt retirement per percentage point; the API layer, a full point of readiness and AV$0.8 million of annual ecosystem revenue per point; mobile and UX, 0.35 points of digital penetration and a quarter-point of NPS per point; data and AI, the strongest single lever on cost-to-income; cybersecurity, the only lever that built brand trust; branch technology, a slow contributor to both cost and digital adoption.
2. **Partner:** FinPago (payments), AgriCredit (rural SME credit), TechBank (open-API core platform), or none.
3. **Product launch:** exactly one of Digital Account (AV$12M), Buy-Now-Pay-Later (AV$25M), SME Suite (AV$45M), Remittances (AV$8M) — or none, holding the whole envelope for infrastructure. The bank's delivery capacity would not absorb two launches in one round, a constraint Reinoso had defended in writing after Proyecto Bravo.
4. **Workforce:** how many of the 412 branches to close (up to 50, each saving AV$1.8 million a year, with reputational cost above twenty) and how many engineering teams to hire (up to five, at 50 people and AV$8 million a year each).

The board would vote the package up or down. The Banco Central would activate Open Banking in Q4 2027, at the end of Round 2.

## Company Background

BancaVerde was founded in 1985 by two co-founders — Alejandro Villalobos and Camila Ortiz — as a regional commercial bank serving coffee and sugar exporters in the Arvelia highlands. Over four decades the bank built a universal franchise across retail, SME, corporate, and wealth management, with 412 branches, 2,900 ATMs, and 11,400 employees across Arvelia and two neighbouring countries. As of year-end 2025 the bank held AV$28 billion in assets and AV$3.1 billion in equity. ROE for 2025 was 11.0%, below the regional peer median of 14.8%; the cost-to-income ratio stood at 68% against a peer median near 45%, net interest margin at 4.2% and falling, digital penetration of the customer base at 34%, and an internal brand-trust index at 50 on a 100-point scale.

The bank's technology stack had grown organically around a 1998 core banking system written in COBOL and Java, wrapped over two decades with a heterogeneous mix of middleware, point-of-sale integrations, and a 2015 mobile banking app that had been shelved as-is for seven years. Two internal platform attempts — "Proyecto Alfa" (2017–2019) and "Proyecto Bravo" (2020–2022) — had been cancelled before delivery, burning a combined AV$120 million. The tech-debt figure of AV$800 million was an internal IT estimate of the cost to bring the stack to parity with a 2023-grade cloud-native core.

Ownership remained concentrated: the Villalobos-Ortiz families together held 38% through a holding company; domestic pension funds held 29%; a Canadian strategic investor held 12%; float and retail held the remainder. The board had nine seats, two of them family-appointed.

## Industry and Market Context

The Arvelian retail-banking market in 2025 was dominated by four incumbents with a combined 71% market share, two mid-sized regional banks, and a growing fringe of digital challengers. NeoBanca Verde, founded in 2020, had grown from 0 to approximately 1.4 million clients by year-end 2025, almost entirely on a zero-fee digital account and a remittances corridor with the United States. Its reported unit economics in its 2024 Series C deck — leaked and widely circulated — showed a blended CAC of AV$42 and an LTV of AV$310. Incumbent blended CAC was roughly AV$180 per digital customer; LTV was higher but degrading as branch-based product attachment fell.

The *Decreto Fintech 2024*, drafted by the Banco Central in late 2024 and ratified in mid-2025, required regulated banks to expose a standard set of APIs — account data, payment initiation, and customer consent — by the last quarter of 2027. The decree set a single bright line rather than a sliding scale: an institution certified at **50% API readiness or above** at activation was compliant, and one below it drew a **AV$45 million** penalty. Arvelian supervisory practice added the part that did not appear in the decree: a sanctioned bank carried the finding in its public supervisory record until remediated, and peers that had been sanctioned under earlier resolutions had seen customer-trust indices fall by twenty points or more in the following year. Implementation of the APIs was estimated at AV$180–240 million for a bank of BancaVerde's size, depending on sourcing choices.

A parallel trend was compressing incumbent margins: payment interchange revenue had fallen 14% in two years under NeoBanca Verde's pricing; branch-originated current-account acquisitions had fallen from 62% of new customers in 2020 to 31% in 2025.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Villalobos's first eighteen months as CEO had been spent stabilizing the commercial side and hiring a new CTO, Paula Reinoso, from a Brazilian neobank. Reinoso's first internal memo had laid out the tech-debt figure — AV$800 million — and the stark implication that the bank could not *both* launch several products and retire the debt at an acceptable pace within a five-year window. A choice was required. What the memo did not say, and what nobody on the board had asked, was how the AV$800 million had been derived, or that at the 60% cap on core-banking spend the bank could retire at most AV$252 million in any one round and AV$756 million across the whole five-year plan — meaning full remediation was not on the menu under any allocation Villalobos could write.

### What Villalobos knew

Internal modelling suggested three regimes.

- **Products-first:** launch the strongest available product and weight the envelope toward mobile and branch technology; digital-penetration and NPS trajectory best in years 1–2, compliance risk highest at activation, and a fine probable.
- **Platform-first:** no product launch; weight core banking and the API layer to the caps, close branches and hire engineering aggressively; digital penetration and NPS stall in years 1–2 but structural cost declines and readiness is comfortable well before activation.
- **Partner-leveraged:** one targeted product (typically SME Suite or Remittances) launched on an external partner's rail, with the envelope split so that the API layer clears the regulatory line without starving the commercial side. Compliance risk removed, market share improved, optionality preserved.

### What Villalobos did not know

How aggressively NeoBanca Verde would price in 2026–2027; whether the Canadian strategic would support a two-year revenue dip; whether the Banco Central would grant compliance extensions for credible-but-incomplete plans; and — the question that had not left his head since a dinner in October — whether a real acquirer would appear in 2028 for a platform-ready incumbent.

### What others were saying

**Paula Reinoso**, CTO, argued for *platform-first*. "A bank with 200 APIs and no products is worth more than a bank with three products and no APIs. We are not selling features. We are selling integration. Put forty percent on core and forty on the API layer, close ten branches, hire two teams, and launch nothing this round."

**Ricardo Mendoza**, Head of Retail, argued for *products-first*. "If we lose another year of primary-account acquisition we do not come back. Launch the Digital Account and put the money where customers can see it — mobile, UX, the branches they still walk into. We fix the pipes in 2028."

**Camila Ortiz** (family representative, Vice Chair) argued for *partner-leveraged*. "Partner with AgriCredit. Launch the SME Suite on their rail. Fund the API layer to whatever the decree requires and not a point more, and spend the rest where it earns. We do not bet the firm on a thesis — we buy optionality."

**Sebastián Rojas** (corporate-development head) had, late in February, passed Villalobos a single-sheet analysis titled "NBV Acquisition Scenario." It modelled the combination in both directions: a NeoBanca Verde offer to acquire BancaVerde at 2.4x revenue in 2028 *if* BancaVerde demonstrated >75% API readiness by that date, and — should the neobank's funding market turn — a BancaVerde acquisition of, or alliance with, NeoBanca Verde as early as Round 2. Villalobos had not yet shared the sheet with the board.

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — Products-First (the Mendoza plan):** Launch the Digital Account. No partner. Allocation 10% core banking, 40% mobile and UX, 10% data and AI, 10% cybersecurity, 10% API layer, 20% branch technology; no branch closures, one engineering team. Best commercial trajectory of the three: digital penetration rises to 57% and NPS to 39 by the end of Round 1, market share to 18.7%. But the allocation yields only 14 points of API readiness in the round, and on the same posture in Round 2 the bank reaches activation at roughly 28% — well inside the penalty. AV$42M of tech debt retired.

- **Option B — Platform-First (the Reinoso plan):** No product launch. Partner with TechBank for core-platform modernization. Allocation 40% core banking, 40% API layer, 10% data and AI, 10% cybersecurity, nothing to mobile or branches; ten branches closed, two engineering teams hired. Readiness reaches 56% in Round 1 alone — compliance settled before the decree is anywhere near activation — and AV$168M of tech debt is retired. The commercial cost is severe: digital penetration stalls at the 34% floor, NPS falls to 30, market share slips to 17.4%, and the TechBank privacy exposure costs eight points of brand trust.

- **Option C — Partner-Leveraged (the Ortiz plan):** Launch the SME Suite in partnership with AgriCredit. Allocation 25% core banking, 15% mobile and UX, 10% data and AI, 10% cybersecurity, 30% API layer, 10% branch technology; five branches closed, one engineering team. Readiness reaches 40% in Round 1 and clears the 50% line comfortably during Round 2; AV$105M of tech debt retired. Market share rises to 19.1% on the rural SME expansion, ROE to 11.7%, and brand trust holds — AgriCredit is the only partner that costs nothing in trust. Rounds 2 and 3 remain flexible.

A fourth, not on the Board agenda — a targeted acquisition of a regional fintech rather than a greenfield launch — had been discussed informally with Rojas but was not yet packaged. Villalobos had 12 hours to decide whether to raise it.

---

Villalobos stood up, crossed the office, and re-opened the NPS chart on the wall. The board would convene at 8:00 a.m. He had to choose among Option A, Option B, and Option C — or raise a fourth he had not yet written. In 24 months the Banco Central would flip the switch on Open Banking. Some bank in Arvelia would own the rails. He did not yet know whether it would be his.
