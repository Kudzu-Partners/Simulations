# Las Acacias: Six Projects, One Block

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1204 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1204 — *Public Goods & Free Riding: The Las Acacias Court* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On the evening of Tuesday, April 14, 2026, Lucía Serrano Vidal unlocked the ground-floor storage room of the Las Acacias apartment block, set out eleven folding chairs and taped a printout to the wall. It showed a figure of €340. That was everything the residents' group chat had pledged, in eleven weeks of messages, towards a €1,200 basketball half-court on the empty lot between the two buildings. The town hall's offer of the lot for community use expired in forty-five days.

Serrano chaired the residents' council. Before the meeting ended, the council had to choose the rule by which the half-court would be paid for, and how hard to campaign for it. Five more projects were queued behind it for the rest of the council's term.

## The Protagonist

Serrano, forty-nine, was a pharmacist who had lived on the third floor of Block A since the building opened in 1998. The residents had elected her chair in January, mostly because she answered messages and had no obvious side in the block's older quarrels. She had never run a vote on money. She had watched the group chat for eleven weeks and noticed that almost every message ended the same way: great idea, let's see what the others put in.

## The Decision

For each of six projects, the council would choose one funding rule and one level of communication effort, then run the project and live with the result (see Exhibit 1). The rules on the table were a voluntary collection, a threshold pledge, a majority vote with an equal levy, and a members-only arrangement behind a lock (see Exhibit 2). The same rule did not have to serve every project.

## The Company and its Setting

Las Acacias was a residential block of 38 households in two buildings in a mid-sized town. Between the buildings sat a fenced, dusty municipal lot of 18 by 22 metres, used now and then as overflow parking. The town hall had offered it free for community use but would not pay to fit it out. If nothing was built within the forty-five days, the lot would revert to parking.

A door-to-door survey run by a group of teenagers in the block had found that 34 of the 38 households said a court would improve their quality of life, and 28 put its value to them at between €30 and €100 a year. Added up, residents declared €2,400 a year of benefit from a court that cost €1,200 once. Anyone living in the block would be able to use it, whether or not they had paid.

## The Situation

The pledges told a different story from the survey. Of the €340 promised, €220 came from five high-use households. Two of the loudest supporters in the chat had pledged nothing. Nobody had yet handed over any money. The chat had turned sour: the generous felt taken for granted, the others felt pressured, and Tomás Beltrán, seventy-one, had written that if the council could not build a court it would never fix the lift in Block B, which had failed twice that year.

The council started its term with nothing funded and nothing collected. Serrano read the mood of the block, on a scale the council used informally from 0 (broken) to 100 (cohesive), at about 50: neither hostile nor united. If the council did nothing different, the chat would go on passing the hat, the offer on the lot would lapse, and the next five projects would start from a block that had already watched one collective effort fail.

The queue was set (see Exhibit 1). After the half-court came a pocket park with benches, two trees and a sandbox, favoured by families with small children and older residents; a public Wi-Fi mesh for the courtyard, which carried a two-year connectivity contract rather than a one-off cost; the €5,000 overhaul of the Block B lift; six security cameras at the entrances and the bike room, which some residents wanted and others saw as a privacy problem; and strings of Christmas lights for the courtyard in December. For each, the council knew the one-off cost, the benefit residents had declared, what the median household said the project was worth to it each year, and what an equal share of the cost would be per household.

## What Serrano Controlled

For each project Serrano and the council settled two things.

The first was the **funding rule**. Under a **voluntary contribution**, households dropped whatever they chose into a sealed box; nobody saw who paid what, and the project went ahead only if the box covered the cost. Under a **threshold pledge**, pledges were public and money was collected only if the total reached the cost; if it fell short, everyone was refunded and nothing was built. Under a **majority vote with an equal levy**, the block voted, and if a simple majority of the 38 households said yes, every household paid the same share, including those who had voted no. Under a **club good**, a group of about a dozen high-use households would fund the project themselves and lock it behind a key, and the households that did not pay would not get in, even though the town hall had offered the lot to all 38.

The second was the **communication effort**, on a scale from 1 to 5: from a single notice on the lobby board at 1, through letters under every door, up to a full campaign of door-knocking, flyers and a residents' meeting at 5. The council's usual effort sat at 3.

After each project the council would see whether it had been **funded**, how much had been **collected**, how many households had **paid** and how many had paid nothing while still able to use the result, the **free-riders**. Across the term it would track four things: the number of projects funded out of those attempted, which the council called **provision**; the **community trust** reading, from 0 to 100; the **cumulative welfare**, the declared annual benefit of everything actually delivered, counting only the households who could use it; and an **equity score** out of 100, where 100 meant that everyone had paid an equal share. At the end of the term, those four numbers would be the council's record.

## Closing

At 8:05 PM, nine residents had taken chairs and two were standing by the door. Iker Molina, sixteen, one of the teenagers who had costed the court, held up his phone with the supplier's quote. Beltrán sat in the front row with his arms folded. Serrano looked at the €340 on the wall and asked the room how the court was going to be paid for.
