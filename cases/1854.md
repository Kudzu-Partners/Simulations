# Lumière Materials NV: Pricing the Transition

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1854 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1854 — *Paying for the Planet* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

Anneke Verbruggen read the vote count twice before she believed it. Lumière Materials NV's remuneration report had cleared its shareholder vote with sixty-eight percent support — technically a pass, and by the standards European boards had come to expect, close to a public rebuke. As chair of the remuneration committee, she now had to redesign the chief executive's bonus so that a credible link existed between his pay and the company's climate targets, and she had to bring a finished proposal to the full board within this cycle, months before the next annual meeting.

## The Protagonist

Verbruggen, fifty-four, had joined Lumière's board as an independent non-executive director three years earlier, after a decade as chief financial officer of an industrial group listed on the same exchange. She had chaired the remuneration committee since her second year, a role that had, until this vote, drawn little attention outside the annual filing. Her own re-election to the board was scheduled for the meeting after next; committee chairs whose pay policies failed twice did not always survive that vote.

## The Decision

The committee had to decide, in stages, how to rebuild roughly €2.94 million of the chief executive's variable pay so that a meaningful share of it depended on verifiable progress toward the company's stated climate targets — without making the package so demanding that the executive credited with the company's recent turnaround chose to leave. Two of the company's three largest institutional shareholders, together holding twenty-two percent of the shares, and both major proxy-advisory firms had said plainly that they would vote against the entire board at the next meeting unless that link became real.

## The Company and its Setting

Founded in 1961, Lumière Materials NV made building materials and insulation across nine countries, generating €4.8 billion in revenue and employing 14,000 people, at a sixteen percent EBITDA margin. It was also, to the large investors who held its stock, a decarbonization story: its low-carbon insulation lines were meant to be the growth engine behind a 2030 emissions target, and that target was the reason those investors owned the shares in the first place (Exhibit 1). The chief executive, Henrik Dumont, had led the company since 2019 and was widely credited with the financial recovery of recent years.

## The Situation

Dumont's total target pay stood at €4.2 million, seventy percent of it variable. Two years earlier the committee had attached a five-percent ESG modifier to that variable pay — a gesture proxy advisors had since described as immaterial. Financial performance drove almost all the rest of the package, and some directors doubted how tightly that pay tracked results. If the committee simply rewrote the same policy in clearer language, the same investors and advisors had already indicated how they would vote at the next meeting: against the board, not just the report. Underneath the immediate vote sat a slower-moving constraint: European rules requiring remuneration policy to be disclosed and sustainability metrics to be independently auditable meant that whatever the committee chose now would be checked against verified data later, not just read once and filed away.

## What Verbruggen Controlled

The first task was a board memo naming why the vote had failed. Four explanations were circulating among the directors. One held that the underlying policy was sound and only the report's explanation to shareholders had been poorly written. A second held that two of the largest investors were picking a fight the board could simply outlast without changing anything. A third held that the chief executive was simply paid too much in total, and that trimming that figure would satisfy the critics. A fourth held that a five-percent ESG modifier was immaterial and that financial pay itself barely tracked results. Tied to that diagnosis was a second question: what would make ESG-linked pay credible. Candidates included an increase in the existing modifier from five to ten percent; a material weight placed on externally verifiable, independently audited climate metrics inside the long-term incentive; no substantive change, only a clearer explanation of the policy already in place; or a large weight placed on self-assessed measures of sustainability culture and stakeholder engagement. Beneath both questions sat a number from zero to a hundred: how bluntly the memo would name the pay-performance mismatch rather than protect the chief executive.

Architecting the €2.94 million meant choosing among four splits between financial and ESG pay (Exhibit 2): financial ninety percent and ESG ten, keeping nearly all of the incentive tied to results already understood; financial eighty and ESG twenty, a split several comparable European issuers had already adopted; financial seventy and ESG thirty, putting close to a third of the variable pool behind the transition targets; or financial sixty and ESG forty, shifting a larger share of the package toward sustainability performance than any peer she could name. Two further choices governed that architecture: what share of the ESG weight, from zero to a hundred percent, would sit in the long-term incentive rather than the annual bonus; and whether ESG would act as a multiplier applied to whatever the financial bonus had already earned, or instead stand as its own weighted scorecard, reported and checked independently.

Two more menus followed. The first offered six candidate indicators in two groups of three: a primary measure — an absolute reduction in direct and purchased-energy emissions, measured each year against a fixed baseline; an internally assessed score describing the company's sustainability culture, compiled by its own staff; or carbon intensity per tonne of product, a ratio expressed relative to production volume — paired with a secondary measure: the share of company revenue earned from its low-carbon product lines, tracked over time; a milestone validated by an outside science-based-target body against the 2030 goal; or a self-reported index measuring how engaged stakeholders felt with the company's sustainability efforts. The second menu set the ceiling on total payout — a hundred fifty, a hundred seventy-five, or two hundred percent of target, or no ceiling at all — and asked two further yes-or-no questions: whether the ESG portion would pay out only if a minimum financial result was also achieved, and whether the company could claw back part of any award if results were later restated.

A late complication reopened all of it. Dumont's own advisor said that, under the design taking shape, realistic payout had fallen by roughly a third, and mentioned a rival's recent outreach. Three responses were available: reduce the ESG weight and raise the cap to restore the earlier payout; adjust the stretch on the existing metrics so the target stayed reachable while keeping the same cap and indicators; or leave the design exactly as approved and let the executive decide for himself. At the same time, one of the two large investors still called the ESG weighting too soft, and the committee had to decide what to hold and what to give: raise the ESG weighting as far as that investor wanted, whatever metrics that implied; drop the financial gate so the ESG portion paid out regardless of the year's financial results; or keep the verifiable targets and the financial gate as fixed points and offer nothing further. A final choice was how much of the finished design to disclose before the meeting — a minimal filing, published only at the annual meeting itself with no advance briefing to the bloc; a full account of targets, audit source, cap, gate and clawback shared with the investor bloc in advance; or a narrative emphasizing ambition with less detail on how the targets would be checked — alongside a number, from zero to a hundred, for how firmly she meant to defend whatever the committee chose.

Whatever the committee proposed would be read on four counts: whether the say-on-pay vote climbed from sixty-eight percent toward the eighty percent that would end the crisis; whether independent reviewers judged the new metrics real rather than decorative; whether Dumont still found the package worth staying for; and how many of the €2.94 million's euros genuinely rode on the climate targets rather than on results already rewarded elsewhere.

## Closing

Verbruggen opened a blank page for the board memo. Four hypotheses about the failed vote sat in front of her, €2.94 million of unassigned variable pay behind them, and a scorecard that no one outside the room had yet seen.
