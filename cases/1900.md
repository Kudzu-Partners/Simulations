# Alpina Executive Learning: The Méridien Brief

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1900 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1900 — *The Custom Brief* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 8 June 2026, Salomé Reyer read the Téléphonie Méridien request for proposal twice at her desk in Grenoble, then turned to the costing her finance and operations lead had stapled behind it. The request asked for a leadership-development programme for 120 mid-level managers and capped the fee at €480,000. The costing, built overnight off the module outline Alpina had sketched in April, came to €430,000. Alpina's school required a margin of at least 20% on custom work, which put the ceiling on deliverable cost at €384,000. The first costing sat €46,000 the wrong side of it.

Reyer had fourteen days. Méridien's selection panel would take proposals on 22 June, and Alpina was not the only unit writing against the brief; a boutique consultancy was bidding as well. What she put in front of the panel had to be a programme the client would buy, a programme the school would sign, and a programme her own team could deliver — and the brief in front of her described three different programmes.

## The Protagonist

Salomé Reyer, 43, had been Programme Director at Alpina Executive Learning for five years, after eight years selling and running custom programmes for a corporate training firm in Lyon. She had built her standing inside the school on refusing to quote work she could not later staff, and on a habit her colleagues found tiresome: she costed a programme before she designed it. Alpina's programme managers owned their client relationships end to end, which meant that a contract Reyer scoped badly stayed hers through delivery and through the renewal conversation that followed.

## The Decision

Reyer had to decide what Alpina would put in front of Méridien's panel on 22 June, and to make the sequence of choices that produced it: what the programme was for, what got built and what got reused, how the €46,000 came out, and what she would say when the panel pushed.

## The Company and its Setting

Alpina Executive Learning was the custom executive-education unit of a mid-sized French business school, run from the school's Grenoble campus as its own profit-and-loss account. It booked €9.2 million of annual revenue at a 22% contribution margin and ran roughly 34 programmes a year, drawing on core faculty employed by the school and a bench of affiliated faculty engaged by the day. About two-thirds of its revenue was repeat business, so the cost of scoping a programme badly was not confined to the contract it was scoped for.

Téléphonie Méridien was a French telecommunications operator with 14,000 employees. The contract on the table was Alpina's largest single opportunity of the year, and Méridien had told the market that the winner would be considered for a multi-year framework agreement.

## The Situation

Three parties inside Méridien had told Alpina what the programme was for, and they had not told it the same thing (see **Exhibit 1**). Anne-Laure Ferrand, the human-resources sponsor who had written the brief, wanted transformational leadership and culture change. Bruno Delcourt, the chief operating officer who controlled the €480,000, wanted measurable operational impact within six months. The 120 managers themselves had been surveyed, and had said they were short of time and wanted material that was practical rather than theoretical. Nothing in the fee cap allowed all three to be delivered at full ambition.

The design itself barely existed. Alpina scored every bid on the same sheet before it left the building, and Reyer's sheet on 8 June was close to empty. Client-fit — whether Delcourt and Ferrand could each see their own outcome in the design — stood at 25 out of 100. Learner-relevance — what 120 time-poor managers would make of the journey — stood at 25 out of 100. Gross margin stood at 10%, against the school's floor of 20%. Of the €90,000 design-and-development sub-budget, a fixed envelope with no top-up, €0 had been committed.

The programme had been outlined as five modules: strategy, leading change, operational execution, coaching with 360-degree feedback, and a capstone. Each could be written from scratch for Méridien, or drawn from Alpina's existing open-enrolment catalogue. Bespoke content read as built for the client and cost more to produce; catalogue content was already written and already taught, and read as what it was. The school allowed open-enrolment material to carry up to 40% of contact hours in a programme sold as custom; beyond that it would not let Alpina call the programme custom at all.

Méridien had also declined to guarantee the cohort. It committed to 90 of the 120 seats, and left the remaining 30 to internal uptake. Design was a fixed cost whichever way the seats went.

If Reyer sent the proposal as it stood, the arithmetic was not in dispute: at €430,000 of cost against €480,000 of fee the margin was 10%, the school would not counter-sign it, and the panel would be reading a module outline rather than a design.

## What Reyer Controlled

Her worksheet held four blocks, and each closed before the next made sense.

The first was the decode. She had to name one primary objective the programme would be funded against, from four readings of the same brief: measurable operational leadership inside six months, the outcome Delcourt had named; a transformational leadership and culture-change journey, the outcome Ferrand had written into the brief; a light, practical toolkit of the kind the surveyed managers had asked for; or all three carried at full scope, transformation and six-month impact and practicality together. She had to fix how the survey entered the work: as the evidence the journey was designed backwards from; as a check run across a design already drawn; or as a passage quoted in the proposal, with the modules drawn from Alpina's own reading of the need. And she had to decide how much of her one-page decode went to the €46,000 gap, from leaving it unnamed to putting it at the centre of the page.

The second was the envelope. The €90,000 had to be split across the five modules, in any proportion, totalling exactly €90,000. Alongside it sat the build choice: bespoke content on operational execution and the capstone with catalogue material on the other three; bespoke content written for Méridien on all five modules; or catalogue material drawn from the open programme on all five.

The third was the format and the bridge. She had to set the share of contact hours running on open-enrolment modules, anywhere from none to a majority, against the 40% limit. She had to pick the first lever that took cost out, from the four Alpina had costed (see **Exhibit 2**), and decide whether to combine a second with it.

The fourth was the room. She had to price the per-learner economics on 90 seats, on 105, or on 120. She had to settle her answer to the challenge she expected — that the programme was either too generic or too expensive — by walking the panel through the survey-led design and the cost bridge, by matching the price the boutique consultancy was expected to quote, or by adding bespoke build to the scope there in the room. She had to say what Alpina would sign as the measure of six-month impact: named operational measures plus movement in the managers' 360 scores, baselined at kickoff and read at month six; satisfaction scores collected at the end of the programme and a completion certificate for each manager; or a description of the change in cultural terms, which Alpina had used before on programmes of this kind. And she had to decide how firmly to hold the 20% floor and the scope behind it once Delcourt and Ferrand started pushing.

## Closing

Reyer clipped the survey extracts to the front of the request for proposal and wrote 22 June on the folder. She had €480,000, a floor of 20%, €90,000 of design money that would not be topped up, 90 guaranteed seats out of 120, and three people inside one client who each meant something different by a good programme. Fourteen days later she would defend whichever version of it she had written.
