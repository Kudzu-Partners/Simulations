# Kantonales Amt für Bürgerdienste: Fourteen Thousand Cases and 72 Per Cent

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-2013 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2013 — *GovTech Steward* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On 11 June 2026 a neighbouring canton suspended its automated social-benefits eligibility system. A newspaper investigation had found it disadvantaged applicants by postal code and household language; a data-protection inquiry opened the same week. Andrea Zumbrunn, director of the Kantonales Amt für Bürgerdienste in the canton of Brunnental, read the suspension notice next to a shortlist her digital programme lead had circulated six days earlier: four candidate artificial-intelligence (AI) systems for KABD, one of them an automated benefits-eligibility scorer.

Two days later the cantonal parliament instructed Zumbrunn to present, before the quarter closed, a plan to modernise KABD with AI that cleared the agency's 14,000-case backlog and cut its 19-day average processing time without repeating the failure next door. The plan would be defended at a hearing of parliament's oversight committee in the last week of the quarter. She had CHF 6.5 million ring-fenced, four candidate systems and one quarter.

## The Protagonist

Andrea Zumbrunn, 53, had directed KABD since 2019, after eight years running the six regional service points and four in the cantonal finance directorate. She was known for a standing instruction that no citizen be told a decision was "in the system". She had never run a machine-learning deployment. Her digital and AI programme lead, Marco Iten, had. Her data-protection and legal officer, Béatrice Roth, had spent the spring on KABD's reading of the revised federal data-protection rules; her service-delivery manager, Selina Egger, owned the backlog; her communications lead, Tobias Frei, owned the trust survey.

## The Decision

Zumbrunn had to decide which of the four systems KABD would deploy first, what governance would be built into it before it went live, how its rollout would reach citizens, and how the whole of it would be accounted for to parliament.

Iten's team had tagged the four by rights-impact — how directly each touched a decision about a citizen's entitlements (see **Exhibit 2**). Document triage and classification, which routed and pre-filled incoming applications, was tagged low; a multilingual citizen chatbot and a fraud-anomaly detector were tagged medium; benefits eligibility scoring, which recommended approve or deny on social benefits, was tagged high and was the function the neighbouring canton had suspended. Together the four cost more than the cap allowed, so one would go first.

## The Company and its Setting

KABD, established in 1998, handled residency permits, social-benefit applications, business registrations and tax-document processing for the 620,000 residents of Brunnental. It employed 540 civil servants across one central office and six regional service points, and ran on an annual budget of CHF 96 million, of which CHF 6.5 million was ring-fenced for the digital-transformation programme.

The legal frame was Switzerland's revised Federal Act on Data Protection together with the canton's administrative-procedure rules. Roth's reading was that any automated decision affecting a citizen's rights carried obligations on transparency, human oversight, contestability and impact assessment, attaching before deployment rather than after it.

## The Situation

KABD entered the quarter with 14,000 pending cases, an average processing time of 19 days, a citizen-satisfaction score of 61 out of 100, and public trust of 72 per cent — the highest recorded for any institution in the canton (see **Exhibit 1**). Roth's assessment scored the agency's safeguard completeness against the revised data-protection rules at 40 out of 100 and rated its governance exposure high. The CHF 6.5 million was unspent. Iten's team had put a figure on the prize: 19 days cut to 6, and roughly CHF 2 million a year saved, on a deployment that had not been authorised.

If Zumbrunn changed nothing, the arithmetic was flat rather than dramatic: the backlog stayed at 14,000, the wait at 19 days, satisfaction at 61, trust at 72 per cent and the CHF 6.5 million in the account, and the committee's hearing took place with nothing in front of it.

Two items sat on the quarter's calendar. An internal audit of the historical case files the candidate systems would learn from was due back inside the quarter; Zumbrunn did not have its findings. And the reporter who broke the neighbouring canton's story had written to Frei asking for an interview about KABD's AI plans.

Inside the agency the argument had settled into two positions. Egger's service managers wanted the largest backlog win taken first, on the grounds that citizens waiting 19 days were being harmed now. A group around Roth wanted no automated system near an entitlement decision until the neighbouring canton's inquiry reported.

## What Zumbrunn Controlled

The programme plan she signed on 15 June ran in four stages.

In the first she would record where she held the delay to originate — in headcount, in paper-bound intake and manual routing upstream of any decision, or in what citizens submitted. She would fix how the four candidates were classified: ranked on backlog impact alone, tiered by rights-impact and trust-risk, or treated as uniformly risky. She would state in one line what KABD would never trade for efficiency — speed of decision, fair and contestable decisions for every citizen, or lowest cost to the canton. And she would set how much management attention went on the neighbouring canton's failure as a working lesson, from none to all of it.

In the second she would name the system to deploy first within the CHF 6.5 million cap and the governance shipping with it: the model alone, with the citizen receiving a letter; a transparency notice disclosing that an algorithm had been involved; or human-in-the-loop thresholds, transparency, appeal and contestability rights, bias testing and a named accountable owner together. She would decide what Roth's sign-off consisted of — skipped, a memo noting the federal act, or a full data-protection impact assessment before deployment — and what share of the CHF 6.5 million, from nothing up to half, was ring-fenced for monitoring, appeals and rollback rather than the build.

In the third she would sequence the rollout. If the audit returned a defect in the historical files, she would proceed unchanged, narrow the use case with compensating human review on the affected segments, or pause to rebalance and re-test before any rights-affecting use. She would set the pilot scope — social-benefits applicants first, canton-wide at once with no pilot, or a narrow pilot on business registrations and document triage at one service point — and what stood between phases: nothing, a quarterly performance report, or phase gates on live bias and error monitoring with a human fallback and a documented rollback plan. Remediation and bias-monitoring spend ran at CHF 0, CHF 0.8 million for a one-off rebalancing pass, or CHF 1.5 million with continuous monitoring.

In the fourth she would account for it. She would choose what to lead the committee with — the efficiency figures, the argument that the neighbouring canton's system was differently built, or what KABD had deployed and why it was fair, accountable and contestable before any efficiency claim. She would answer the reporter's comparison with the system under inquiry by declining it, by citing compliance with the federal act, or by showing the safeguard architecture, the bias work, the human review points and the appeal data. Under committee questioning she would point at the best-looking metric, attribute the gap to the vendor's model or the legacy data, or name the residual risk, the monitoring watching it and the person accountable. And she would state, as a percentage, how firmly she held that protecting trust came ahead of the processing-time target.

The programme board tracked four numbers and one rating, recorded beside each stage's decision. Citizen trust was the survey share of residents saying they trusted the agency, at 72 per cent. Backlog cleared counted pending cases resolved out of the 14,000. Governance compliance, on a scale of 0 to 100, scored safeguard completeness against the revised federal act, and stood at 40. Budget remaining was what was left of the CHF 6.5 million once build, safeguards and monitoring had come out of it. Governance exposure was a rating rather than a figure — contained, elevated or high — and KABD's was high, with lower being safer.

## Closing

At 07:20 on 15 June Zumbrunn wrote the four candidates on a single sheet and drew two columns beside them, one for how many of the 14,000 cases each would clear and one for what each would decide about a citizen. The hearing was in the last week of the quarter. The budget was CHF 6.5 million, the trust figure was 72 per cent, and the audit was not back.
