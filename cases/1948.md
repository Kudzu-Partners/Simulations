# Saraswati Textiles and Exports: Two Clocks, One Position

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-1948 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1948 — *Trade, Rules and Risk* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Wednesday, March 4, 2026, Meera Raghavan read the European Union notification twice before she called the shipping desk. A spring order worth INR 96 crore — one crore is ten million rupees — sat on hold in Rotterdam pending a compliance review, and the review named two of the fourteen dyeing and embroidery units that worked for Saraswati Textiles and Exports Ltd. under sub-contract.

By that evening three more documents had reached her desk in Tiruppur. The largest European buyer, worth INR 260 crore a year and 22% of revenue, invoked a contractual right to suspend orders unless Saraswati filed a remediation plan within 30 days. A second buyer required a third-party social and environmental audit of every sub-contracted unit within 60 days or it would move volume to another supplier. A coalition of non-governmental organisations, whose complaint about effluent discharge and excessive overtime had triggered the review, gave notice that it would publish a named report in 45 days. In New Delhi, the trade ministry told the industry association it was preparing to challenge the European measure as a disguised barrier — a proceeding that would run 18 to 36 months. Raghavan, General Counsel and Company Secretary, had to decide what position Saraswati would file. There would be one position, and the board, the buyer and the ministry would each hear it. The first deadline fell on April 3.

## The Protagonist

Meera Raghavan, 44, had been General Counsel and Company Secretary of Saraswati since 2019. She had spent eleven years in a Chennai commercial-disputes practice and four as head of contracts at an automotive-components exporter before the Velan family recruited her to professionalise the legal function. She had drafted the current framework agreements with the sub-contracted units in 2021. Twice — in the 2024 budget cycle and again in 2025 — she had proposed a standing audit programme covering those units, and twice the proposal had been deferred in favour of capacity investment. Both proposals were in the board minute book.

## The Decision

Raghavan had nine weeks and four filings. First, an exposure assessment for the board that Friday. Second, the company's legal and compliance posture towards the European measure and towards the government's dispute. Third, a costed due-diligence and remediation programme within the cash the company could actually commit. Fourth, a single recommendation she would have to defend in one sitting to the board chair, to the largest buyer and to an official of the trade ministry.

## The Company and its Setting

Saraswati Textiles and Exports Ltd., founded in 1994 by the Velan family and managed by professionals since 2017, made cotton knitwear in Tiruppur, Tamil Nadu. It ran three integrated units — spinning, dyeing and garmenting — employing 3,200 workers, roughly 60% of them women. Revenue was INR 1,180 crore with an operating margin of 11%, or INR 130 crore, and free cash flow of about INR 85 crore a year (see **Exhibit 1**). The European Union took 48% of revenue and the United States 27%; together the two markets were 75% of revenue, INR 885 crore.

The company's own units held the organic-cotton and chemical-safety certifications the European retailers asked for, and Saraswati operated the first zero-liquid-discharge effluent plant in the Tiruppur cluster. Around a third of its dye volume, however, went out to the fourteen sub-contracted units. Those units had never been audited, on effluent or on hours.

The European measure conditioned market access on documented environmental and human-rights due diligence across a supplier's own chain. Bringing all fourteen units to the standard the measure implied — effluent treatment equivalent to Saraswati's own, verified hours and wages — had been costed by the company's engineering team at INR 70 to 110 crore.

## The Situation

The company's board tracked the crisis on four measures, each scaled from 0 to 100, and on a single cash line. **Market access** stood at 100 and expressed how much of the EU and United States revenue was still secured. **Legal exposure** stood at 55 and combined Saraswati's own non-compliance with the risk of the dispute going against it; a lower number was better. **Reputational risk** stood at 50 and covered the pending report, the buyers' environmental and social committees, and their investors; lower was better here too. **Workers protected** stood at 100 and measured the share of sub-contractor jobs kept and brought up to standard. The cash line was cumulative compliance and remediation cost committed, against the INR 85 crore of free cash flow.

The deadlines did not align (see **Exhibit 2**). If Raghavan filed nothing, the shipment stayed frozen, the largest buyer's cure period lapsed on April 3, the coalition published on April 18, and the second buyer's audit deadline passed on May 3. None of those dates moved with the ministry's timetable.

## What Raghavan Controlled

For the board on Friday she had to rank the three exposures — legal, commercial and reputational. She could put the commercial and reputational deadlines first and treat the trade-law question as a separate, longer track; put the legal question first and settle Saraswati's position on the measure before answering the buyers; put the public record first and treat contracts and the dispute as consequences of it; or present the three as one simultaneous exposure with no ranking. She also had to characterise the company's own compliance — that the certified units were clean and the exposure lay in the measure; that the certified units were clean but a third of dye volume ran through fourteen unaudited ones; or that the sub-contractors were independent firms whose practices were not Saraswati's. And she had to name a figure for revenue at risk.

Then the posture. She could support the ministry's challenge and hold the company's position until the panel ruled; accept the standard and file a full remediation and due-diligence plan on the public record; or meet the buyers' requirements on their timetable while keeping the trade-law argument formally reserved. Separately, she could file jointly with the ministry and adopt its argument as Saraswati's own public position, support the systemic argument privately while filing a firm-level plan that conceded nothing for the industry, or disavow the dispute publicly. And she had to tell the board which calendar governed the next 60 days: the panel's or the contracts'.

Then the money. Within INR 85 crore she could fund audit and effluent upgrades at the sub-contracted units; bring critical dyeing capacity inside her own certified plants; and pay for third-party auditing, a worker grievance channel and wage verification. Whatever she did not fund was, in effect, an exit — and she had to choose the form: a phased wind-down with severance and re-placement, immediate termination of the non-compliant units, or continued sourcing without disclosure. Against the coalition she could go to it before publication with the plan and the evidence and contest what she believed wrong, write warning of legal action, or make no approach.

Finally the defence. To the buyer she could send the costed schedule with audit scope and dates, a general undertaking of full compliance without the schedule, or a statement that the sub-contractors were independent and the measure unlawful. To the board she could name the trade-off across all four measures, hold the remediation spend down to protect margin, or put unfreezing the shipment ahead of the other three measures. To the ministry she could answer that the remediation was voluntary practice with rights reserved, that the plan admitted non-compliance, or that the dispute should be dropped. The chair had also asked her to put a number on the last line of the memorandum: how firmly, on a scale of nothing to certainty, she held that both calendars could be managed at once.

## Closing

At nine on the Friday morning Raghavan laid the four documents side by side: the notification, the buyer's cure letter, the coalition's notice, and the engineering costing. The cure period ran to April 3, the report was due on April 18, the audit on May 3, and a ruling somewhere between eighteen and thirty-six months out. She had INR 85 crore and one position to file.
