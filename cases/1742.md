# DataSense — From Dashboards to Decisions

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1742 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1742 — *DataSense: From Dashboards to Decisions* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

One Monday morning, at 8:52, Laura Mendes-Carvalho's phone lit up with a screenshot from her Chief Marketing Officer, Tatiana Brugal-Henríquez, and eight words underneath it: *what does this actually mean — board prep Wednesday.* Three numbers were circled in red: customer acquisition cost up 18 percent quarter on quarter, from USD 38 to USD 45; conversion from prospect to funded account down 6 percent, from 11.2 to 10.6 percent; and a note that the attribution model — which credited a channel for winning the customer — had changed mid-quarter, on September 4, from one that spread credit across every channel touched to one that gave all the credit to the first channel touched. The dashboard did not say where that change happened.

Laura was the Marketing Operations Lead at Caribia Banca Digital, and she had forty-eight hours — until the Wednesday board prep — to turn the confusion behind that screenshot into something Tatiana could defend in front of the board.

## The Protagonist

Laura ran two teams inside Caribia's marketing function — campaign analytics and martech-and-attribution — after being promoted into the newly created Marketing Operations Lead role. Her dotted-line manager, María Camila Vélez-Ortiz, who chaired the bank's cross-functional committee on marketing and customer data, had already messaged her: *Tati's going to ask me before she asks you again. What do I tell her?* Laura's solid reporting line ran to Tatiana. This week, her accountability ran to both of them at once.

## The Decision

Laura's forty-eight hours, tracked artifact-by-artifact in Exhibit 2, broke into three working sessions and a closing conversation. Monday morning, before committing any analyst time, she had to decide how to answer Tatiana and how to direct her two team leads. Monday afternoon, she had to decide what survived a rebuild of the Q3 dashboard and how the September 4 change would be explained. Tuesday afternoon, she had to decide what went onto the single slide Tatiana would carry into the board prep, after her martech lead's first draft — six numbers, ten sentences, no recommendation — landed on her desk without one.

## The Company and its Setting

Caribia Banca Digital was a Caribbean-region digital bank headquartered in Santo Domingo, Dominican Republic. Marketing operated as its own function inside the bank, organized into directorates that included Laura's two groups. The Q3 dashboard her teams had built sat on a single tab in the reporting tool: twelve charts, refreshed daily, with no place on any of them to flag the day the attribution model changed. Wednesday's board session would include two non-executive directors with backgrounds in marketing analytics — one a former CMO, one a former Director of Marketing Sciences — who would recognize an unannotated model change on sight.

## The Situation

By the time Laura's afternoon session started, Sofía had triangulated the 18 percent CAC move into three pieces: roughly six percentage points from a shift in channel mix, nine points from the attribution-model change itself, and three points of real cost movement. Close to half the headline number, in other words, came from how the credit was counted rather than from what customers actually cost to acquire. The unit-economics picture looked steadier — per Exhibit 1, the lifetime-value-to-CAC ratio stood at 9.3 times, against 9.5 times the previous quarter. Within the existing Q4 budget, roughly USD 0.7 million looked recoverable through reallocation — though the nine-point methodology estimate carried a range, six to twelve points, still to be confirmed. If the message and board materials went out exactly as the dashboard first showed them, the 18 percent figure would reach the board circled in red and unexplained — a true number, but only about half of it about customers actually costing more to acquire.

## What Laura Controlled

On Monday morning, before any analyst time was spent, four things were hers to decide. What she told Tatiana could be a promise that the full answer would be ready Wednesday morning, no caveats attached; a promise of a defensible read with a named hedge by Wednesday; or an immediate, flat statement that the movement was nothing more than the methodology change. What she told Diego, who led martech and attribution, could be to send him straight into the multi-touch backcast before the scoping note was done; to hold him until the note cleared at eleven, then assign him after; or to have him start the attribution-method note before scoping was finished. What she told Sofía, who led campaign analytics, could be to run the full channel-by-channel query set for the quarter; to hold her back until the note had cleared; or to have her run only the channel-mix breakdown the note would need. And the note itself could go out as a verbal alignment simply raised at the next team stand-up, a one-page note naming what the data could not yet say, or a long page covering the full methodology end to end.

Monday afternoon carried four more. The rebuilt dashboard could mean keeping all twelve charts on the tab and redesigning each one to read more cleanly; narrowing down to six charts, keeping only the ones with the highest traffic; narrowing to three: the annotated CAC trend, channel spend versus budget, and lifetime value by cohort; or narrowing all the way down to two, just the trend chart and the budget chart. That change on the trend chart could be marked with a small footnote at the chart's bottom; a vertical line on September 4 with a two-line callout on the chart itself; or a thirteenth chart, added on its own, showing the methodology effect separately. The attribution-method note could run as two short, conversational paragraphs without the supporting numbers; one page in four short paragraphs with the bridge calculation spelled out; or a five-page methodology document with full statistical appendices. And before any of it reached Tatiana, Hernán, who had signed off the September migration, could be skipped and the note sent straight to Tatiana; brought in for a verbal alignment over the phone; or asked for a written email approval first.

Tuesday afternoon narrowed to the single slide, and five choices sat inside it. The first number could be the headline blended CAC of USD 45 on its own; the bridge-adjusted CAC of USD 41, with the USD 45 headline shown alongside for context; or the share of attribution held by each channel in the mix. The second number could be a complete channel-by-channel CAC table broken out by acquisition source; the LTV-to-CAC ratio, 9.3 times against 9.5 times the prior quarter; or the conversion-to-funded-account headline number, presented entirely on its own. The third could be the quarter's movement in the brand-awareness survey index; the recoverable Q4 upside, roughly USD 0.7 million, available through reallocation; or the share-of-voice figure across the bank's markets. The single recommendation could be increasing the marketing budget for the fourth quarter across the board, reviewed once results came in; reallocating roughly USD 0.7 million within the existing Q4 budget across two named channels, reviewed at the mid-November mark; or waiting for the Q4 mid-quarter read in November before taking any action at all. And the one named risk on the slide could be the possibility that the nine-point methodology estimate understated the true effect, with a range from six to twelve points; the general macroeconomic environment the bank expected heading into the fourth quarter across its markets; or the possibility that the same channel-mix shift seen in Q3 simply continued into the fourth quarter.

In the closing minutes, Laura had one more thing to name out loud: which of four habits she would practice not falling back into for the next thirty days at her desk. Running the queries before the actual question had even been scoped out. Treating a confused reading of the dashboard as the reader's problem rather than the dashboard's. Promising a single, final answer when the data only supported a defensible read. Or putting the most material disclosure of the week into a footnote instead of the lead.

## Closing

By Wednesday morning the board prep had gone the way Laura hoped: Tatiana led with the hedge, and the analytics-background board member simply nodded. What was left was smaller and, in its own way, harder — which of those four habits she would actually spend the next month refusing to fall back into.
