# Helio Verde Beverages: The Master File

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1381 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1381 — *From Spreadsheet to Database — Why Excel Breaks at 1M Rows* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, April 7, 2026, at 15:10, Camila Ferreyra-Ocampo watched a progress bar crawl across her screen for the sixth minute. The file was the master sales workbook of the Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP) team at Helio Verde Beverages, S.A., and it held 1.4 million order lines. At 15:16 it opened. At 15:17 her phone rang. Inés Garrido-Lobos, the chief financial officer (CFO), had one question: what, exactly, was slowing this down?

It was the first of nine questions. Garrido-Lobos would not sign the second-quarter board pack until someone could defend the numbers in it. She wanted Ferreyra-Ocampo to diagnose why the workbook failed, design what would replace it and recommend the platform the board should pay for, one logged answer at a time. The first three were due before the board met on April 23 to decide whether to leave the spreadsheet at all.

## The Protagonist

Ferreyra-Ocampo, thirty-one, had run the S&OP team's analysis for three years. Colleagues called her when a lookup formula broke, and she had built much of the master file herself. She wrote some SQL, the standard database query language, mostly for extracts. She had never designed a database or defended a technology budget. Twelve analysts worked from the file, and the CFO had just made her lead of its migration.

## The Decision

Ferreyra-Ocampo had to decide, nine times, what was true about the workbook and what to build in its place, and explain each answer to a CFO who would repeat it to the board. Three answers would diagnose the failure, three would shape the new database if the board approved one, and three would settle how analysts used it and which platform it ran on (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde Beverages was the soft-drinks division of the Helio Verde consumer-goods group, with about €380 million in annual revenue, 1,600 employees and two bottling plants. It served roughly 14,000 retail accounts, from supermarket chains to neighbourhood shops (see Exhibit 1). Each account ordered one to twelve times a month, and each order line carried product, quantity, price, discount, promotion, delivery, invoice and payment fields: about four million lines a year.

The S&OP team kept the trailing four months in one workbook, shared through SharePoint co-authoring. A single sheet stopped at 1,048,576 rows, so the file was split across sheets stitched together with lookups. From it came promotion returns, volume forecasts and the CFO's explanations whenever a quarter missed plan. The board's audit committee expected those numbers to stand up to a Sarbanes-Oxley standard of evidence.

## The Situation

The week before, a memo from the S&OP director, Ignacio Bustamante-Leal, had leaked onto an internal chat channel. The file crashed about twice a week, it said, usually in the afternoon, when twelve people had it open. It took 6 minutes 12 seconds to open on the 16-gigabyte laptops and 9 minutes 40 seconds on the 8-gigabyte machines. A conditional sum across the file took 38 seconds the first time and 11 seconds on recalculation. A pivot table took 70 seconds to refresh.

It also described an incident. Marta, in Trade Marketing, had typed *Mercado del Sur* into a customer cell. Eleven minutes later Pedro applied a filter that re-sorted the sheet. When the co-authoring service merged the two, Marta's entry landed on an unrelated row. Three minutes after that, Ana's autosave stored a pivot table built on the damaged range. Nobody could tell the CFO what Mercado Sur, one of the largest customers, had bought in the first quarter.

A dashboard the team built over the weekend found 47 spellings of *Mercado Sur* in the customer column. There were 312 product strings for 280 products, 58 promotion codes for 40 active promotions and 31 territory names for 22 territories. The fourth-quarter promotion report had shown a 14% lift for Mercado Sur; the January board pack said 9%. Version history went back 30 days. The November snapshot was gone, and nothing recorded who had edited what, or when.

Garrido-Lobos kept a scorecard, updated after every logged answer. Board confidence stood at 30 of 100. Audit defensibility, how far a reported number could be traced to its source and its edits, stood at 20. Query performance, how quickly the analysts' standard questions came back, stood at 25. Migration progress stood at 0%. A migration score out of 100 combined the four and stood at 19. Teodoro Vidaurre-Smits, an external database architect retained by the CFO, marked each answer as one he would sign or not. The log kept each answer, his mark, and the board, query and overall readings after it. At the end the programme would be graded Beginner, Apprentice, Solid or Excellent.

If nothing changed, the file would keep taking in a month of orders every month, keep crashing, and reach the second-quarter board pack with numbers nobody could trace. The scorecard would stay at 19.

## What Ferreyra-Ocampo Controlled

In the diagnosis phase she gave three answers. **What bound the file's speed**: the disk, too slow to read a file that size; the network, slowed by syncing co-authored edits; memory, exhausted, with recalculation said not to use all of a laptop's cores; or the graphics card, which the software did not use. **What caused the Mercado Sur corruption**: Marta's mistake in not locking the cell first; a tool designed for one editor, with shared editing added later and breaking under sorts and filters; a defect to report to the software's vendor; or weak backup habits, fixed by restoring the previous day's copy and banning Tuesday edits. **What rule should trigger leaving the spreadsheet**, for the board: crossing the row ceiling of a single sheet, the software's hard limit; performance becoming unacceptable to the people using the file; any one of four problems (speed, shared editing, integrity, audit trail) biting hard; or never, since a disciplined team could keep the file working.

If the board agreed, IT would load the full order history into a PostgreSQL database on a small virtual machine. Hernán Olavarría-Pinto, the most senior analyst, already wanted to import the workbook into one large table and start querying. **How to organise the schema**: that one table, as it stood; customer details copied onto every order line, for faster queries; master tables linked by keys to order, line, invoice and payment tables; or the strictest normal form for every table, whether or not a problem required it. **How to stop the 47 spellings returning**, with three departments editing names: a wiki page of canonical names that analysts were trusted to follow; a weekly script that found and merged duplicates; a rule that every order point to an existing customer, added by one owner; or free text checked by a fuzzy-matching validator as names were typed. **Which columns to index**: none, trusting a modern engine to manage it alone; every column, since disk space was cheap; primary keys only, which the engine indexed anyway; or keys plus the dates and other columns analysts filtered and joined on.

The last three answers belonged to the running database. **How to serve dashboards** that joined seven tables: keep them on the transactional schema, since hardware was cheap; move analytics to a document database said to aggregate better; a separate layer, one central sales table ringed by descriptive tables, loaded nightly; or a nightly export file for analysts to open in Excel. **How to list customers who ordered in 2023 but not in 2024**, a standing Marketing request: an inner join on customer, with date filters in the join; one year's customer count subtracted from the other's; a left join keeping 2023 customers with no 2024 order; or two pivots and a lookup, rebuilt in SQL. And **which platform tier to recommend**: spreadsheets alone, under stricter discipline; spreadsheets plus the Access desktop database; PostgreSQL on a single small virtual machine; a managed cloud database plus a warehouse stack for analytics; or the warehouse stack alone, skipping the transactional layer (see Exhibit 2).

## Closing

At 15:18 Ferreyra-Ocampo put the CFO on speaker and pulled the timing log onto her second screen. The workbook was open, for now. Garrido-Lobos was waiting, and the first line of the migration log was blank.
