# Aarav Mobility Systems: The Capability Cycle

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1989 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1989 — *Resilient by Design* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, April 13, 2026, Rohini Deshmukh read the three funding submissions that had reached her desk over the weekend and noticed that none of them acknowledged the other two. Aarav Mobility's internal-combustion division wanted ₹900 crore to hold the powertrain business flat. The software-defined-vehicle line wanted ₹480 crore to hire, to tool, and — if the board allowed it — to buy a forty-engineer software firm for ₹380 crore. The international team wanted ₹520 crore for a plant in Southeast Asia. The asks came to ₹1,900 crore (see **Exhibit 2**). The board had approved ₹1,200 crore.

Deshmukh had been Head of Strategy for eleven days. Anil Barve, the board chair, had given her the four quarters to March 2027 — the board called it the capability cycle — and a mandate of one sentence: make Aarav resilient by design rather than dependent on a single shrinking platform. Her first submission, a capability diagnosis with a resilience baseline attached, was due to the strategy committee on May 8.

## The Protagonist

Rohini Deshmukh, 43, had joined Aarav in 2014 and had run its Stuttgart engineering hub from 2021 until her appointment in Pune. Her career had been built inside the combustion business she was now being asked to judge — eight years in commercial-vehicle powertrain at a European supplier, then five on the accounts that still paid for most of the group. In 2022 she had written a memo arguing that the electrified-driveline programme was under-resourced by a factor of three. It was received politely and shelved. Her long-term incentive, unlike her predecessor's, moved with the board's resilience score rather than with group margin.

## The Decision

Deshmukh had to split ₹1,200 crore across claims worth ₹1,900 crore, decide how Aarav would enter Southeast Asia, and hold back whatever the firm would need to change course inside the cycle.

Suhas Rane, the chief financial officer, had put the last part to her plainly: every rupee left unallocated earned nothing, and he would have to explain it.

## The Company and its Setting

Aarav Mobility Systems Ltd., founded in Pune in 2004, supplied powertrain components, control electronics and software modules to commercial-vehicle and off-highway manufacturers. FY2025 revenue was ₹6,800 crore (about US$815 million) at a 13.5% EBITDA margin. It employed 9,400 people across plants in India, the Stuttgart hub and a Mexican assembly facility opened in 2024, and sold into 22 countries on 44% export revenue (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two facts shaped everything else. Combustion powertrain components were 71% of revenue at a 14% divisional margin — the cash that funded the rest — and that revenue was falling 6% to 9% a year as commercial fleets electrified. Two anchor customers had told Aarav they expected to halve their combustion orders within three years. The software-defined-vehicle and electrified-driveline lines were the other 29%, growing above 30% a year at a −4% margin.

## The Situation

The electrified line, under Tanvi Menon, had bid for three vehicle-platform contracts in eighteen months and been shortlisted for none. Menon read the three debriefs as saying that Aarav could supply the hardware but not own the embedded software and systems integration a platform required. Prakash Iyer, who ran the combustion division, read them differently. The relationships with those customers were thirty years old and would carry Aarav into their electric programmes on service record alone. The international team read them a third way: the binding gap was presence, not engineering, since Aarav had no brand and no channel in the Southeast Asian markets where commercial-electric volume was forming. Operations read them a fourth way: the engineering had never been the problem, and Aarav had lost the bids because it could not promise platform volume from plants it did not yet have.

Deshmukh's instruments were thin. The board would read four numbers each quarter and one clock. The Resilience Index — a 0-to-100 composite its advisers had assembled from capability breadth, financial slack and optionality — stood at 38. Market share retained, against the contested electric and software-defined volume, stood at 100%. The optionality reserve, the unspent portion of the budget, was ₹0. Group margin was 13.5%. The clock would count the quarters Aarav needed to redeploy capacity after a discontinuity; Barve said he would read it before the margin.

The risk committee had put three discontinuities on the register and declined to rank them: a withdrawal of electric-vehicle purchase subsidies that would revive combustion demand for several quarters; a fall in combustion orders faster than the 6-to-9% trend; and an anchor customer taking a module in-house. Its instruction was to assume one would land inside the cycle and to say nothing about which. The planning team estimated that a company still redeploying long after such an event would give up 6 to 9 points of the contested share and not recover them.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was undramatic. The combustion business would keep earning 14% on a shrinking base; the electrified lines would keep growing above 30% at −4% on a base too small to matter; the platform bids would keep going to firms that could write the software. Group margin would hold two or three years on the strength of the declining engine, then follow it down.

## What Deshmukh Controlled

In the first quarter she had to name which of four candidate constraints — customer relationships, Southeast Asian brand and channel, embedded-software and systems-integration depth, or plant capacity — bound Aarav's ability to capture the electric opportunity, or record that none of them did. She had to say where the firm was weakest as between reading a shift early, acting on it at scale, and reconfiguring its assets away from combustion, or that no one of the three stood out. She had to fix an opening posture: protect the combustion margin and the customers funding the group, move decisively into the electrified lines, or run both and hold capital back. And she had to set the Resilience-Index baseline, between 20 and 60, she would be judged against.

In the second she had to divide the ₹1,200 crore between sustaining the combustion business, building the capability she had named, and earmarking capital for the entry move — and to say how the software depth would be built: by hiring and training, by acquiring the forty-engineer firm, or by deferring the question to a later cycle. Whatever she did not allocate stood as the optionality reserve.

In the third she had to lock the entry mode: a wholly owned plant at US$62 million (₹520 crore), thirty months to build, full control; a joint venture at US$29 million (₹240 crore), quicker to stand up, control and risk shared; or licensing and export at US$8 million (₹70 crore), light on capital and light on control (see **Exhibit 2**). A discontinuity would be reported to her inside the same quarter. She would then say whether to hold the plan, reallocate capacity toward wherever demand had moved, or commit harder to the position already taken; whether to fund that from the reserve, from emergency borrowing, or by cutting the capability build; and how much of the quarter's capacity to redeploy at once.

In the fourth a second-order complication would follow — a partner seeking to renegotiate, acquired engineers at flight risk, a competitor moving sooner — and she would leave it, fix it once, or reconfigure around it. She would commit Aarav to a standing routine of scanning cadence, modular capacity and a reserve rule; to a scanning cadence alone; or to nothing standing. And she would tell Barve what the cycle had proved — that the margin held, that Aarav had read the discontinuity correctly, or that it had built the capacity to absorb whichever one arrived — and record, as a percentage, how firmly she held it.

## Closing

At 9:20 that Monday, Deshmukh wrote three figures on a sheet of paper — 900, 480, 520 — drew a line under them and wrote 1,200. The strategy committee met on May 8; Barve wanted the allocation by July and the entry mode locked by October. She had ₹700 crore less than the company had asked for, four numbers, and a clock she had not yet started.
