# Verlinden Industrial Solutions: Choosing Where to Digitize

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1855 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1855 — *Digital Edge* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 11 March 2026, Bieke Vandenberghe, B2B Marketing Director at Verlinden Industrial Solutions NV, read, a second time, a letter from the company's largest customer. Hanssen Food Systems, which bought EUR 14.8 million of fastening, sealing and fluid-handling components a year — 8 percent of Verlinden's revenue — was moving its procurement onto a digital portal in the third quarter. Any supplier that could not integrate via EDI/punch-out and expose live stock and pricing through an API by 1 September would drop to a backup tier, capped at 30 percent of category spend — a cut of roughly EUR 10.4 million of annual revenue.

The board gave her ninety days to bring back a recommendation, and one budget to fund it: EUR 6.0 million, fixed for eighteen months, with no top-up if she spent it on the wrong thing.

## The Protagonist

Vandenberghe had run B2B marketing at Verlinden for six years, building the role around one belief: customers were not really buying fasteners and gaskets, but the certainty that the specified part would work in their machine — backed by a technical-applications team of 38 who had built that certainty one specification at a time. The Hanssen letter was the first document in years to make her wonder whether that belief still described what customers were paying for.

## The Decision

Over the ninety days that followed, Vandenberghe had to move through the problem in stages: locate where the advantage was actually eroding and which response addressed that rather than a symptom of it; commit the fixed EUR 6.0 million to one lead investment and, if the numbers allowed, a second; redesign how orders reached Verlinden and what its sales reps did with their time; and take the plan to the board, where a live move by a competitor would test it.

## The Company and its Setting

Verlinden Industrial Solutions NV, headquartered in Kortrijk, Belgium, manufactured and distributed industrial fastening, sealing and fluid-handling components to 3,200 active B2B accounts from a catalogue of 28,000 SKUs. Revenue in 2025 had reached EUR 184 million at a 12 percent EBITDA margin. Of its 290 employees, 64 worked in field and inside sales, and 38 formed the technical-applications group that specified components against customer problems.

## The Situation

Verlinden's digital revenue share stood at 9 percent, against a sector benchmark of 28 to 35 percent; the board wanted at least the low end within eighteen months. Two other numbers had already been moving before Hanssen's letter arrived: the win rate on competitive quotes had slid from 41 percent to 33 percent over two years, and two pure-play digital rivals were quoting online in seconds at 6 to 9 percent below Verlinden's list price. Separately, eleven further accounts worth a combined EUR 41 million — 22 percent of revenue — had signalled through the CRM that they expected the same kind of digital procurement Hanssen was now demanding. If nothing changed, Hanssen's cut alone cost roughly EUR 10.4 million a year, the eleven accounts could follow on their own timelines, and the win-rate slide would continue against rivals already quoting faster and cheaper (see **Exhibit 1**).

Three gauges would track whatever she decided. Digital revenue share measured the percentage of sales moving through digital channels rather than phone or a rep visit. Moat strength, scored zero to a hundred and starting at 28, measured how defensible the technical-applications team's expertise still was. Margin health, also scored zero to a hundred and starting at 45, tracked how well EBITDA held up against a price war with the pure-plays. A fourth simply tracked euros committed against the EUR 6.0 million ceiling.

## What Vandenberghe Controlled

The first task was diagnosis: four readings of where the value proposition was eroding. That the pure-plays were simply cheaper and Verlinden had to match them on price; that the components themselves had become interchangeable commodities that any competent rival in the market could now ship; that customers could not order, check stock or get pricing digitally, though the expertise behind it still carried value; or that nothing had structurally changed and Hanssen's ultimatum was posturing that would fade if she waited it out. She also had to name which of three places to test first: moving straight to a subscription-based business model that charged for managed inventory; automating the ordering and pricing process, from stock visibility through to real-time quotes; embedding data services into the components themselves before touching anything else; or spreading the six-million-euro budget evenly across all three at once. A separate scale, zero to a hundred, set how sharply her brief would name the technical-applications team as the thing worth protecting.

Next came the money (see **Exhibit 2**): one investment named as the lead, funded at depth. The process package — API/EDI integration, a self-service portal and a real-time pricing engine, for EUR 2.6 million over six months; the product package — embedding IoT-based data services into high-value components, for EUR 3.8 million over fourteen months; or the business-model package — converting managed-inventory MRO accounts to a subscription fee, for EUR 4.4 million over eighteen months. Alongside the lead, a secondary, partial investment: piloting the subscription model on a single MRO segment for EUR 2.0 million; holding the remaining budget in reserve rather than committing any of it now; or seeding the product's data service on a starter set of SKUs for EUR 2.2 million. A separate figure, zero up to the full EUR 6.0 million, set how much she committed now versus held back for a sequenced bet. And she had to describe the trade-off to the board: naming explicitly which path would not be funded at depth this year; leaving the question open to be decided later; or telling the board all three paths could be pursued within budget.

Then came the channel and the sales force: a target, zero to 80 percent, for how much of Verlinden's order volume would move to self-service within eighteen months, and how many of the 64 field and inside-sales reps would be redeployed from taking orders toward technical, solution-based selling. She had to price the digital channel against the assisted one: matching list price across every channel to keep pricing simple; charging a service premium wherever expertise applied, keeping self-service lean on routine parts; or undercutting the pure-plays online by eight percent to compete on their own terms. And a mechanism, or none, for the friction this could create with the sales force: crediting reps for every digital order placed within their own accounts and redesigning commissions around solution value; mandating digital ordering outright and policing any rep who steered customers back to the phone; or leaving reps to adapt on their own once the portal proved itself.

At the board meeting, four more decisions closed the case: the governance dashboard she would commit to — reporting digital revenue alone to the board on a quarterly cycle, without other measures attached; building a one-time dashboard for launch week, then moving to the next priority; or committing to digital share, adoption and margin impact together at six, twelve and eighteen months. An answer to a pure-play that had, that morning, quoted Hanssen eight percent below Verlinden's list: holding price and showing Hanssen the integrated availability and specification value the pure-play could not match; conceding four percent, splitting the difference to keep the account warm; or matching the eight percent cut outright to hold onto Hanssen at any cost. A pivot trigger for redirecting any reserve to a second layer: a general review of the plan again sometime next year; a specific number — eighteen percent digital share missed at month twelve — redirecting the reserve; or no trigger at all, committing fully to the chosen path. And, on a final scale of zero to a hundred, how firmly she would defend, in the room, that digitization had to make the technical team's expertise worth more rather than trade it away.

## Closing

Vandenberghe set the Hanssen letter on the win-rate chart and the CRM list of eleven accounts, beside a blank sheet where the EUR 6.0 million would be divided. The board wanted its recommendation in ninety days. Whatever she chose would meet Hanssen's procurement portal on 1 September, and whatever the two pure-play rivals did before then.
