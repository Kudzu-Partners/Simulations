# Aceros del Plata S.A.: Through the Cycle

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1884 · **Companion simulation:** Express — *Cycle Strategist* (sim 1884), 4 rounds, EN · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

In the second week of May 2026, Lucía Ferreyra carried the same slide into her third meeting of the week: Aceros del Plata's mini-mill in Campana running near 92 percent of capacity, order books full, and a market-intelligence memo warning that the conditions producing that number were already turning. The board wanted her recommendation on a US$140 million expansion within thirty days. The equipment supplier would hold its price and its production slot for forty-five.

As VP of Strategy, Ferreyra had spent two years watching demand for rebar and wire rod outrun the mill's two rolling lines. Her own market-intelligence team was now flagging that same demand as a peak already turning. The new line would take twenty-four months to build — long enough, on the team's reading of the cycle, to come online after the market had already turned.

## The Protagonist

Ferreyra was forty-four. She had grown up a few blocks from the mill gate her grandfather had worked behind, joined Aceros del Plata in 2015 as a commercial analyst, and had run corporate strategy since 2021 — the year the board began asking her, not only the CFO, to open the monthly numbers review. She had not been on the payroll during the 2001–02 collapse, but she had grown up on family accounts of the mill running half shifts and cutting a third of its workforce inside a year, and the memory sat underneath most of the strategy work she did, named or not.

## The Decision

The board wanted one recommendation on the expansion. In practice Ferreyra was making four decisions across the year ahead, each constraining the next: how to read the market's position in its cycle, when — or whether — to commit new capacity, how to finance and hedge whatever got built, and what competitive move to make once the cycle bottomed. Underneath all four sat one question the board kept returning to: could the company clear the coming downturn without breaching its lending covenant, and still hold the means to act if the bottom created an opportunity?

## The Company and its Setting

Aceros del Plata ran one electric-arc-furnace mini-mill and two rolling lines in Campana, Buenos Aires province, turning scrap steel into rebar, wire rod and merchant bars for the domestic construction sector. The mill employed 1,150 people, with a nameplate capacity of 600,000 tonnes a year and typical utilization near 78 percent. On 2025 revenue of AR$540,000 million — about US$450 million — it had posted a 16 percent EBITDA margin, a 12 percent return on invested capital, and net debt of 1.4 times EBITDA (Exhibit 1). It held about 22 percent of the domestic long-steel market, behind a larger incumbent's roughly 45 percent.

## The Situation

Two years of strong demand and a steadier peso had pushed utilization from its usual 78 percent to near 92, and the board had grown used to reading that number as good news. Ferreyra's own market-intelligence team read the same indicators — cement dispatches down two quarters, central-bank tightening, softening mortgage approvals, a sovereign spread 250 basis points wider in a month — as signaling a downturn within twelve to eighteen months. A typical Argentine construction downturn cut long-steel volume 20 to 30 percent within a year; the worst on record had cut it 35 percent (Exhibit 2).

The expansion on the table — a third rolling line and furnace upgrade, US$140 million (AR$168,000 million), for 40 percent more capacity — would take twenty-four months to commission. Funded entirely with dollar debt, it would carry net leverage to roughly 3.4 times EBITDA at the current peak, against a bank covenant of 3.5 times; measured against the EBITDA the mill was likely to earn if the downturn arrived, the same debt implied leverage near 5.0 times. The order was priced in dollars against revenue almost entirely in pesos. The larger incumbent had already announced its own expansion, and the smaller, over-leveraged rival was rumored to be within reach of a distressed sale, at a price then estimated near US$60 million.

## What Ferreyra Controlled

The first call was a diagnosis. The market might be mid-cycle and steady, with room left to run before anything turned toward a reversal; early in a longer expansion, where the recent strength was itself the new baseline; or near the top of the cycle, with the leading indicators already turning. Attached to it was a choice of evidence: none of the four signals, on the premise that current demand was the best forecast of future demand; the cement and mortgage data alone, with the monetary and sovereign-spread readings set aside as noise; or all four — cement dispatches, mortgage approvals, central-bank tightening and the sovereign spread — read together as one system. She also had to state, as a percentage, the probability that a downturn arrived within eighteen months.

The second call was the capacity itself: deferring the whole expansion to the trough, on the logic that the same capacity would be cheaper once the market had turned; approving the full line now, locking the supplier's slot and building to the demand the mill was seeing that month; declining any new capacity entirely and running the two existing rolling lines harder instead of building; or committing only the furnace upgrade now and holding the rolling line as an option, deciding the rest as conditions clarified. Bound up with it was the supplier's forty-five-day hold: sign the complete order that same week, before the deadline; let the hold lapse and accept whatever the supplier then charged; or place a refundable deposit that held the slot without full commitment. She also had to choose which EBITDA the return would be measured against: the EBITDA the mill happened to be earning that month, projected forward; the EBITDA likely in the trough the line would commission into; or a blended average taken across booms and busts alike.

The third call set the balance sheet: the company's own cash, spent down to avoid taking on new debt; the bank's dollar facility for nearly the whole amount, the least expensive option on offer, repayable in dollars against peso revenue; a blend of retained cash and modest dollar debt with an equity issue in reserve; or local peso debt, which matched the mill's revenue currency but cost more and matured sooner. She also had to set a leverage ceiling against trough, not peak, EBITDA, and a minimum liquidity buffer: a cushion of US$70 million, sized to survive the downturn with something left over; no minimum at all, investing the balance sheet fully in the expansion; or a thin cushion of US$30 million, enough to cover working capital through a rough quarter. On the dollar order against peso revenue: leave the exposure entirely open, on the view that the peso would hold through the build; hedge the dollar progress payments so a peso move could not inflate the project's cost; or hedge half the exposure and leave the remaining half of it open.

The fourth call, once the trough arrived, was competitive: keeping the balance sheet entirely defensive, adding no capacity and making no competitive move; holding the existing lines and defending the mill's share on cost and service through the downturn; preserving dry powder to acquire the smaller, over-leveraged rival near the bottom, at a price then estimated near US$60 million; or matching the incumbent's own expansion move for move, adding capacity for capacity at the top of the market. She then had to say whether the move reconciled with the leverage and liquidity already committed: partly, on the understanding that financing would be arranged later if the opportunity actually appeared; that the question needed no answer yet, since the balance sheet would catch up eventually; or yes, because the ceiling and buffer had been set with exactly this move in mind. Alongside it she had to state, as a percentage, her own conviction that the cycle functioned as a strategic instrument rather than only a hazard to be endured.

## Closing

Ferreyra closed the market-intelligence memo and opened a blank page for the board. Thirty days to recommend; forty-five before the supplier's price moved against her; twenty-four months before whatever she decided arrived on the mill floor, into whatever the cycle had become by then. The board's own draft of the recommendation, she already knew, leaned toward yes, in full, this quarter.
