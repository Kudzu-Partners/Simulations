# Pay & Comply: Solvio Pay's Triple Bind

On the morning of 2 June 2026, Élodie Fontaine found three folders on her desk and realised they described the same quarter. The first was a cost model showing that Solvio Pay's card-scheme payment rail was losing ground against a cheaper alternative. The second was a compliance calendar: the company's first mandatory sustainability report under the EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive was due with the annual accounts, on a compliance budget of EUR 1.1M to EUR 1.9M. The third was a note from the head of communications: a financial-press journalist was preparing a piece questioning whether Solvio's "green fintech" claims were substantiated, and wanted a comment by Tuesday.

Fontaine had one quarter — to the FY26 filing deadline — to decide how Solvio would price its payment rail, spend a ring-fenced climate budget, and disclose both to regulators, investors and the press.

## The Protagonist

Élodie Fontaine, 44, had been Chief Financial Officer of Solvio Pay SAS since 2023, after seven years in payments finance at a French banking group. She had spent her first eighteen months at Solvio tightening unit economics rather than chasing growth. She reported to a board including two directors appointed by the Series C investors, both impact-oriented funds. Her rule, formed over two decades in finance, was that a number either held up under scrutiny or it did not, and she had little patience for language that sounded better than it could be defended.

## The Decision

Eighteen months earlier, Solvio had raised EUR 60M from two impact funds whose term sheet embedded a sustainability covenant: a credible, assured sustainability report, and a ring-fenced budget share directed to climate action. That promise now collided with three converging facts: eroding payment economics, a CSRD filing due within the quarter under a double-materiality lens weighing financial exposure against impact on people and the environment, and a journalist asking whether the climate spend was real or cosmetic.

Fontaine had to decide how to frame this for her board: as three separate workstreams, with payments, sustainability and reporting teams each owning one piece and reconciling only at the end; as a sequence, payment economics fixed first, the CSRD filing and the climate spend addressed once the P&L looked safe; as mainly a reporting exercise, filing the CSRD report on schedule while treating the rail and the climate budget as separate, routine operations; or as one connected system, in which the rail, the climate budget and the disclosure all had to tell the same story. She also had to name the single biggest combined exposure if things went wrong: the payment-cost swing alone was the number that decided whether the quarter succeeded or failed; a bad week of unflattering press was a cost the company could absorb and move past; each risk — cost, disclosure, press — was contained on its own and would not spread to the others; or a greenwashing exposé could trigger a regulator's marketing-claims inquiry into Solvio's public claims. Whichever way she framed it, she had to decide how tightly to hold her team to one consistent account, rather than letting each function tell its own story.

## The Company and its Setting

Solvio Pay SAS, founded in Paris in 2017, ran a payments-and-budgeting app used by 2.1 million customers across France, Spain and Germany, charging a EUR 5.99-a-month premium subscription alongside interchange and foreign-exchange revenue. It processed EUR 4.3B in annual payment volume, earned EUR 86M in FY25 net revenue, and operated on a 6% EBITDA margin — roughly EUR 5.2M — thin for a growth-stage fintech (Exhibit 1). It held an Electronic Money Institution licence supervised by the ACPR, with marketing claims within the AMF's remit. The founders had built the company's identity around being, in their words, "the bank that's good for your wallet and the planet" — a line that had helped raise the Series C and now sat next to every number Fontaine's team produced.

## The Situation

Solvio's card-scheme rail cost roughly 1.05%, blended across its card mix. An account-to-account instant-SEPA rail cost roughly 0.35% — a swing worth an estimated EUR 8M to EUR 12M a year on Solvio's volume, larger than the company's entire EBITDA line (Exhibit 2). The ring-fenced climate budget the covenant required stood at EUR 2.4M, about 2.8% of net revenue. If nothing changed before the filing deadline, the payment-cost gap would keep widening, the climate budget would still need a home, and the sustainability report would still need words for an assurance provider, a board and a journalist already working the story.

## What Fontaine Controlled

On the payment rail, three paths were on the table: staying on the card-scheme rail at roughly 1.05%, keeping the checkout flow customers knew and adding a premium "green checkout" feature; moving fully to the instant-SEPA rail at roughly 0.35%, banking the estimated EUR 9M saving in one step; or running a phased hybrid, keeping cards while migrating lower-risk segments to the new rail over time, sized as a share of volume. Alongside whichever rail she chose, she had to set the adoption-support and fraud-operations investment: nothing; a partial uplift of around EUR 1M, covering basic onboarding nudges for customers moving to the new rail; or a fully staffed fraud-operations desk with tuned authentication, at around EUR 2M. She also had to decide whether the rail's emissions implication should be modelled now and handed to sustainability, left for the reporting team to estimate at year-end, or treated as a pure cost decision with no emissions angle.

Separately, Fontaine had to split the EUR 2.4M climate budget across three uses: carbon offsets at roughly EUR 10 a tonne, fast to deploy, though the additionality of some projects — whether the reduction would have happened anyway — was already in question; measurable emissions reductions inside Solvio's own cloud and payments infrastructure, slower and costlier per tonne but a verifiable change to the company's own footprint; or a customer-facing green product, such as carbon-tracked spending or round-up climate financing, engaging users directly but overlapping with existing subscription features. Across whatever mix she chose, she also had to decide whether to target the spend at where the rail decision had added emissions, to allocate it more loosely toward whatever would read well in the report, or to treat the budget as a standalone line unconnected to the rail.

For the filing, Fontaine had to choose a disclosure posture: report the bare minimum the standards required, saying as little as the rules allowed about the offset-versus-abatement split; commit publicly to ambitious language and targets, including a net-zero date; or report the material topics with the offset-versus-abatement split disclosed and commit only to targets tied to measured baselines. She had to set how much of the compliance budget to spend on assurance: a smaller amount covering only minimum assurance, leaving some figures unverified; a mid-range amount covering limited assurance on the headline metrics only; or a larger amount funding both better underlying data and assurance across the full disclosure. When the journalist's questions came, she had to decide what to say: restate the company's founding mission, good for your wallet and the planet, without breaking out the offset-versus-abatement numbers; decline to comment on the specifics and let the filed FY26 report speak for itself; or lay out the abatement spend, the assured baseline and the rail's modelled emissions, while acknowledging what remained offset. One more question underlay the board memo: how firmly to hold that an honest, assurable disclosure earned more investor and market trust than a more impressive-sounding one.

## Closing

By 8:30 that morning, Fontaine had cleared her calendar for the week. The rail decision, the climate-budget allocation and the CSRD filing all had to reconcile into one board memo before quarter-end, and the journalist's Tuesday deadline would not move to suit Solvio's timetable. She opened a blank document and began with the number that mattered most: EUR 4.3B in volume, and a rail decision that would move more of the company's P&L than anything else she would sign.
