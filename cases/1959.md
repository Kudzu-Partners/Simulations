# Meridiano Capital: The Mandate Under Constraint

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1959 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1959 — *The CSR Portfolio* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

At 6:40 on Monday morning, March 9, 2026, Renata Solís opened the overnight risk pack at Meridiano Capital Gestores in Monterrey and found the number that mattered on the second page. The portfolio environmental, social and governance (ESG) score of the Fondo Responsable Global had printed at 62.8; on Friday it had been 66.4. An index-provider rebalance had gone through overnight and two holdings had been marked down after controversy findings. The investment management agreement Meridiano signed with the fund's client in 2023 required at least 65.

The agreement allowed ten business days to cure a breach, which put the deadline at Friday, March 20. The client's investment committee met the following Monday, March 23. Between those dates Solís had to lift the score above 65 and hand the committee a portfolio that sat inside the three other limits in the same document — and two of those three had almost nothing left in them.

## The Protagonist

Renata Solís, 43, had managed the Fondo Responsable Global since 2019, having joined Meridiano in 2014 from the investment office of a Mexican pension administrator. Two items in her record bore on the week. In 2023 she had negotiated the ESG language into the investment management agreement, telling her partners that an auditable floor would hold the relationship where a marketing claim would not. And in January she had told the client's investment committee, on a slide she had written herself, that responsibility and return were not in conflict at Meridiano. Her bonus moved with the fund's three-year information ratio and with the retention of the mandate.

## The Decision

Solís had to decide how to restore the score, what to do with the energy holdings, and what to say to the committee on March 23. The three were one decision, because the fund was one portfolio and every trade that moved one of its numbers moved the others. She had five trading days, March 9 to March 13. The agreement ranked none of the four commitments against the others: all four were absolutes, and all four had to be true of the same portfolio on the same day.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridiano Capital Gestores, S.A. de C.V., founded in Monterrey in 2009 by two former development-bank treasurers, managed MXN 38,000 million — about US$2,100 million — across institutional mandates. Its flagship was the Fondo Responsable Global, a US$900 million multi-asset sleeve run for the pension reserve of a Mexican industrial group, benchmarked to a 60/40 global equity-and-bond index and reported in US dollars. It was roughly 43 percent of the firm's assets and the anchor relationship.

The 2023 agreement had made two responsibility measures contractual rather than aspirational: a portfolio ESG score of at least 65 on a 0–100 rating scale, and weighted carbon intensity — the emissions of the holdings per unit of revenue — below the benchmark's. Meridiano's board had separately set an ex-ante tracking-error budget of 4.0 percent and an ex-ante value-at-risk limit of 3.0 percent, measured over one month at 95 percent confidence. Tracking error described how far the fund's returns were expected to drift from the index's over a year, in percentage points; the value-at-risk limit, the loss the fund was not to exceed in a month in 95 months out of 100, as a share of assets.

The desk was four people: Solís; Paulina Berdejo on ESG research and stewardship; Tomás Iriarte on risk and attribution; Julián Cifuentes on energy and commodities. Adriana Rentería, who led client relationships, sat on every call.

## The Situation

The book's position that morning was the one set out in **Exhibit 1**. The ESG score stood at 62.8 against the floor of 65; weighted carbon intensity 8 percent above the benchmark's, against a limit of below it; tracking error 3.70 percent against the 4.0 percent budget; value at risk 2.80 percent against the 3.0 percent limit. The Sharpe ratio — return per unit of total risk taken — was 0.62, and the fund was 65 basis points behind the benchmark for the year to date after fees. The quarter's realised trading cost line still read US$0.0 million.

Brent crude had risen 14 percent in eight sessions on a supply disruption, and the fund's energy holdings — 9 percent of the fund, about US$81 million of Latin-American energy equities — had risen with it. Cifuentes put the odds of the move giving back a substantial part of itself within the month at roughly even; the desk's January note had called the disruption structural.

The 65 basis points had a longer history than the week. Iriarte's first pass at attribution put most of the shortfall in the fund's underweight to energy — the sector the responsibility screen penalised and the sector that had just rallied. He could not say how much of that underweight was the screen and how much was a position the desk would have taken anyway. Rentería reported the client's committee's own view: the screen cost return, and the fund's numbers showed it. Berdejo's argument ran the other way. The two marked-down names were already under a stewardship plan with filed resolutions, and selling them would hand the vote to a holder who would file nothing.

Iriarte had a rule of thumb from the last three rebalances, recorded in **Exhibit 2** with the week's calendar: each point of score bought by selling had cost roughly a quarter of a percentage point of tracking error, because a score bought that way was bought by concentration.

If Solís traded nothing, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The score would still read 62.8 on March 20, the breach would become reportable to the client's board, carbon intensity would remain above the benchmark, and the risk lines would go into the committee meeting where they already sat. On the agenda for March 23 was a review of a US$900 million mandate.

## What Solís Controlled

She controlled the remediation route: sell the lowest-rated holdings outright, which lifted the score by a known amount as the trades settled, concentrated the book into fewer names and realised trading cost and a tax charge; keep those holdings under the stewardship plan and rebuild the weighted average by adding high-rated, benchmark-like positions, which moved the score more slowly and by an amount that depended on how much she added; or sell only the two controversy names and hold the rest under the plan, which lifted the score least and realised the smallest cost. She controlled how far above 65 to rebuild — from the floor itself to the low seventies — and how large a replacement sleeve of high-rated positions to fund: none, about 4 percent of the fund, or about 8 percent.

She controlled the energy book four ways: leave it as it stood, which kept the position behind the quarter's only strong return; sell it back to benchmark weight, which cut risk and the carbon line and realised the gain; keep the shares and overlay them with sector futures, a put spread or both, which cut the downside without selling anything, at 35 to 45 basis points of traded notional for a full overlay; or sell into lower-carbon energy-transition companies, which kept exposure to the same price driver on a lower carbon footprint. Within that choice she set how much of the exposure to neutralise, from none to all of it, and how many instruments to stack: one, two or three.

And she controlled March 23: what to put before the committee, what to claim about the screen and the 65 basis points, which two of the four commitments she would defend first if the chair made her rank them, and how firmly she would stand behind what she had said in January.

She would be judged on six numbers: the ESG score, carbon intensity against the benchmark, tracking error, value at risk, the Sharpe ratio, and the realised cost of every trade she put on, in US dollars.

## Closing

At 8:15 Solís wrote the four limits across the top of a sheet of paper, one column each, and left the order blotter empty beside it. The cure expired on Friday the 20th; the committee met on Monday the 23rd. Whatever she did to one column, she would have to defend in the other three.
