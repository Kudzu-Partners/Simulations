# Verdebuild Alliance: Forging the Standard

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1794 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1794 — *Building the Market* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 12 October 2026, Priya Anand took a call from the site manager of Speldhurst House, the mass-timber office tower meant to prove her organisation's whole case. Three weeks before completion, the project's insurer had withdrawn cover, citing the absence of any agreed fire-and-durability standard for the building system. By lunchtime a trade headline was circulating: "Green-build dream goes up in (theoretical) smoke." By the end of the week, Verdebuild Alliance's funders had made their position clear: the next £4 million tranche of support would depend on proof, within twelve months, that a genuine market for low-carbon structural materials was forming in the UK — not one flagship tower, but a working system of buyers, insurers, lenders and standards that could carry the category on its own.

Anand, Verdebuild's Ecosystem Director, had twelve months, a team of fourteen people and £6.5 million of catalytic funding to show it. She would make the case in four rounds of decisions, starting with the most basic question of all: if the materials already worked, why did the market not yet exist?

## The Protagonist

Anand, 44, had joined Verdebuild at its founding in 2024, after eleven years designing accreditation schemes for the offshore wind supply chain at a European standards body, followed by three years advising a green-infrastructure fund on how underwriters priced the risk of unfamiliar building technologies. She had taken the Ecosystem Director role on the strength of one argument to Verdebuild's board: that the barrier to low-carbon construction would never be the materials, which already performed, but the missing plumbing — the standards, certifications and risk models that let strangers trade with each other at scale. The flagship's collapse was the first test of that argument under pressure, in public, with a funding deadline attached to the answer.

## The Decision

Over four rounds, Anand had to work through the whole shape of the problem. First, she had to diagnose why the market had failed to form, and decide what kind of organisation Verdebuild actually was. Second, she had to choose which single institutional gap to close first, and divide the £6.5 million catalytic fund across the fronts competing for it. Third, she had to decide whom to bring into the coalition that would give any standard its legitimacy, and how open that standard should be. Fourth, she had to structure Verdebuild's own capital and decide what evidence of a forming market to carry into a meeting with the lead funder and a development bank's investment committee.

## The Company and its Setting

Verdebuild Alliance was a London-based, not-for-profit organisation founded in 2024 with a single purpose: to help a functioning UK market for low-carbon structural building materials — mass timber, low-clinker cement, recycled-steel systems — come into being. It employed fourteen people and held £6.5 million of catalytic funding, committed over three years by a coalition of foundations and a development bank. It owned no factories and sold no products; its relationships were with the manufacturers, developers, insurers, lenders, regulators and certifiers who would eventually need to trade with one another.

The ecosystem around it was still small and fragmented. Roughly thirty mid-tier property developers were potential buyers of low-carbon structural systems. Two major insurers and three lenders sat on the risk-and-finance side. Three rival proto-standards already circulated among manufacturers, certifiers and developers, none yet accepted widely enough to matter, and one of them backed by a single large manufacturer with the scale to push its own version hard. A regulator watched from the sidelines, able to lend a standard legitimacy but under no obligation to.

## The Situation

Verdebuild's own scorecard, going into the crisis, read as follows (Exhibit 1). Four organisations were actively participating in the low-carbon systems Verdebuild was trying to build a market around, against a twelve-month target of at least twenty-five. Standard legitimacy — a 0-to-100 reading of how credible Verdebuild's eventual technical standard was seen to be, as distinct from how quickly it had been written — stood at 22. Private capital committed to the category stood at £12 million, against a twelve-month target of at least £75 million in genuine private finance; grant money would not count toward that figure. Of the £6.5 million catalytic fund, none had yet been spent.

If those numbers did not move, the arithmetic was straightforward. Twelve months on, Verdebuild would still be short of both the participant target and the capital target, the £4 million tranche would not be released, and the only one of the three proto-standards with real momentum behind it would be the one already backed by a single large manufacturer.

## What Anand Controlled

In the first round, Anand had to name the binding constraint behind the flagship's collapse: low awareness among developers of how good the low-carbon materials already were, the technology itself needing more research before it was ready, price levels that Verdebuild might subsidise down, or the absence of a shared, insurer-accepted standard for pricing risk. Bound up with that was a second question — whether Verdebuild's actual job was to run a campaign promoting the materials, to manufacture and sell the systems itself, to fund pilot projects and absorb their losses, or to build the institutions that let other parties transact. She also had to set how rigorously her team would defend whichever diagnosis it reached, a matter of degree rather than a single choice.

In the second round, she had to decide which institutional gap to attack first — forging the shared standard, standing up an insurance product, setting up a finance facility, or moving on a standard, a certifier, insurance and finance all at once — and then divide the £6.5 million across four competing lines: standard-setting and certification design, an underwriting pilot with an insurer, coalition convening, and direct subsidy of materials to buyers (Exhibit 2). She also had to decide how strictly to treat the idea that a standard had to exist before insurance was possible, and insurance before finance would follow: sequencing the three strictly, running them in parallel, or pursuing whichever partner agrees first.

In the third round, she had to decide whom to recruit first to anchor the coalition's legitimacy: the insurer that had just walked away, the regulator, a single willing developer, or the large manufacturer behind the rival proto-standard. She had to set a stance for the standard itself — fully open and available to every developer in the ecosystem, a hybrid of an open base paired with a governed certification mark, or the large manufacturer's proprietary version, controlled solely by that manufacturer — and decide how to handle the rival proto-standard: negotiating it into a shared governance structure, out-competing it for adopters, or letting it become the default. A further decision set how widely the standard itself would be shaped together with the thirty-odd mid-tier developers, rather than written in-house and presented to them afterward.

In the fourth and final round, she had to structure Verdebuild's catalytic capital — as a first-loss layer absorbing early risk ahead of private lenders, as a top-up of further foundation grants until the £75 million figure was reached, or as money lent directly by Verdebuild into the projects themselves — and decide what evidence of a forming market to present to the funders: broad evidence of many participants joining, one anchor developer's signed agreement, or a pipeline of mostly non-binding letters of interest. She had to commit an actual private-capital figure to the investment committee, and decide how firmly to argue that catalytic money was meant to draw private finance in, rather than replace it.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 12 October, Anand opened a blank sheet and wrote down the four numbers she now had to move: four participants, a legitimacy score of 22, £12 million in private capital, and £6.5 million still unspent. Twelve months, and one meeting with the investment committee at the end of them.
