# Cláirseach Dairy: The Verified Promise

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1940 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1940 — *Supply-Chain Brand* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 3 March 2026, Aoife Ní Riain read the letter twice before carrying it down the corridor. Nordmilk Nutrition bought €242 million of Cláirseach Dairy Group's nutritional ingredients a year — 22% of the co-operative's revenue. From its 2028 sourcing year, the infant-formula group had written, it would buy only from suppliers whose farms held independently verified credentials: carbon, water and animal-welfare figures audited farm by farm against Ireland's national Origin Green programme. The audit was set for March 2028, with the bar at 95% or more of the volume Nordmilk bought.

Ní Riain, Cláirseach's marketing director, had been given €9 million by the board eleven days earlier for a national sustainability brand. The requests against it came to roughly €14 million. Four decisions stood between the letter and the audit. The first was due at Friday's executive meeting: what Cláirseach would say about itself, and what it would spend to make it true.

## The Protagonist

Ní Riain, 43, had been marketing director since 2022. She joined Cláirseach in 2016 from a branded-foods group in Dublin, where she ran export accounts for cheese and butter. Her first years in Kilkenny went into the ingredients story for buyers who bought on specification, not advertising. The grass-fed, low-carbon description of Irish dairy that opened every Cláirseach presentation was largely her wording, drafted in 2019 from national sector averages. Nobody had asked her, until the letter, to produce it farm by farm.

## The Decision

Ní Riain had to decide what Cláirseach would claim, how far and how fast to verify its farms, who would pay, what the €9 million would build, and how to defend the plan while the case behind it remained a range. The decisions ran in sequence, each closing before the next opened. Two dates governed: Nordmilk's audit in March 2028, and before it the November 2026 annual general meeting, where 2,400 farmer-owners would read the plan as an effect on milk price.

## The Company and its Setting

Cláirseach Dairy Group was founded in 1974 as a farmer-owned co-operative and remained one. From two sites in Kilkenny, 1,400 staff turned the milk of 2,400 supplier farms into butter, cheese and nutritional ingredients — the base powders and protein fractions used in infant formula and clinical nutrition. Turnover was €1.1 billion, 68% of it exported.

Nutritional ingredients carried a margin of roughly 18%; commodity butter about 7% on a much larger volume of litres. The nutritional end was also the scrutinised one: its customers audited the farms behind the specification, because their own consumer claims depended on what their suppliers could evidence. Two competitors sold into the same accounts: a New Zealand exporter and a Dutch co-operative, both with farm-level schemes years old.

## The Situation

Cláirseach's own count put 60% of its 2,400 farms in a state where an auditor could verify their numbers. The remaining 40% — about 960 farms — could not: their carbon, water and welfare data were unmeasured, incomplete, or unusable by an external auditor. Bringing one farm to that standard cost roughly €3,500, putting the whole gap at about €3.4 million. The position she inherited was set out in **Exhibit 1**.

The board tracked four measures. **Verified supply** was the share of the 2,400 farms whose numbers were audit-ready, and stood at 60%. **Brand credibility**, on a 0–100 scale, was the board's judgement of how far the claim rested on evidence rather than assertion; it opened at 12. **Buyer confidence**, also 0–100, read Nordmilk's trust in Cláirseach as a supplier; it stood at 20 and governed whether the €242 million stayed. **Projected return** was the annual value expected once premium and demand were applied to the volume; it opened at zero. Beside them sat the budget line: €0.0 million of €9.0 million.

The commercial case was a range, not a figure. Market data put the price premium on verified low-carbon dairy ingredients between 3% and 8%, and the demand response to a consumer-facing brand between 2% and 6%. On the Nordmilk volume alone, those ranges spanned a factor of nearly three (see **Exhibit 2**).

The executive argument had already formed. Padraig Nolan, supply-chain and sustainability director, held that nothing could be said in market that an auditor could not confirm on a random farm. Cormac Deasy, brand and communications manager, held that a brand needs eighteen months to register with a procurement committee, and that Nordmilk's re-tender would open well before March 2028. Mairéad Hyland, a farmer-director, put a third point in writing: 2,400 owners would read whatever was decided as a contribution to or a deduction from milk price.

If the gap stayed where it was, the arithmetic was not in dispute. Nordmilk's auditors would arrive to a farm base of which 40% could not produce its numbers, the €242 million would move to a supplier with an established scheme, and the 15% of turnover held by customers applying comparable standards would follow the signal.

## What Ní Riain Controlled

At the first decision point she set the claim Cláirseach would stand behind: the national grass-fed description applied across all 2,400 farms, as the sector had long used it; one scoped to the 60% of milk whose farms were already audited; a verified-low-carbon claim made across the whole co-operative now and evidenced as farms came through; or a dated public commitment to net-zero dairy by 2030. She named where the value at risk sat: the €242 million of Nordmilk volume and the 18% margin behind it, the commodity butter business at about 7% on the greater share of the litres, an even spread of attention across every customer and product line, or the domestic retail shopper and the home market behind it. And she set, on a nought-to-hundred scale, how candidly the team stated what the farms could and could not yet evidence.

At the second she committed the supply chain: enter all 2,400 farms into the scheme at once, verify only the number of farms the audit itself required, verify the farms supplying Nordmilk volume first and reach the rest by the deadline, or defer verification and revisit it once demand was proven. She set who paid, across three shares totalling 100% — the co-operative funding it, which protected milk price into November; each farm funding its own audit from its own accounts; or a co-operative advisory grant set against a farmer co-payment. And she committed a figure in euros from the €9 million to the gap.

At the third she built the programme. She chose the architecture: an ingredient brand for buyers such as Nordmilk, with an endorsement they could pass to their consumers; an ingredient brand for those buyers alone, with nothing aimed at the shopper; a campaign aimed at the shopper alone, with nothing aimed at the buyers; or a corporate campaign about Cláirseach the company. She chose what the brand would stand on: the sustainable, natural and trusted language common to the whole category, the lowest landed cost of any comparable supplier, the farm-by-farm audited data held in the Origin Green scheme, or the co-operative's own history since 1974. She set the euros going into the system that turned a farm's audit file into a claim an auditor would accept, and set, again on a nought-to-hundred scale, how firmly claims stayed inside what the second decision verified.

At the fourth she allocated what remained: euros to trade marketing aimed at Nordmilk and the other ingredient buyers, and euros to a consumer pull campaign. She named the scenario the plan was built to survive — the bottom of both ranges, a 3% premium and a 2% demand lift; the middle, a 5% premium and a 4% lift; or the top, an 8% premium and a 6% lift — and decided how firmly to hold, before the board and the Nordmilk buyer, that brand spend had never run ahead of verified supply.

## Closing

Ní Riain put the letter face down and wrote two columns on the pad: what Cláirseach could prove that morning, and what it would need to prove by March 2028. The Friday meeting was in three days; the annual general meeting was in November. She had €9 million, 960 farms that could not yet produce a number, and a sentence to write.
