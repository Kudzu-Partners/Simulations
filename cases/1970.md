# Karst Foods: The Signal Behind Vista's Premium

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1970 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1970 — *Brand Signals* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 2 June 2026, Nika Bregar reached the Karst Foods offices outside Ljubljana with two documents open on her phone. The first was a consumer-watchdog post, twenty-one hours old and still spreading, that set a photograph of a Vista carton beside five words: "premium prices, vague green claims." Underneath it the account had reproduced a page from the challenger brand Polje's audited sustainability report and asked its followers to find Vista's equivalent. The second was the May wave of Vista's brand tracker, delivered the previous Friday.

Before the autumn range launch and the retail review that followed it, Bregar had to decide what Vista's €14 million marketing budget would buy: how much would fund sustainability work and how much would fund the campaign that talked about it. Aleš Novak, Karst Foods' chief financial officer, had already set the test. If stated purchase intent was not back above 60% by the autumn review, finance would cut the brand's budget by 20% and reposition Vista as a mid-tier value line.

## The Protagonist

Nika Bregar, 41, had been Brand Director for Vista since 2022, after six years running the company's chilled portfolio and five years at a Ljubljana agency, where she had built her reputation on launches rather than defences. She had written the 2023 repositioning that put "clean" and "natural" on every Vista carton — the words the watchdog account had quoted back. Her bonus moved with Vista's rate of sale and with the brand's contribution, which was a function of the premium.

## The Decision

Bregar had the campaign quarter to decide what the €14 million was aimed at, which work it funded, in what order the work and the talking about it would happen, and what she would say at the board review that closed the quarter, which Novak had said would judge the plan on return rather than reach.

## The Company and its Setting

Karst Foods d.o.o., founded in 1998 and headquartered near Ljubljana, marketed Vista, a €95 million brand of plant-based dairy alternatives, snacks and bottled beverages sold in Slovenia, Croatia, Austria and northern Italy. Vista sold at an 18% premium to mainstream comparators. The premium was the company's profit engine, and it rested on buyers believing the brand was worth more.

Karst had spent years on sustainability work it had never told anyone about: regenerative sourcing agreements in the Vipava valley, a partial conversion of packaging lines, water recovery at the Postojna plant. None of it had been assembled into anything a shopper could see. The €14 million was weighted to general brand advertising; the part that touched sustainability work or its communication was small, split across four markets and three agencies, and had never been reported as a single line.

Polje had launched in 2021 with the opposite proportions: it published audited emissions and sourcing data every six months, sold at parity with mainstream brands, and had taken stated purchase intent into the high forties from the low thirties a year earlier (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Situation

Vista's tracker reported three scores. Warmth — whether buyers liked the brand and thought it meant well — stood at 72 out of 100 and had barely moved. Competence — whether buyers thought Vista made a good product — stood at 58, also near-flat. Morality — whether buyers thought the brand was honest — stood at 49, down nine points across two waves, with the fall concentrated among buyers under 35. Stated purchase intent — the share of category buyers saying they would choose Vista next time — had fallen from 62% to 55% in six months (see **Exhibit 1**).

Damjan Rus, the sales director, had brought his own number: buyers in two of the four markets had warned that Vista lines would come off the autumn planogram if rate of sale kept softening. Rus then repeated the question he had asked since March: what Vista would do about price if Polje moved.

Petra Šuštar, who ran communications, had asked a different one. Whatever claim went on the autumn pack, she wanted to know what Karst would hand over if a journalist asked to see the file behind it. The audit that produced such a file had a lead time: the certification body's process completed in month four of the campaign quarter, and the autumn campaign was scheduled for week one.

Novak had told the review what he would look at, and it was not awareness. He wanted the additional margin the plan produced set against the money it consumed — return on marketing investment, or ROMI, a ratio Karst reported for trade promotions and never for brand advertising.

The arithmetic of doing nothing was not in dispute. If the May tracker held and intent stayed at 55%, the autumn review would trigger the 20% budget cut and the repositioning, and the two delisting warnings would land on the same agenda.

## What Bregar Controlled

Four commitments stood between 2 June and the board review.

The first was a written diagnosis, filed before any money moved. She had to name which of the three tracker scores was holding intent down: whether the problem was that buyers had cooled on the brand, that they doubted the product, that they doubted the brand's honesty, or that no single score was decisive and all three should be lifted together. She had to name whose view the plan was built around: the over-45 buyers who had carried Vista for a decade, the under-35 buyers among whom the post was circulating, or the whole target base without distinction. And she had to state what had moved intent from 62% to 55%: fewer buyers hearing from Vista lately, a change in what buyers believed about Vista, or an 18% premium that had outrun what buyers would now pay.

The second was the plan. One sustainability programme would lead. Independently audited carbon and sourcing data with a certification mark meant opening supplier records to a third party and finishing in month four. Converting the remaining packaging lines to recyclable formats was capital work on Karst's own schedule, with a result visible on shelf. A community-garden and cause programme across the four markets could begin within weeks and needed no outside party. Running no new programme meant the autumn plan rested on work Karst had already done. One channel would carry it: paid advertising across television and digital, the largest reach and Vista's own voice; earned media and trade press with retail point of sale, less reach with the claim carried by journalists and retailers; or a roster of paid creators, direct reach to the under-35s with the claim carried by people Karst paid but did not employ. And she had to decide whether each line of spend was tagged to the score it was meant to move, funded as one sustainability programme without that split, or weighted toward the score Vista already led on.

The third was the money: €14 million divided between the programmes and the campaign that communicated them, in any proportion, capped in total at that figure, with anything uncommitted held in reserve. With it went a sequence — run the campaign from week one, hold it until the audit and certification landed, or run both at once.

The fourth was the defence: what Karst would produce if a journalist asked for the file behind the autumn claim — the audit itself, an argument about framing, or nothing; what Vista would do if Polje cut price — match it, hold and argue that Vista was worth more, or go below Polje; and what her board case would lead with — the reach the campaign bought, the return it produced, or the fact that the budget had been fully deployed. Novak had also asked her for a number: how much of her own credit she would stake on the position she took.

## Closing

At 8:20 the watchdog post passed nine thousand shares. Bregar printed the three tracker scores on one page and drew two columns beside them, one for what each euro would fund and one for what it was meant to change. The autumn launch was thirteen weeks out; the board review closed the quarter. She had €14 million, a morality score at 49, and a certification that finished in month four.
