# Nutrisur Envases Industriales: A Peso Business on a Dollar Supply Chain

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-372 · **Companion simulation:** Express 372 — *La Red Global — Supply Chain Architecture* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, March 9, 2026, Valeria Quiroga, Director of Operations and Supply Chain at Nutrisur Envases Industriales S.A., reread the board resolution that would define her next two years. Nutrisur, a flexible-packaging maker in Córdoba, Argentina, bought 72% of its materials in US dollars and sold 85% of its output in Argentine pesos. The board wanted the dollar share of material costs brought down to 50% within 24 months.

Quiroga had one week to bring her first answer to the operations committee: how much of the company's aluminium barrier film to keep buying from China and how much to move to a Brazilian supplier, and how many days of safety stock the plant should carry. In the same month, Nutrisur's largest customer had asked for a supply-chain resilience plan for the following year.

## The Protagonist

Quiroga, 44, was an industrial engineer who had spent eleven years in purchasing and logistics at a Córdoba auto-parts maker before joining Nutrisur in 2021. She had lived through the 2023 devaluation from the purchasing desk, rebuilding price lists every week while the plant waited on imported inputs. She reported to the CEO and worked alongside a CFO who controlled the transformation budget.

## The Decision

Quiroga faced four decisions spread across the 24 months. The first was the aluminium split and the safety-stock target. The second was a sourcing portfolio for the largest dollar-priced inputs, inside a fixed budget. The third would come whenever a crisis struck the supply lines or the currency: how to protect production and how fast to pass costs on to customers. The fourth, near the end of the period, was whether to use the supply chain to grow, and which lever to pull to close whatever gap remained to the 50% target.

## The Company and its Setting

Nutrisur made multilayer bags, stand-up pouches and polypropylene films for packaged-food companies, fruit and vegetable exporters from Argentina's northwest, and mass-market cosmetics brands. It employed 680 people. Annual revenue was ARS 98,400 million, about USD 96 million at ARS 1,025 to the dollar, and EBITDA was ARS 8,230 million, a margin of 8.4%. Its largest customer, a national food group, accounted for 18% of revenue and had run out of packaging in 2024 when Chinese aluminium film arrived late.

Materials cost ARS 48,000 million a year (see Exhibit 1). Polypropylene resin, the largest item, came from a Brazilian petrochemical group and from Asian imports, with a local producer able to cover about 30% of demand. Flexographic inks and laminating adhesives were imported from Europe, the United States and Brazil. Aluminium barrier film came from China.

Argentina ran a crawling peg that devalued the peso by about 2% a month. Inflation had fallen sharply but remained high, and analysts put the probability of a discrete jump of more than 20% in the exchange rate within eighteen months at about 35%.

## The Situation

The currency mismatch had already cost Nutrisur once. The devaluation of 2023, in which the peso lost 54% against the dollar, took the EBITDA margin from 8.4% to 2.1%. The company recovered by raising prices, but with a lag of four to five months that produced real operating losses.

Quiroga's dashboard opened at ARS 8,230 million of EBITDA, 72% of material cost in dollars, an average supplier lead time of 95 days and 38 days of safety stock. No working capital had yet been released. The CFO had set aside ARS 2,800 million, about USD 2.7 million, for the whole redesign over 24 months.

The aluminium question came first (see Exhibit 2). The Chinese supplier quoted USD 4,200 a ton with a 120-day lead time. The Brazilian alternative quoted USD 5,100 a ton, 21% more, with a 25-day lead time, lower freight and lower management overhead, but a higher defect rate. Both quoted in dollars. Buying from Brazil would not by itself change the dollar share; it would change how long Nutrisur waited for each order and how much stock it had to hold against a late ship. Stock on the floor was cash tied up; stock released was cash freed, at the price of thinner protection.

If nothing changed, nearly three-quarters of Nutrisur's material bill would stay in dollars while its sales stayed in pesos, the plant would keep waiting four months for Chinese film, and the next jump in the exchange rate would reach the margin the way the last one had.

## What Quiroga Controlled

In the first round, Quiroga would set the share of aluminium film bought from Brazil, anywhere from zero, all from China as today, to 100%, and the target safety stock, between 10 and 60 days, against 38 today.

In the second, she would build the sourcing portfolio. For polypropylene resin she could sign a long-term contract with the Brazilian supplier billed in pesos but adjusted to the exchange rate, which changed the invoice currency more than the exposure; raise the local producer's share to 60%, priced in pesos at about 12% above the international price; or invest ARS 4,200 million in backward integration, more than the entire budget. For inks she could move to the Brazilian unit of an international ink maker, billed in reais with a 30-day lead time but requiring every ink to be recertified with customers; or ask the current European supplier to hold consignment stock in Córdoba, paid for only when used, with no change of currency. For aluminium she would settle the final pattern: full migration to Brazil, or a split of 60% China and 40% Brazil.

If a crisis struck, such as a shipping disruption on the Chinese route or a discrete devaluation, she would decide how to cover an aluminium shortage: buy at emergency spot prices, ration stock to priority customers, or negotiate longer delivery dates with every customer. And she would decide how to pass on higher dollar costs: an immediate increase equal to the devaluation for every customer, a gradual adjustment over three months, or a differentiated increase in which key customers absorbed less and smaller ones more.

In the last round, she would decide whether to buy Packing del Sur, a distressed competitor in Rosario offering its client portfolio, about ARS 12,400 million of revenue at a 6% margin, and its supply contract with the same Brazilian ink maker for ARS 3,200 million, or to concentrate on finishing the transformation already under way. And she would choose the lever for the final reduction in dollar exposure: financial hedging with forward contracts, further local substitution of inputs, or indexing Nutrisur's own sales contracts to the exchange rate.

After each round Quiroga would see EBITDA in pesos, the dollar share of material cost, average lead time, days of safety stock, the budget still unspent and the working capital released. The board would score each round's decisions and, at the end, look at whether dollar exposure had reached 50% without giving up the margin.

## Closing

Quiroga printed the two aluminium quotes and laid them side by side on her desk. The price gap sat on the first line; the lead times sat four lines below. The customer's request for a resilience plan was clipped to the back, and the operations committee met the following Monday.
