# Biscayne Executive Learning: The MeridianHealth Audition

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1935 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1935 — *Custom Cohort* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, March 9, 2026, Ximena Ocampo wrote four numbers on the whiteboard behind her desk and left them there: 11, 4, 280, 8. Eleven topics MeridianHealth wanted taught. Four days of classroom time it would release its people for. Two hundred and eighty thousand US dollars, the ceiling. Eight weeks to the first session. At Biscayne Executive Learning's standard rates the eleven topics costed at roughly US$410,000 and took fourteen weeks to design.

The brief had arrived before six that morning from Yolanda Prieto, chief human resources officer of MeridianHealth, and it ran to a single page. Prieto wanted a proposal in her hands on March 27 and the first cohort teaching on May 4. Ocampo would present it herself, and she would have three minutes before the questions started.

## The Protagonist

Ximena Ocampo, 44, had led Custom Programs at Biscayne Executive Learning (BEL) since 2019, having joined in 2012 as a learning designer after six years running management development in a Miami logistics group. Colleagues quoted her rule back at her: she would not sell a program she could not staff. Twice she had broken it, and both times the client had been delighted and the engagement lost money. Her compensation moved with the unit's contribution margin and with the share of accounts that renewed.

## The Decision

Ocampo had to decide what MeridianHealth was actually buying, what BEL would build for it and what it would not, what to charge, and what she would say on March 27 when Prieto pushed back on each in turn.

## The Company and its Setting

BEL was the self-funded custom-programs unit of a large urban public university's business school in Miami, Florida. It built tailored leadership programs for corporate, government and healthcare clients across Florida, Latin America and the Caribbean — roughly 60 custom cohorts a year. It billed US$14 million in 2025 at a 28% contribution margin after faculty fees, instructional design and delivery costs.

Two facts governed how Ocampo priced. First, 64% of BEL's revenue came from clients who had bought before, and no engagement went out below a 25% contribution margin, because bespoke design was paid for out of the margin on the job that commissioned it. Second, MeridianHealth was evaluating two other providers; the one that mattered was Ardmore Learning Group, a national firm quoting US$240,000 for a finished product, the Healthcare Leadership Academy.

## The Situation

MeridianHealth was a 9,000-employee hospital network and BEL's second-largest account, worth US$1.9 million over three years (see **Exhibit 1**). Its master-services agreement came up for renewal in the fourth quarter of 2026: five years, US$3.2 million. Prieto had told the business school's dean that this cohort was the audition.

The cohort was 48 clinical managers promoted within the past year — nurse managers, lab supervisors, a few physician leads — none of whom had managed people before. MeridianHealth's talent team had surveyed them in February and sent the results with the brief; Prieto had read the same survey, and her conclusion was the eleven-topic list.

The constraints were not compatible. Four classroom days across eleven topics came to about thirty-five minutes each. The US$280,000 ceiling was US$5,833 a participant against a US$410,000 build. Eight weeks was BEL's fourteen-week design-to-launch cycle with six weeks removed — and three of the five faculty the brief named were committed elsewhere for most of it, including Ramón Alvarez, named by the client for his healthcare-leadership work and in Singapore until Week 7.

Ocampo's account team kept four readings on a live proposal. On March 9, client fit — how far the client judged a program built for it rather than adapted from the catalogue — stood at 20 out of 100; renewal probability, the chance the agreement was re-signed in Q4, at 30; deliverability, whether the program as drawn could launch on the promised date inside the client's constraints, at 25. Contribution margin — price less delivery cost, over price — had no value yet, because there was no price.

If Ocampo built the brief as written, the arithmetic was already on the page: US$410,000 of delivery against a US$280,000 ceiling, launched six weeks before it was designed, taught by faculty who were not in the country. If she did not bid, the only proposal on Prieto's desk would be Ardmore's at US$240,000, and the Q4 renewal conversation would open from there.

## What Ocampo Controlled

She began with what the brief was about. Four explanations were available, each producing a different program: that the managers lacked subject knowledge and needed the eleven topics taught in the order the brief listed them; that the eleven topics were a certification requirement and every manager had to be signed off on all of them; that Prieto wanted a comprehensive academy she could show her board at the end of the fiscal year; or that clinicians promoted for clinical excellence were failing at the parts of the job that had nothing to do with it. Against whichever explanation she took, she had to rule on the eleven topics (see **Exhibit 2**) — build all eleven, carry seven or eight, or keep three or four and put the rest out of scope — and decide how much of the fortnight to spend putting questions back to MeridianHealth before she drew anything, from designing straight off the page to a full re-intake with Prieto, her nursing vice-presidents and a sample of the 48.

Then the shape of the program. She could put three days in a classroom and carry the rest through virtual sessions, coaching circles and ward assignments; run six classroom days back to back as an immersive block; or run one classroom day and deliver almost everything else online. She had to commit to a number of classroom days against the four the client would release. She had to decide how much was written from nothing: everything built new, nothing built new with the catalogue reused as it stood, or a few modules built new and the rest drawn from stock. And she had to close the faculty gap: hold the launch until the named roster came free in Week 7; staff the whole program from the unit's associate bench, at the lowest delivery cost; or anchor it with available senior faculty and an outside healthcare practitioner, Alvarez's module in a later week.

Then the money. She could hold near the US$280,000 ceiling and name what the difference bought; quote level with Ardmore at US$240,000 and win on the number; quote US$215,000 and sit below Ardmore; or propose a paid first phase with the rest contracted at renewal. She had to name a figure. When the arithmetic did not close she could take cost out of the parts the client never saw — logistics, room hire, the seniority mix on each module; add something chargeable, such as coaching circles or hospital delivery; drop one of the modules she had decided to keep; or leave the design alone and accept a thinner margin. She also set how much teaching happened at MeridianHealth's sites rather than on campus, from none to all: hospital delivery cost more and was the only way some of the 48, on rotating shifts, would attend.

Then March 27. Asked why topics she had requested were missing, Ocampo could tie each kept module to manager retention and patient safety; offer to put the dropped ones back inside the four days; or say the budget had not stretched to them. On the US$40,000 gap to Ardmore she could name in one sentence what the difference bought; match Ardmore's number at the table; or rest the answer on the school's name. On the eight weeks she could commit to dated milestones with named owners, reconciled against build times; to the full program on the promised date, the build to follow; or to no dates, the schedule set as the work went. And she had to settle, before she walked in, how much of the first cohort's margin she was prepared to give up to win the five-year agreement, with the unit's 25% floor underneath her.

## Closing

At 7:20 Ocampo photographed the whiteboard, sent it to her four program directors without a message, and opened the participant survey. Eighteen days to the proposal. Fifty-six to the room.
