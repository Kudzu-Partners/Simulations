# Sari Wangi Nusantara: Guarding the Warungs

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1832 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1832 — *Competing with Giants* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 4 May 2026, in Surabaya, Ratna Halim, chief executive of Sari Wangi Nusantara (SWN), read the notice her commercial team had forwarded overnight: Continental Brands, a consumer-goods company with roughly USD 70 billion in worldwide revenue, had signed a national modern-trade listing agreement in Indonesia and would enter SWN's core laundry and haircare categories at full scale. Continental had committed USD 120 million, about Rp1.9 trillion, to an eighteen-month launch and had priced its mass-market laundry brand twelve percent below SWN's own. The modern-trade shelves would go live in 120 days. Halim had until then to decide how SWN would meet a rival that outspent her company's entire marketing budget roughly six to one.

## The Protagonist

Ratna Halim, 44, had run Sari Wangi Nusantara since 2019, the third generation of the Halim family to lead the company her grandfather had founded in 1989 out of a single soap workshop in Surabaya. Trained as a chemical engineer, she had spent a decade rotating through the company's regional sales offices before taking the top job, years she credited with teaching her that SWN's real asset was not any formula but the relationships its reps carried into 480,000 warungs, the small family-run kiosks where most Indonesians bought their soap and detergent. SWN answered to a family-shareholder board spanning three branches of the Halim family, several of whom had spent careers inside the business and none of whom had ever managed a fight with a global multinational.

## The Decision

Halim had to decide, over the months before and after Continental's launch, how Sari Wangi Nusantara would compete against a company many times its size. The choice began with diagnosis: where, category by category, Continental's global scale actually threatened SWN, and which of SWN's own assets — its warung relationships, its advertising, its balance sheet, its research capability — a company of Continental's size genuinely could not copy. It continued into commitment: a competitive posture for the business, a pricing response in core laundry, and a choice of what to lean on in defending it. It ran through the hardest trade-off of all: how to split a fixed, non-renewable Rp350 billion across everything the company might want to fund at once, and what to do about a lucrative offer arriving from Continental itself. And it ended, eighteen months out, in a pitch to her own board about what Sari Wangi Nusantara should become next.

## The Company and its Setting

Sari Wangi Nusantara made laundry detergent, dishwashing liquid, bar soap and a line of haircare built for Indonesian water and hair, from plants in Surabaya, Medan and Makassar. Annual revenue was Rp4.2 trillion, about USD 265 million, at a 13% EBITDA margin (see Exhibit 1). The company held 34% national volume share in laundry, first in the category, and reached the market through 38 regional distributors and a fleet of motorbike sales representatives who visited warung owners weekly, extended informal credit, and knew most of them by name. Continental, by contrast, could bring far greater scale to bear on almost every other dimension of the fight.

## The Situation

Continental's stated ambition was 15% of the national laundry market within eighteen months. SWN's own commercial analysts had a rougher estimate for what inaction would cost: something like 8 to 12 share points in laundry, at roughly Rp95 billion of annual revenue per point (see Exhibit 2). Matching Continental's twelve-percent price cut across the portfolio, an option several board members had already raised, would pull EBITDA from 13% toward something closer to 6% — on the order of Rp290 billion of profit erased in the process. Supermarkets and minimarkets, the fastest-growing retail channel, were separately pressing SWN for higher slotting fees and threatening to drop its slower-moving products to make shelf room for Continental. Continental's recruiters had also approached SWN's best regional sales managers with packages roughly 40% above market. If Sari Wangi Nusantara did nothing different from what it had always done, the likely outcome was not sudden collapse but erosion: a slow bleed of share, margin and the sales talent who carried the warung relationships that were the company's real advantage.

## What Halim Controlled

Halim's first move was diagnostic rather than financial: naming, honestly, where Continental's scale would actually decide the fight — the premium tier and modern trade, every channel equally, the warungs themselves, or nowhere serious at all — and naming which SWN asset a company like Continental genuinely could not copy: the warung relationships and local product fit, the brand advertising, the balance sheet, or the underlying research capability. Attached to that diagnosis was a dial, from zero to a hundred, for how candidly she and the board would name the ground SWN stood to lose rather than insist on defending everywhere at once.

From there came commitment. Halim had to set a primary competitive posture for the business — fortify the warung-strong core and sidestep the premium tier; hold every category and every product line at full strength; meet Continental head-on with SWN's own marketing and innovation; or concede laundry and haircare and manage the business for cash. Paired with the posture was a pricing decision in core laundry: hold price and compete on local trust and access, respond only on a handful of the most exposed products, or match Continental's twelve-percent cut across the board. And paired with both was a choice of principal weapon: the warung network and local product development, a television and modern-trade marketing push, or deep promotional discounting to hold shelf space.

The hardest arithmetic came next. Halim had a fixed Rp350 billion, no possibility of a top-up over the eighteen months, and four competing claims on it: protecting warung distribution through rep coverage, credit terms and trade incentives; upgrading local product formulation and research; defending a foothold in modern trade through slotting fees and key accounts; and retaining the regional sales managers Continental was recruiting away. Every rupiah placed against one claim was a rupiah unavailable to another. Layered onto that allocation was a separate decision: Continental's relationship manager had privately proposed a co-distribution arrangement, carrying Continental's own products through SWN's warung network for a share of the margin. Halim could decline it outright, accept a narrow version limited to non-competing products and bounded in time, or accept it in full across all 480,000 warungs.

Eighteen months on, with the market's verdict in, Halim's last set of choices concerned what came next: whether to extend SWN's playbook into adjacent product categories and neighbouring Southeast Asian markets, compete directly with Continental in premium modern trade, or hold the existing core with no attempt at growth; whether to build the local advantage into the company's systems, succession planning and staff retention, continue relying on the founding family's personal relationships as it always had, or make no structural change at all; and what headline to bring to the family board — an unvarnished account of what had been defended and at what cost, a claim of victory built on having matched Continental's price cut, or a recommendation to sell the company to Continental while its brand still carried value. A final dial asked how firmly she would stand behind competing on local advantage rather than Continental's scale, expressed as a percentage from zero to a hundred.

## Closing

By the time Halim's assistant brought in a second cup of coffee, the wire alert about Continental's launch was still open on her screen beside a blank sheet on which she had started, and abandoned, three different one-page plans. She had Rp350 billion, a warung network Continental could not buy, and 120 days before the modern-trade shelves went live.
