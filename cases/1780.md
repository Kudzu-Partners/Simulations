# Cadence Labs: The Focus Decision

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1780 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1780 — *Strategic Roadmap — Cadence Labs Scale-Up* · v1.0 · September 2026

On the morning of 6 October 2026, Meera Ashworth reread her own notes from the board meeting the night before and found she had underlined one sentence three times: trying to win everywhere is how scale-ups run out of money. The lead investor's slide had shown Cadence Labs' growth rate bending downward while its monthly cash burn held flat — the shape, he said, of a company spread across too many half-finished bets. She had six weeks before the board met again, and inside that window a resourcing decision she could not put off a second time.

## The Protagonist

Ashworth, 34, had co-founded Cadence Labs in 2019 after four years inside a mid-market logistics operator, watching dispatchers reconcile the same shipment across six spreadsheets. She had no engineering background; her degree was in operations research, and the company's first eighteen months of sales calls had all been hers. She still ran the Monday pipeline review herself, a habit her board had once called a strength and now called a bottleneck. Her equity, like her three co-founders', vested against the company surviving to a Series C, not against any one growth story succeeding.

## The Decision

Ashworth had to commit, within the six-week window, to a single strategic question that would anchor everything else Cadence did for the next four quarters — and then to a resourcing split, an explicit call on the company's AI ambitions, and an order of execution that made the question real rather than aspirational. She could not answer all four separately; each one constrained the others, and the board had made clear it would judge the whole package, not the intentions behind it.

## The Company and Its Setting

Cadence Labs Ltd., headquartered in London with a smaller engineering team in Lisbon and a support office in Manchester, sold a workflow-automation platform to mid-market logistics companies. Four years after launch it employed 190 people and had reached £14 million in annual recurring revenue (ARR), growing 55% year on year — the number that had first attracted its £28 million Series B. But the growth was decelerating while three separate initiatives drew on the same finite pool of engineers, salespeople, and capital: a push upmarket into enterprise logistics accounts, an expansion into two adjacent verticals, and a new AI-driven analytics module. None of the three had been resourced enough to succeed outright, and none had been stopped.

## The Situation

The evidence of strain was concrete. Net revenue retention — the measure of whether existing customers were expanding or shrinking their spend — had fallen from 118% to 104%, meaning the base was still growing but barely; below 100% it would start shrinking outright. The average enterprise sales cycle had doubled from 75 to 150 days as an underfunded sales motion competed against two well-capitalised incumbents. Cash stood at £19.8 million against a monthly burn of £1.1 million, roughly eighteen months of runway (see **Exhibit 1**), and the Series C the company would need to raise assumed a credible path to £40 million in ARR. Six to nine months would be needed to close that round once talks began, which meant Ashworth's board wanted at least fourteen months of runway left at all times as a working floor — burn through that line and the company would be raising from weakness rather than strength.

A second clock was running alongside the cash. A larger competitor had just announced its own AI analytics module, and Cadence had roughly two quarters before the market judged it a follower rather than a peer. Two reference customers, worth £1.4 million of ARR between them, had told the account team they were holding their renewals until Cadence said, one way or another, what it intended to do about AI.

The same set of facts supported five different diagnoses of what actually mattered, and the leadership team split roughly along those lines. Owen Baptiste, VP of Sales & Growth, argued the enterprise motion needed proper resourcing before a better-capitalised rival closed the gap for good. Naomi Sarkis, VP of Product, argued the AI module was the fight that would define the category within eighteen months, competitor announcement or not. A third view, gaining ground among the account managers who covered the two new verticals, held that manufacturing and retail were the only source of growth left outside an already-saturated logistics base. Grace Lindqvist, the CFO, kept returning to the retention numbers, arguing that the leak had to close before anything built on top of it. And a fifth possibility sat underneath all three, needing no campaign because it was already happening: advancing every front together, on the logic that no one had yet forced a stop.

## What Ashworth Controlled

Four decisions were hers to make together, and each constrained the others. First, the single strategic question the roadmap would answer — committing to one of the five diagnoses above, rather than a list that tried to honor all of them at once. Second, an allocation of exactly 100 "focus points" — the engineering, sales, and capital capacity available for the next four quarters — across the four bets and a reserve she could choose to hold back or spend, with the bets alone asking for more capacity than she actually had (see **Exhibit 2**). Third, a direct call on the AI module itself: tell customers plainly that Cadence would not lead there, build a leaner version and ship a fast second, leave the question open for now, or fund it properly and race the competitor to market. Fourth, the order in which the kept moves would run across the next four quarters: phase the moves out across all four quarters, launch every move in the first quarter and bet on speed, or stabilise the existing base before expanding into anything new.

Whatever she chose would be visible within two quarters in four places: the ARR trajectory toward £40 million, the net revenue retention number, the monthly burn rate, and the runway it left behind. If she changed nothing about how the three bets were funded, the board's chart would keep bending the way it already was — slower growth, flat burn, a retention line drifting toward the point where the customer base stopped growing on its own.

## Closing

At 7:50 the next morning, Ashworth opened a blank page headed "Q4 2026 Roadmap" and looked at the four decisions she had to turn in as one package: a question, a split of 100 points across bets that wanted 165, a call on the AI module the competitor had already made for its own company, and an order of execution. The board meeting was six weeks out. She had one page, four decisions, and no more time to keep all three bets alive at once.
