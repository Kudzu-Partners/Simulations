# Méridian Learning Studio: Building the Flagship

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1803 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1803 — *Designing the Learning Journey* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 6 January 2026, Camille Rousseau opened three documents on her desk at Méridian Learning Studio and found they described the same course from three different directions. The first was five years of completion data for the school's executive certificates, showing a median finish rate of 61% and a cliff between weeks three and five where a third of any cohort quietly stopped logging in. The second was a one-page brief from the Dean: a new flagship certificate, "Leading Transformation," priced at €6,900 a seat for a 120-strong cohort of senior managers, already announced to the school's board as the centrepiece of the October 2026 EQUIS re-accreditation visit. The third was three internal memos — from the faculty lead, the digital learning producer, and the finance and operations manager — each proposing a different design for the same course, each convinced the other two were wrong.

Rousseau, the Studio's newly appointed Programme Director, had fourteen weeks to launch the certificate, a fixed design-and-production budget of €540,000, and forty-seven of the hundred and twenty seats already sold. Her first task, before anything else, was to decide why Méridian's executives really stopped showing up in week four — and which, if any, of three feuding proposals actually fixed it.

## The Protagonist

Rousseau had spent eleven years designing corporate and executive-education courses before Méridian's Vice-Dean for Innovation recruited her to lead the Studio's flagship work two years earlier. Trained originally as a cognitive scientist before moving into instructional design, she had shipped four open-enrolment certificates for the Studio since joining — each profitable, none as visible as this one. Like every designer at the Studio, her annual bonus was tied to a measured learning-outcome-attainment score, not to how many learners finished a course; the Vice-Dean had introduced that policy specifically to stop the Studio from shipping, as she put it, content people finished but did not learn from. This was the first programme the Dean had personally sold to the board with Rousseau's name on it.

## The Decision

Over the coming months, Rousseau would have to commit, in turn, to a diagnosis of what was actually breaking engagement in the Studio's certificates, a costed design for how "Leading Transformation" would be delivered, a week-by-week sequence for how its learners would be assessed, and — once a pilot cohort had actually gone through the course — a revised plan for the full 120-seat launch. Each commitment would be made inside the same €540,000 envelope and against the same fourteen-week clock.

## The Company and its Setting

Méridian Learning Studio was the in-house course-design unit of a private grande école on the French Riviera, reconstituted in 2021 from what had been a smaller teaching-and-learning centre founded in 2014. It ran a team of thirty-four instructional designers, media producers, learning technologists and analysts on an annual production budget of €4.8 million, and had shipped twenty-eight courses to just over eleven thousand learners the previous year (Exhibit 1). Its open-enrolment portfolio brought in €9.6 million in tuition at a 31% contribution margin, and its corporate clients renewed at a 78% rate. Among its French peers, the Studio was known for one unusual policy: designers were paid on whether learners demonstrably learned, not on whether they merely finished.

## The Situation

"Leading Transformation" would sell for €6,900 a seat against a 120-seat target — roughly €828,000 in tuition — but the budget Rousseau had to design and produce it with was fixed at €540,000, and the clock had started the day marketing opened enrolment. Forty-seven seats were already sold.

Four numbers described where the course stood before she made a single decision. Projected completion — the share of a cohort expected to finish all ten weeks — sat at 61%, matching the median for every executive certificate the Studio had shipped before; the board wanted it above 85%, and anything below 70% in the pilot would be treated as an outright failure of the design. Outcome attainment — a 0-to-5 score for how much learners actually demonstrated they had learned, the number her bonus rode on — stood at 2.5. Engagement quality, a 0-to-100 score meant to capture relevance, momentum and social accountability rather than surface polish, stood at 30. Cost per learner — the design budget divided across the 120-seat cohort — was €0, because nothing had yet been committed.

If she changed nothing, the new flagship would simply repeat the pattern already in the data: a three-in-five finish rate, a cliff in the same three weeks, and outcome scores no better than the certificates already on the shelf — offered to the accreditation panel as the school's flagship example of learner-centred design.

The three memos on her desk could not all be funded. The faculty lead wanted live in-person residentials; the digital learning producer wanted a fully self-paced online build; the finance and operations manager wanted whichever mix protected margin. Added together, what the three of them were asking for came to €880,000 — considerably more than the €540,000 Rousseau actually had (Exhibit 2).

Before she could touch that budget, she had to settle a diagnosis. The twenty-four-point gap between the historical completion rate and the board's target might be a design problem — courses that felt like lectures, with no sense of momentum or social accountability by week four; a content problem — material that was not yet deep or good enough; a production problem — video and slides that were not premium enough; or a fit problem — senior managers with too little time to spare for a ten-week course. Separately, she had to name the lever she believed truly changed how engaged learners became: relevance, a visible sense of progress and social accountability; broadcast-quality production and a more polished platform; a wider menu of optional extra readings and webinars; or the belief that motivation was fixed before the course began, whatever Rousseau built. And she had to decide how much of the coming design to concentrate on the historical weeks three to five, against spreading the same attention evenly across all ten.

## What Rousseau Controlled

Once she had filed a diagnosis, Rousseau would allocate €540,000 across a menu of formats: one two-day in-person residential, two of them bookending the course, or none at all, at €95,000 plus €1,100 per learner in travel apiece; some number of live, synchronous online sessions across the ten weeks, at €6,000 each; a self-paced asynchronous backbone built as a full replacement for live contact at €90,000, a leaner €60,000 layer, or a fuller €120,000 structure meant to support rather than replace the live sessions; and an investment in a peer learning-circle and cohort-milestone layer meant to build social accountability among time-poor executives (Exhibit 2).

Later, she would sequence ten weeks of assessment: how many low-stakes formative checks to author at €1,800 apiece; whether to place them at the historical drop-off window, spread one per week, or back-loaded near the end; whether to commission a sponsor-visible capstone at €22,000 alongside the formative checks, instead of them, or in place of a single high-stakes final exam; and how rigorously each assessment needed to evidence a stated learning outcome for the accreditation panel.

Finally, once a pilot cohort had run the course and the telemetry came back, Rousseau would have to decide whether the gap between projection and outcome was a design fault, an unlucky cohort, or something about this audience that no course could fix; where to send the next euro for the full 120-seat launch — toward whatever the diagnosis pointed to, toward more in-person contact regardless, or by leaving the design as it was; whether to protect learning outcomes by engineering engagement and outcomes together, by pushing past the board's 85% completion bar before addressing outcome attainment, or by tightening rigour and a harder final assessment; and how firmly to stand behind her own account of what had gone wrong when she pitched the revised design to the Vice-Dean.

## Closing

Rousseau pulled a blank sheet toward her, wrote "Weeks 3–5" at the top, and underneath it began listing everything in the persona research, the completion curve, and the three memos that might explain why Méridian's executives really stopped showing up.
