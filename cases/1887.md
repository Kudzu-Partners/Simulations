# Lumera Cloud: The Price of Candour

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1887 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1887 — *Learning Loop* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 3 June 2026, Priya Anand sat through the fourth sprint retro of the quarter and heard two of her senior engineers say, almost in the same breath, what the last engagement survey had already told her in numbers: nobody on Atlas gave real feedback anymore, because every piece of it eventually landed in a score that decided someone's pay. Atlas had just missed its third consecutive sprint commitment, delaying a feature two of Lumera Cloud's largest customers were waiting on. Anand had one quarter — a ten-week delivery window — to turn the squad around without losing a single engineer from it.

Her VP of Engineering had given her the mandate that afternoon: diagnose what had actually broken, rebuild it, and do both without switching off the very system many people in the room blamed.

## The Protagonist

Priya Anand, 38, had led the Atlas squad since it was assembled around Lumera's core observability engine, and had a reputation inside the company for shipping calmly under pressure. She had never run a turnaround where the thing working against her was a platform the company itself had built and funded. Her own standing that quarter, like every team lead's, would rest partly on whether Atlas hit its delivery numbers and partly on a trust score she had not designed and could not personally reset.

## The Decision

Anand's first job was to explain, to herself and then to her VP, why a system built to increase feedback had instead made it disappear. She could read the collapse four ways: that the plain question "how am I doing?" had grown costly to ask, since the answer fed straight into a pay score; that the squad had too few structured places left to give or receive feedback at all; that Atlas's engineers, once the most disciplined squad in the building, had quietly stopped holding one another to a standard; or that the dashboards themselves were tracking the wrong things entirely, picking up noise rather than any real signal. Whichever cause she named, a second call followed it: whether only the scoring system needed to change, with the culture following automatically behind it; whether culture and scoring were two distinct systems, each broken in its own way and each needing its own repair; whether only the culture needed attention, through a renewed conversation about candour, regardless of what the algorithm measured; or whether culture and scoring were the same fight, so that fixing what the platform measured would fix the culture too. She would also have to attach a number to her own conviction — how sure she was of that diagnosis before she acted on it, somewhere between a guess and a certainty.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumera Cloud Pty Ltd was a Sydney-based B2B SaaS provider with 410 staff, including 240 engineers organised into 22 squads, and annual recurring revenue of roughly A$96 million at a 78% gross margin. The company had closed a Series C round in 2024 that came with a board mandate to reach cash-flow break-even within eight quarters. Some time after that raise, Lumera had rolled out PulseHR, a platform that scored every engineer weekly on delivery velocity, code-review turnaround and peer recognition, and fed those scores into quarterly pay calibration. It had been sold internally as a way to make performance conversations continuous and objective. The board had paid A$1.1 million for it and was not going to write that off because one squad was struggling.

## The Situation

By the numbers Anand had in front of her that morning (see Exhibit 1), voluntary feedback-seeking — an engineer asking a peer or a lead "how am I doing on this?" — had fallen from roughly 14 times per engineer each quarter, before PulseHR, to 3. The engagement survey's item on trust in how performance was evaluated sat at 34 out of 100. Work that had been marked "done" and was later reopened for rework had gone from 9% of stories to 22%, and the time a sprint took to complete now varied twice as much as it used to; together, the team's internal readout put its capacity to adapt under pressure at 38 out of 100. Atlas's own morale index had slipped to 52. An internal delivery-risk reading, on a scale where lower was safer, stood at 70 out of 100 — because a fourth missed sprint inside the ten-week window would trigger an A$540,000 service-credit clause with the two customers, worth A$7.2 million in combined revenue, whose feature was already late. Losing one more senior engineer to frustration would cost roughly A$180,000 to replace. If none of it changed, the fourth miss would arrive on schedule, the credit would be paid, and the two customers watching the delay would be listening to a team that could no longer give them, or itself, a straight answer about its own progress.

## What Anand Controlled

Beyond the diagnosis, four rounds of choices awaited her across the quarter (see Exhibit 2). She had six engineer-hours per person per week to spend on anything that was not shipping code, divided across five possible channels: structured, named peer feedback exchanged on a regular cycle; a redesigned sprint retro; a written after-action review for each engineer; an anonymous channel for feedback aimed upward at her; and a loop that carried customer signal back into the team's own work. However she split those six hours, she still had to decide what became of whatever the team learned inside them: whether it reached her only once, at the next quarterly calibration; whether it stayed in a private journal that nobody else ever read; or whether every reflection produced one concrete change carried straight into the following sprint.

PulseHR itself she could not remove, only retune. She could leave velocity, review-latency and peer-recognition exactly as they were, all three at full weight; add further signals, since the finance team wanted more granularity for its own reporting; soften the two signals most associated with the gaming, and leave peer-recognition as an informal input only; or retire the platform's scoring altogether, so none of its signals carried any weight at all. Underneath that sat two further calls. Scores could stay attached to the individual, or move to the squad as a whole. And development and pay could be split apart entirely, each running on its own evidence; or one score could go on serving both a development conversation and quarterly pay; or pay alone could use it, with development conversations handled on separate evidence. She would also decide how much of their own PulseHR data engineers were allowed to see and contest, on a scale from almost none to nearly all of it.

For the ninety days that followed, she would anchor the recovery dashboard on one number: sprint velocity, the number the board had been watching from the start; feedback-seeking and trust, which tend to shift a few weeks ahead of delivery; or on-time delivery, which only moves once the sprint itself resolves. She would set the condition at a fourth missed sprint and nothing earlier; commit fully, with no stated condition for changing course at all; or set the condition at the trust score failing to move by week six. And she would decide how far whatever worked at Atlas travelled: keep the redesign inside Atlas alone, indefinitely, extending it no further; pilot it with a handful of volunteer squads and measure before extending it; or mandate it across all 22 squads, effective the following Monday morning. Her own conviction in that pitch, too, she would have to state as a number, not a feeling.

## Closing

Anand closed her laptop after the retro, opened a blank page, and wrote the date at the top: 3 June 2026. Ten weeks remained on the delivery window. Three sprints were already gone. She had a VP expecting a diagnosis by the end of the week, a platform she was not allowed to switch off, and a squad that had just told her, out loud, why it had stopped being honest with her.
