## Abstract

Cooperativa Cafetalera y Cacaotera del Altiplano (COCACAP), a 640-member smallholder cooperative in the Huehuetenango highlands of Guatemala, entered 2026 with USD 42,000 in cash, an average SCA cup score of 79.2 (specialty threshold 80.0), and a sales mix of 94% commodity-grade, 6% specialty. The "C" coffee futures market had traded between 160 and 305 cents/lb over the preceding twenty-four months, and the Guatemalan highland harvest was exposed to both a roya (coffee leaf-rust) outbreak that had re-emerged in January 2026 and to the arrival of a weak El Niño forecast for the Q3 harvest window. The cooperative's gerente general, Don Mateo Ramírez, had been given a board mandate for a five-year transformation of COCACAP from commodity-dependent production into specialty markets. Year 1 would spend at most USD 120,000 across four levers: quality investment, certification, hedging, and buyer-relationship development. The case places Ramírez at the cooperative's Año Cafetalero planning meeting and presents three internally coherent Year-1 plans from three respected voices.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate capital-allocation decisions in a low-resource smallholder cooperative under commodity-price volatility.
2. Analyze the interaction between cup-score thresholds, certification portfolios, and end-buyer pricing.
3. Design a five-year transformation plan that balances near-term cash preservation with long-run optionality.
4. Diagnose the role of hedging and buyer diversification in managing price and climate risk.
5. Recommend a Year-1 plan that preserves member trust while moving the cooperative's mix toward specialty.

On the evening of **March 18, 2026**, at the end of the first full week of the Año Cafetalero, **Don Mateo Ramírez** sat at the long wooden table in the COCACAP general assembly hall in San Pedro Necta, Huehuetenango, and, by the light of a single overhead fluorescent, read for the fourth time the one-page planning summary his daughter Lucía, the cooperative's accountant, had prepared. The summary carried three numbers on three lines. *Caja: USD 42,000. Puntaje taza promedio: 79.2. Precio C: 232.4.* Behind him, stacked on a smaller table, sat three proposals.

---

## The Protagonist

Don Mateo Ramírez, 58, had been gerente general of COCACAP for nine years. Before assuming the role he had worked thirty years in his own parcel of coffee and cacao in Todos Santos Cuchumatán, learning his trade from his father and his father's father. He had been elected gerente twice and re-elected, uncontested, at the 2023 general assembly. His formal education ended at the Instituto de Cooperativas in Quetzaltenango; his practical education, as he had said in the opening of the most recent assembly, was "el cultivo, el secadero, y la pala." His compensation was a fixed salary set annually by the board and a small commission on any margin realized above the commodity reference.

His mandate from the assembly on February 12, 2026 was unambiguous: *De la finca al mundo.* From the farm to the world. In five harvests.

---

## The Decision

By **March 25, 2026**, Ramírez had to table, for a binding member vote, a Year-1 allocation of at most USD 120,000 across four levers:

1. **Quality investment** — micro-mill upgrades, fermentation tanks, raised beds, moisture meters, and quality training.
2. **Certification** — among Fair Trade, Rainforest Alliance, Organic, and Café Practices (Starbucks C.A.F.E.), with variable fee and audit structures and variable buyer reach.
3. **Hedging** — cooperative-level forward sales on the "C" market, at various ratios of production, through the cooperative's bank or a specialized export partner.
4. **Buyer-relationship development** — specialty-buyer visits, Specialty Coffee Association cupping submissions, auction lot preparation, and direct-trade contract negotiation.

In addition Ramírez would declare a strategic stance: *Ahorrador (conservative), Transformador (balanced), Arriesgado (bold).* The board would vote the combination up or down as a single package.

---

## Cooperative Background

COCACAP had been founded in 1987 with 112 founding members, as part of the Guatemalan cooperative movement that had gathered smallholder producers across the Huehuetenango highlands into collective processing, marketing, and credit structures. By 2025 the cooperative had 640 active members, an average parcel size of 1.8 hectares, a combined annual production of approximately 22,000 quintals of parchment coffee and 960 quintals of cacao, and an ownership structure of proportional rights based on multi-year average delivery.

Revenue for 2025 had been USD 6.1M, of which 94% derived from commodity-grade sales into a single export buyer (ExporCafé Centroamérica) at the "C" market reference plus a modest Guatemalan-highland differential. Specialty sales, representing 6%, had been placed through three relationships: a Copenhagen specialty roaster, a Tokyo green trader, and a Seattle private-label line. Member payments had, for the last three harvests, averaged USD 186/quintal against a Central American smallholder band of USD 158–210.

## Industry and Market Context

The global coffee market in 2026 sat inside a multi-year volatility regime shaped by weather in Brazil, roya resurgence in Central America, and specialty demand growth that had decoupled from commodity demand. The "C" market had traded from 160 to 305 cents/lb over twenty-four months, with implied volatility near historical highs. Specialty differentials — the premium over "C" paid for coffees cupping 84+ SCA, with certification, and with documented provenance — had held at 60–180 cents/lb across major consuming markets.

Guatemalan highland coffee carried a structural reputation advantage (altitude, varietals, fermentation craft) but an industry-wide yield-per-hectare disadvantage relative to Brazilian mechanization. The roya outbreak of January 2026, concentrated in the Huehuetenango and Alta Verapaz departments, had resulted in early-season estimates of a 12–22% yield loss, pending mid-season confirmation. A weak El Niño for Q3 2026 was on the NOAA-IRI consensus.

Research on specialty-market transitions in smallholder cooperatives had converged on three findings: (i) cup-score crossings from 79 to 84 required an average 18-month investment in fermentation and drying craft before pricing shifted; (ii) certification portfolios interacted non-linearly — two certifications added more pricing power than the sum of each — but added organizational cost; and (iii) direct-trade relationships paid more but required the cooperative to take on credit risk that a commodity buyer had previously carried.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The February 2026 assembly had crystallized a two-year member debate. Younger members had argued for specialty transformation; older members had argued for cash-flow stability. The assembly had voted 72%–28% for transformation, with the condition — written into the minutes — that no single Year-1 decision could leave the cooperative without at least USD 20,000 in working-capital reserve entering the May harvest window.

### What Ramírez knew

Lucía's modeling placed the four levers against a five-year horizon: quality investment moved cup scores slowly but compounded; certifications placed the cooperative on new-buyer lists within twelve months; hedging reduced price-volatility exposure at the cost of upside if the "C" ran; direct-trade buyer-relationship development produced the highest long-run margin but carried the highest near-term execution risk. The arithmetic was clean; the allocation, however, was not.

### What he did not know

Whether the roya outbreak would peak at 12% or 22%. Whether ExporCafé would accept a reduced-volume contract — allowing the cooperative to commit more lots to specialty — or would insist on priority on the commodity volume. Whether the Copenhagen roaster, who had signalled interest in a 300-bag direct-trade contract for the 2027 harvest, would reconfirm after the roya assessment.

### What others were saying

**Lucía Ramírez**, Contadora, argued for **Transformador**: *"Quality USD 40K, Certification USD 30K, Hedge USD 30K, Buyer USD 20K. We keep USD 22K in reserve, above the assembly floor. We move the mix to 18% specialty by Year 3."*

**Don Rigoberto Xol**, senior member, 41 years in the cooperative, argued for **Ahorrador**: *"We do not know the roya. We hedge 70% of production. We spend USD 15K on certification maintenance, USD 10K on micro-mill repair. The rest stays in caja. We move the mix later."*

**Ana Gabriela Méndez**, board member, representing the younger generation, argued for **Arriesgado**: *"Fund the Copenhagen relationship — USD 35K. Fund SCA cupping submissions — USD 15K. Quality investment USD 40K. Certification USD 20K. No hedge. If the 'C' drops we lose; if specialty contracts close we win the five years in one."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — Ahorrador (Don Rigoberto plan):** Quality USD 10K; Certification USD 15K; Hedge USD 60K (70% of production); Buyer USD 10K; Reserve USD 25K. Year-1 cup-score trajectory +0.4; specialty mix +1 pt; cash at risk minimal.

- **Option B — Transformador (Lucía plan):** Quality USD 40K; Certification USD 30K; Hedge USD 30K; Buyer USD 20K; Reserve USD 22K. Year-1 cup-score trajectory +1.2; specialty mix +4 pts; cash at risk moderate; Copenhagen contract probability 55%.

- **Option C — Arriesgado (Méndez plan):** Quality USD 40K; Certification USD 20K; Hedge USD 0; Buyer USD 35K; Reserve USD 25K. Year-1 cup-score trajectory +1.6; specialty mix +6 pts; cash at risk high if "C" drops to 170; Copenhagen contract probability 75%.
