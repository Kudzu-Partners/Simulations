# The Hub: Four Weeks to Open

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1230 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1230 — *The Hub: Founding Day — Sole Trader, Partnership or Limited Company* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, April 27, 2026, at 12:55, Maya Lindqvist sat at the corner table of the Alder Ridge International School cafeteria with a one-page printout from the school librarian and €4,000 of pooled savings that did not yet legally belong to anything. The corner was hers and her two co-founders' from Monday, May 25, if The Hub, their student-run snack bar, was open for business by then. If it was not, the slot went to another student group.

The school's programme coordinator, Mr Okafor, had set one condition. The Hub had to operate as a real business, with a proper registration, a proper bank account and proper records. The three founders had to hand him a single form by Friday naming the legal form The Hub would take and justifying the choice in one paragraph. His advisory committee would review it the following Monday.

## The Protagonist

Maya, seventeen, was in her penultimate year at Alder Ridge, an international school in a mid-sized European city. The Hub was her idea. It came out of a six-month volunteer placement at a community café, where she had learned to run a till, order stock and serve a queue. She wanted the doors open in four weeks and planned to run service, ordering and the daily cash herself. She had been friends with her two co-founders since they were thirteen.

Daniel Moreau had put in €2,000 of the €4,000. He was the one with the spreadsheets, and he worried about getting his money back. His parents had already asked him what would happen if The Hub ran up debts it could not pay. He had also said, half joking, that the person who put in half the money should have half the say.

Aisha Rahman had put in €800. She had a standing offer of a paid part-time tutoring job and would commit to The Hub only if her downside was bounded. She had read about small businesses going under and was the one who kept asking what would happen if it did not work. Maya's contribution made up the remaining €1,200.

## The Decision

Before The Hub could sign a supplier contract, open a business bank account, take card payments or hire a younger student for the lunchtime rush, the three had to choose its legal form. The printout listed five: sole trader, partnership, private limited company, cooperative and social enterprise (see Exhibit 2). The form would decide who paid if The Hub failed owing money, who decided things, what happened if one of them left, and whether anyone could invest later.

## The Company and its Setting

On day one The Hub would sell speciality coffee, hot chocolate, three kinds of toasted sandwich, fresh fruit and a small bakery selection. The bakery items came from a nearby bakery on a sale-or-return basis, but the bakery would not sign that contract with three teenagers, only with a registered entity. The corner was theirs from 12:50 to 15:30 on weekdays, after the regular lunch service, under a written agreement with the facilities manager. The founders' plan assumed 80 to 120 customers a day at an average ticket of €2.80: about €1,200 of weekly revenue and about €400 of weekly contribution after the cost of goods (see Exhibit 1).

The €4,000 sat in a temporary holding account in the name of Maya's mother. That had to change before The Hub could trade.

## The Situation

The forms differed sharply in what they cost to set up. Registering as a sole trader cost little more than a tax registration, but only one person could be the sole trader; the other two would be informal helpers. A private limited company, depending on the country, could absorb €1,500 to €3,000 of the €4,000 in registration and notary fees and a first year of accounts, and some countries required far more paid-in capital than the founders had. Cooperatives in many jurisdictions required a minimum number of members, often seven or more.

Mr Okafor had asked the founders to test whichever form they chose against three futures before defending it (see Exhibit 1). In the first, three months in, the bakery delivered €3,000 of stock that sat unsold through an exam break; The Hub owed €3,000 with €400 in the bank, and a final-demand letter arrived asking for the missing €2,600. In the second, after a year, Aisha took the tutoring job, wanted out and wanted her €800 back, while Maya and Daniel wanted to carry on. In the third, after a year, the parent association offered €15,000 in exchange for a stake, to pay for a permanent fit-out and a second counter at the sports pavilion.

If the founders chose nothing, nothing happened: no bank account, no bakery contract, no opening, and on May 25 the corner went to someone else. If they chose in a hurry, the form would settle, in advance, the conversations about debt, exits and control that none of the three had yet wanted to have.

## What the Founders Controlled

The committee wanted the founders' reasoning in three steps, and the founders took them in order.

First, **what mattered most to each founder**. For Maya, Daniel and Aisha in turn, they had to name the single axis of the choice that mattered most to that person, from six: **liability**, who paid if The Hub failed; **control**, who decided; **access to capital**, how easily more money could come in later; **continuity**, what happened if a founder left; **set-up cost**, the speed and money needed to register; and **profit distribution**, how any surplus was shared.

Second, **which form best handled each future**: for the bad week, the friendship break and the wild success, one of the five forms each.

Third, **the final choice**: the one legal form The Hub would register under, and the single axis the founders were weighting most heavily in making it. The committee would then put to them the strongest objection it could find to the form they had picked.

The €4,000 of seed capital and the twenty-eight days to the slot would not change while they deliberated. What the committee would record, round by round, were two marks and their sum: an **alignment** mark, for how well the founders' choices fitted the founders' goals and the futures in front of them, and an **insight** mark, for the quality of the reasoning behind those choices. The running total, and the committee's written feedback after each step, would decide whether Mr Okafor signed off.

## Closing

At 13:20 Daniel arrived with a laptop and a spreadsheet. Aisha arrived five minutes later with the tutoring offer printed out and folded in her bag. Maya pushed the librarian's printout into the middle of the table. There were five forms on it, four days to Friday, and three founders who did not yet agree on what they were protecting.

| Form | Liability | Who decides | Adding capital later | If an owner leaves | Set-up | Profits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole trader | Unlimited, one owner | The owner | Personal loans only | Business ends with the owner | Near zero; tax registration | All to the owner |
| Partnership | Usually unlimited; each partner liable for the whole debt | Partners, per agreement | Partners' own resources | Ends unless agreed otherwise | Low; written agreement advised | Per agreement |
| Private limited company | Limited to capital invested | Votes in proportion to shares | Can issue shares, borrow more easily | Company continues | Medium to high, varies by country | Dividends in proportion to shares |
