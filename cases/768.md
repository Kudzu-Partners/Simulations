# Halo Brands: Four Tribes, Four Launches

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-768 · **Companion simulation:** Express 768 — *Launch Lab: Design the 4P Mix For Your Tribe* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, January 5, 2026, Amara Osei found a single slide waiting in her inbox at Halo Brands Ltd. It listed four new products, four target customers and four launch dates, one per quarter. At the bottom, the marketing director, Tom Whitfield, had typed one line: "You own all four. First sign-off Friday the 16th."

Osei was a junior brand manager. By January 16 she had to decide how the first product, a plant-based protein powder, would be made, priced, sold and promoted. Three more launches would follow, and in the fifth quarter the board would ask her to explain which of the four had done worst, and why.

## The Protagonist

Osei was 26. She had studied business at university in Leeds, joined Halo's graduate scheme in 2023 and spent two years in sales analytics before moving into brand management the previous autumn. She had worked on other people's launches. These would be the first four with her name on them.

## The Decision

For each of the four launches, Osei would set four things: the version of the product, its price, where it would be sold and how it would be promoted. After each launch, a focus group and a market test would report back, and Halo's launch committee would rate the launch from one to three stars. In the fifth quarter she would face the board's review of the whole portfolio. The committee added up the stars from all five quarters.

## The Company and its Setting

Halo Brands was a mid-sized United Kingdom consumer goods company based in Reading, selling food, drink and household products through supermarkets, online grocers, convenience stores and its own website. Its strategy for 2026 was to stop launching products for "everyone" and to build each new product around one clearly defined group of customers. Inside the company, those groups were called tribes.

The research agency had profiled one tribe for each launch (see Exhibit 1). Priya, 34, lived in London, earned £65,000 a year, trained four times a week, read every label and trusted specialists over supermarkets. The Greens, Chris and Sam, 40, lived in Bristol with two children on a middle income; they shopped with a large online grocer and a national supermarket, trusted third-party certifications, and said they would pay around 15% more for a greener product, but not 50% more. Kai, 19, was a university student in Manchester who found brands on short-video apps, distrusted anything that looked like an advert and bought on impulse in convenience stores. Jess, 39, was a full-time nurse with two children who wanted dinner on the table in 20 minutes, shopped at the two big supermarkets near her home and noticed shelf prices more than advertising.

## The Situation

The launch calendar was fixed: protein powder for Priya in the first quarter, eco-friendly laundry sheets for the Greens in the second, an energy drink for Kai in the third and a no-frills meal kit for Jess in the fourth. If Osei did not sign off a launch by the end of its quarter, the slot passed to another category team and the product waited a year.

Each product came with four versions, four price points, four routes to market and four promotion plans, prepared by the product, pricing, sales and media teams (see Exhibit 2 for the price points). Every option had an internal sponsor. The sales team liked wide distribution; the media team liked campaigns it could measure; the finance partner, Grace Adeyemi, reminded Osei that a better product cost more to make and that the profit on each launch would appear in the board pack.

For the protein powder, the product team offered basic whey with artificial flavours in a plastic tub; standard whey with natural flavours; a premium plant blend with 25 grams of protein and added amino acids; or an organic plant-based powder with 30 grams of protein in compostable packaging. Pricing offered £15, £28, £38 or £52 a tub. Sales offered discount grocers and pound shops; a mass-market listing on a large online marketplace; an upmarket supermarket plus direct sales through specialist gyms; or invite-only pop-ups and boutique fitness studios only. Media offered daytime television and press; paid search and display advertising; small fitness and wellness influencers on social media; or billboards plus sponsorship of elite sporting events only.

## What Osei Controlled

In each of the first four quarters, Osei would choose one option in each of four areas: the **product** version, the **price** tier, the **place** where it would be sold, and the **promotion** plan. The choices for the later launches followed the same pattern, from basic to premium or specialist versions, from discount to premium price points, from discount retailers to subscriptions sold direct, and from mass media to creators and in-store material.

After each launch, she would see five measures from the focus group and market test (see Exhibit 2). **Brand fit**, scored out of 100, measured how well the launch matched its tribe. **Mix consistency**, as a percentage, measured how tightly the four choices hung together. **Purchase intent**, as a percentage, measured how many of the focus group said they would buy. The **sales forecast** was given in units, and the launch's **profit** in pounds. The committee's **stars** for each launch would accumulate across the year.

In the fifth quarter, there would be no product to launch. The board would ask Osei three questions: which of the four launches was the **weakest**; what its **primary cause** was, whether a positioning that did not match the tribe, a price outside what the tribe would pay, a channel that missed where it shopped, or promotion that missed the persona; and on what single basis Kai's group was best defined, whether by age, income and family stage, by region, by lifestyle and values, or by usage and the benefits sought.

## Closing

On Friday, January 16, at 4 p.m., Osei had four printouts on her desk, one from each team, each with a different tub of protein powder on the cover. Whitfield put his head round the door and asked whether she was ready. The launch committee met at five.
