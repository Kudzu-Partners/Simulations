# Atelier Apprend: The Transfer Test

On Tuesday morning, 9 June 2026, Camille Berthier opened an email from Nordpharm Industries that reopened a brief she had thought was settled. Nordpharm, a EUR 4.2 billion European pharmaceutical-manufacturing group and one of Atelier Apprend's largest clients, had a new chief human-resources officer, and the CHRO wanted one thing added to the flagship leadership program before design lock: proof that it changed what people did at work, not just how they rated it. The unproven AI personalization engine Atelier Apprend had built its pitch around would also have to appear in the design as a visible differentiator. Design lock was three days away. The framework behind it — EUR 3.6 million over three years — would renew only if this first cohort of 240 operations managers, spread across nine sites in six countries, proved the training had transferred to the job.

Berthier had ten weeks to get the program into the field, a budget capped at EUR 4,500 per learner with no contingency line, and, in seventeen weeks, a session with the CHRO and a works-council representative in which she would have to show, not assert, that it had worked.

## The Protagonist

Berthier, 42, directed the custom-programs studio inside Atelier Apprend, the tailor-made executive-education unit of a business school in Lyon. She had come up through corporate learning and development before joining the school six years earlier, drawn by its founding claim that executives learn fastest working on their own real business problems, not case studies about someone else's. She had shipped more than thirty programs, most well reviewed. None had been asked, until now, to prove that a manager behaved differently afterward — and across the unit's last twelve programs, the number that mattered to a skeptical client was 22%, the share of learning that had measurably changed behavior at work, against an average satisfaction score of 4.3 out of 5.

## The Company and its Setting

Atelier Apprend billed roughly EUR 14 million a year, ran more than eighty custom programs annually, and drew on 22 in-house designers and a bench of 60 associate faculty. Every program it built was anchored to a client's live problem, with an accountable sponsor inside the client — a model recently extended with an AI personalization engine that adapted content, pacing, and coaching prompts to each learner, a capability few competitors offered but one not yet proven at the scale Nordpharm was now asking for. Nordpharm itself needed the program to develop 240 mid-level operations managers running plants across nine sites in six countries — people the CHRO wanted making faster, better decisions on the shop floor.

## The Situation

The brief Berthier had to design against was not, yet, one problem. Nordpharm's sponsor had described it three different ways: managers did not decide fast enough; there was a collaboration gap between sites; first-line leadership was weak. Whichever of those was true would determine what the program spent its sixteen weeks doing, and a program built to satisfy all three risked proving none of them. The economics were equally unforgiving: a high-touch, action-learning design — live project teams, coached sponsors, in-person sessions across the six countries — cost roughly EUR 6,200 per learner on its own, well past the ceiling; a scalable, cohort-based digital design cost about EUR 2,800 per learner alone, comfortably under it, but a digital-heavy cohort was exactly what had produced the unit's historic 22%. EUR 4,500 per learner, times 240 managers, was EUR 1.08 million total, and none of it was contingency (see Exhibit 1). Two supporting numbers would move alongside transfer: a capability score on the scoped challenge, which new designs typically opened around 30 out of 100, and completion, the share of the cohort finishing the program, which typically opened around 60%. If Berthier changed nothing about how the unit designed, priced, or measured its programs, the arithmetic pointed at the same result the last twelve programs had produced: a satisfied cohort, a transfer rate no better than 22%, and a client who had already said that number would not renew EUR 3.6 million.

## What Berthier Controlled

The first decision was what single behavior the program would be built to change. Berthier could adopt the framing that managers needed to decide faster, since of the three concerns it read as the easiest to put a number on; converge on one specific, measurable behavior, such as operations managers resolving cross-site quality escalations within 48 hours, tied to a single business metric; keep the brief broad, framing it as strengthening first-line leadership so it touched all three of the sponsor's concerns; or try to move on all three fronts at once — collaboration, decision speed, and leadership — so that none of the board's worries went unaddressed. Whichever she chose, she also had to decide what the program would be judged on — movement on the scoped business metric, such as escalation-resolution time across the sites; a pass rate on an end-of-program knowledge test administered to the cohort; or the cohort's average satisfaction score, the measure the unit's last twelve programs already reported — and how disciplined the accompanying out-of-scope list would be, expressed as a percentage of the brief she was willing to explicitly rule out.

Format came next. Against the EUR 4,500 ceiling, Berthier had to blend four elements, each priced per learner: live action-learning project teams working Nordpharm's own challenge; coached reviews with each manager's own sponsor, accountable for results; in-person intensives across the six countries; and scalable digital modules (see Exhibit 2). She also had to decide how the program would drive completion across 240 busy managers — by grading and ranking learners to pressure them through; by mandating that everyone log the full module hours; or by making each manager's sponsor accountable for visible progress on the real project.

The AI engine required its own decision, in three parts. How far to embed it: deploy it fully, including a skills-diagnostic that built a data profile of every learner; leave the engine out of this cohort altogether, since it remained unproven at this scale; use it for adaptive pacing and AI coaching prompts between sessions, with no learner profiling; or lean on it to carry more of the program, thinning the live project work to save cost. Alongside that, what data and consent design to build for the works council and GDPR: minimize the data collected, make it opt-in, and keep a human in the loop over the results; issue a privacy notice with no opt-in and manager dashboards on by default; or switch the tool on and deal with privacy questions if they came up. And what fallback to hold if the works council objected to the diagnostic mid-launch: no fallback at all, leaving the team to improvise if the objection materialized; a pause of the entire launch until the objection cleared; or a manual self-assessment and facilitator triage that left the action-learning core untouched.

Finally, Berthier had to decide how she would prove any of it. She could measure manager and sponsor behavior ratings at 90 days, combined with movement on the Round-1 business metric; a pre/post knowledge test, comparing scores before and after the program; or a post-program satisfaction survey, gathered from the cohort after the program ended. She had to set a target transfer rate against the 22% baseline — 25%, roughly 3 points above it; 80%, more than triple it; or 45%, roughly double it — and decide how to frame the one-paragraph case for renewal: leading with the evidence of behavior change the measurement plan would produce; leading with the AI deployment itself, the differentiator the board had asked to see; or leading with the program's polish and ratings, the results learners already gave it. Underneath all of it sat a single dial: how firmly, on a scale of 0 to 100, she was prepared to argue that a program which changed what 240 managers did at work mattered more than one they simply rated highly.

## Closing

Design lock was Friday. Berthier laid the three versions of the sponsor's brief on her desk next to the EUR 4,500 ceiling and the 22% baseline, and began writing down the one behavior the program would be built to change.
