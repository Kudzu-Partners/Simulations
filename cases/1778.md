# Aurena Group AG: The Attention Budget

On the morning of 12 June 2026, Mareike Voss read the two names her CEO had circled on a printed slide the night before. Both were product concepts that Aurena's own crowdsourcing platform had received years earlier, filed by outside contributors, and passed over by Aurena's review team as not looking like anything Aurena made. Both had since been launched, profitably, by other companies. Her CEO had one question: if "Aurena Open" had those two ideas the whole time, what exactly had the review process been filtering for?

Voss had until the closing plenary of the Aurena Open Innovation Summit on 21 November 2026 to run the platform's next call, decide which of the submissions her small review team would actually read, and bring a funding recommendation to the board. The board's mandate was specific: the next call had to surface and fund at least one idea that was genuinely distant from what Aurena already made, not another variation on it.

## The Protagonist

Voss, 39, had led Aurena's open-innovation function for three years, having moved into it from the consumer-insights team after eight years at the company. She had built the platform's growth from a modest pilot to a registered community of 180,000 contributors — customers, hobbyist inventors, university teams and suppliers who submitted product ideas year-round. She reported to Aurena's Chief Innovation Officer and was measured, formally, on how many submissions the platform generated. Nobody had ever measured her, until this call, on what happened to the ones that were hard to recognize.

## The Decision

Each phase of the call put a different set of choices in front of Voss. First came the prompt itself: she could pose it as an open, provocative challenge — solve the underlying job and ignore Aurena's existing categories entirely; she could keep it narrow, asking contributors directly to improve the existing home-care range; or she could frame it around a clear theme while leaving room for adjacent solutions the platform hadn't tried before. Alongside the prompt sat the submission format: a free-form submission with no required structure at all, leaving contributors to describe their idea however they saw fit; a rigid template with fixed fields, which made every entry quick to compare against the last; or a lighter structure with a few required fields and space for open description. And she had to decide who to invite and what to reward: restrict the call to the existing 180,000-member community and reward whichever entry fit best with Aurena's current range; open eligibility to anyone who wanted to submit, with recognition for the strongest idea overall; or actively court contributors outside the community — university labs, adjacent-industry engineers — with a prize for the most unusual entry.

Before any of that, she had to fix a lens: how many dimensions of distance from Aurena's core business to score explicitly, anywhere from a single overall impression with no explicit axis at all, up to four: technological distance, category distance, user distance and business-model distance.

Once the submissions arrived, her team could deeply evaluate only a small fraction of them, and how she filled that capacity would decide what her team ever saw. She could build a scoring process that explicitly weighed each entry's distance from Aurena's current business before ranking it; she could let the community itself vote entries into the review slots, cheap to run and simple to explain; or she could hand the selection to Aurena's own review panel, trusting their judgment of what looked worth Aurena's time. Layered onto whichever method she chose, she could set aside anywhere from all 120 review slots down to none of them for entries that scored as distant outliers, leaving the remainder to be filled however the triage method decided. And she had to decide who sat on the review panel itself: add a few voices from marketing and from outside the company to the core R&D reviewers; build a panel drawn from other industries, academia and Aurena's own customers alongside R&D; or staff it entirely from Aurena's own R&D group, the people who knew the product lines best.

At the end, with a shortlist in hand, she had to allocate the development budget. She could put the full €6,000,000 behind the near idea, whose revenue case was already credible at €4,000,000; she could fund the near idea in full and set aside a smaller, staged pilot on the distant one to test what it could prove; she could commit the full €6,000,000 to the distant idea's €20,000,000 upside in one commitment, with no pilot first; or she could divide the €6,000,000 so that both the near idea and the distant one were fully funded at once. Whatever she funded on the distant side, it came with an internal sponsor who had so far shown little enthusiasm for it, and she could choose how much work to put into bringing that sponsor onside — anywhere from a single briefing after the decision was made to naming them the bet's executive owner from day one.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurena Group AG, founded in 1962 and headquartered in Hamburg, made home-care, personal-care and small-appliance products sold in 40 countries, with 2025 revenue of €2,600,000,000 and 9,400 employees (see Exhibit 1). Its innovation pipeline had, in recent years, produced mostly line extensions and reformulations rather than category-defining products. "Aurena Open," the public crowdsourcing platform Voss ran, cost the company roughly €4,000,000 a year to operate and was, alongside Aurena's internal R&D function, the firm's main channel for ideas that did not originate inside the building.

## The Situation

The call Voss was about to run would generate roughly 4,000 submissions if she kept the prompt narrow, fewer if she opened it wider. Her review team, whichever way the numbers landed, had the capacity to deeply evaluate only about 120 of them — a fraction of any total she was likely to see. Four figures would be tracked once the call closed and stayed visible through funding: a distant-idea capture rate, describing what share of the genuinely distant ideas in the pile actually reached deep review rather than being screened out before anyone read them; an average novelty score for the pool her team actually reviewed; the raw count of submissions deeply reviewed against the fixed capacity of 120; and an innovation value figure, the risk-adjusted expected return in euros on whatever she ultimately funded. Alongside these, the board would also be watching how much of the €6,000,000 budget got committed and whether "Aurena Open" itself was judged, at the end, to be worth its running cost.

If the call ran the way the last several had — a familiar prompt, quick triage by whichever method was cheapest, a review panel drawn from the people who already knew the product range — nothing about that pattern would change on its own. Two ideas the crowd had already offered once had gone unfunded and were now on shelves that belonged to somebody else's balance sheet, and the CEO's question about what the filtering process had actually been selecting for would still be sitting on the table in November.

## What Voss Controlled

Voss's decisions covered the full length of the call (see Exhibit 2): the lens she used to define distance, the framing, format and incentives of the prompt itself, the triage mechanism and how many review slots it reserved for outliers, who sat on the review panel, how the €6,000,000 was ultimately allocated across the shortlist, and how much effort she put into winning over the sponsor of whatever distant idea made it that far.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 12 June, Voss opened a blank call brief and looked again at the two names her CEO had circled. Somewhere in the next 4,000 submissions, or the smaller pile a different kind of prompt would bring in, was whatever the board was asking her to find. She had until November, a review team that could read maybe 120 entries out of however many arrived, and a first decision — how to word the invitation — that would already be narrowing the field before a single idea came in.
