# Boréal Matériaux: Sequencing the Transition

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1980 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1980 — *Lead the Transition* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, April 14, 2026, Camille Rioux read two documents that had reached her eleven hours apart. The first was an open letter from a fund holding 4.8% of Boréal Matériaux inc., demanding a net-zero pathway the market could believe and stating that it would nominate its own slate of directors at the annual general meeting otherwise. The second was a single page from her finance director: a federal carbon-price step-up and a tightening of the large-emitter rules had added CA$90 million to Boréal's projected 2027 carbon bill.

Rioux had two quarters. Her board had told her to bring a transition plan to that annual meeting — four decision points, ending in front of the shareholders — and in that time she would have to establish what Boréal could actually abate, commit a CA$600 million transition budget across four competing options, settle what she would offer a union, a province, environmental organisations and two investor camps that wanted opposite things, and say in public which of her numbers were commitments.

## The Protagonist

Camille Rioux, 54, had been President and Chief Executive of Boréal Matériaux since 2022. She joined the company in 1998 as a process engineer, ran its oldest kiln as plant manager from 2006 to 2011, and spent four years as Chief Operating Officer before the board appointed her. She knew the chemistry of a kiln at first hand, and had lived five years in the town it supported. At her first annual meeting as chief executive she raised the dividend and told the register that Boréal paid its owners. Her long-term incentive moved with total shareholder return over three years.

## The Decision

Rioux had to decide, quarter by quarter, what Boréal would abate and in what order, where the CA$600 million would go, what she would offer the groups the change reached first, and which of her public numbers were commitments rather than intentions.

## The Company and its Setting

Boréal Matériaux inc., founded in Montréal in 1971 and listed on the Toronto Stock Exchange, ran four plants: two cement and lime kilns in Québec, an aggregates operation in Ontario, and a gypsum-board line. It employed 3,200 people, most of them unionised and concentrated in towns where Boréal was the largest employer. Revenue in 2025 was CA$1.9 billion, EBITDA CA$323 million on a 17% margin, and market capitalisation stood near CA$2.4 billion (see **Exhibit 1**). About 60% of the share register was held by income-focused investors who owned the stock for its dividend.

Boréal's kilns emitted roughly 2.6 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. About 60% of that total was process emissions — carbon dioxide driven out of limestone by the chemistry of making clinker, and released whatever fuel or electricity heated the kiln. Under the federal carbon price, each of those tonnes was scheduled to cost CA$170 by 2030.

## The Situation

The CA$90 million added to 2027 was the near end of a longer arithmetic. At 2.6 million tonnes and CA$170 a tonne, an unmanaged Boréal would pay roughly CA$440 million a year in carbon costs by 2030 — more than the CA$323 million of EBITDA it earned in 2025 (see **Exhibit 1**). If Rioux changed nothing, the bill would arrive on that schedule, the activist's slate would stand at the meeting, and the plants would run on unaltered while the liability grew.

The people who would question her did not agree with one another. One group of institutional holders wanted an aggressive published pathway this cycle. The income-focused majority of the register read every dollar of transition capital as a dollar off the dividend, because capital spent in a year cut earnings per share in that year. The union at the oldest kiln represented 480 jobs in a town where Boréal accounted for 30% of employment, and knew that the same kiln was the largest single item on the emissions inventory. The provincial government wanted the emissions cut and the regional jobs kept. Environmental organisations had said in advance that a carbon-capture pilot would be read as public relations unless someone independent verified it.

Boréal reported earnings quarterly, and a major transition announcement had moved comparable stocks between 8% and 12% in a single day.

Rioux's team tracked four numbers and one judgement. The share of the 2.6 million tonnes on a credible path to being cut stood at 0%. A coalition index kept by corporate affairs on a 0–100 scale, combining how far the investors, the union, the province and the environmental groups would go along with a Boréal plan, read 50. The share price was indexed to 100 on the day the letter arrived. A plan-credibility score from 0 to 10, taken from a standing analyst panel asked whether the pathway as described could actually be delivered, read 5.0. The judgement was her corporate secretary's assessment of the risk that the activist's slate would carry the meeting: High.

## What Rioux Controlled

At the first decision point Rioux fixed a diagnosis and a reading of the field. She could treat the whole 2.6 million tonnes as addressable and set a net-zero target across all of it; treat it as an energy-sourcing problem and switch to clean electricity; or separate the roughly 60% that was process emissions and take those cuts in cost order. She recorded whether the conflict would concentrate on the activist alone, whether a technically sound plan would settle the disagreement on its merits, or whether the field was divided across investors, workers, government and campaigners at once. She chose an opening posture: announce a headline target this quarter, commit to nothing and manage quarter to quarter, or set out a sequence and table the plan later. And she set, from 0 to 100, how much structured consultation with the affected groups to build in before deciding anything.

At the second she allocated the CA$600 million across the four options her engineering group had costed — alternative fuels, clinker substitution, kiln electrification and a carbon-capture pilot — whose costs per tonne avoided differed by a factor of five (see **Exhibit 2**). She did not have to spend all of it, and she also set what share of whatever she committed would be spent in the first year rather than staged across the programme.

At the third she faced the four camps in the same week. On the oldest kiln she could idle it, removing 600,000 tonnes immediately; keep it running and leave the question until after the meeting; or announce a phased conversion. For the investor base she could lead with the aggressive published pathway, lead with the dividend base and keep the transition quiet, or take one message to both framed around the carbon liability. With the province and the campaigners she could tell the premier the plan was settled and leave the environmental groups outside it, work the premier on jobs alone, or negotiate a joint programme. And she took a position on the dividend: cut it to fund the capital programme, say nothing, or state that it would be held.

At the fourth, at the annual meeting itself, she chose what to commit to publicly — a 50% cut by 2030 and net-zero by 2035, no specific interim numbers, or only the reductions her funded measures and her workforce plan could deliver. She chose what to report every quarter: the headline target alone, whichever measure best suited that quarter, or a fixed audited set of measures. Facing the proxy slate and a dividend-cut headline, she could adopt the activist's demands in full, refuse to engage, or defend the plan with whatever support she had assembled. Last, she recorded on a 0–100 scale how firmly she would hold her line when the room pushed back.

## Closing

Rioux set the two documents side by side and wrote four numbers across a blank page: 0%, 50, 100, 5.0. The meeting was two quarters out. She had CA$600 million, four options priced very differently per tonne, five constituencies with incompatible demands, and a 4.8% holder counting votes.
