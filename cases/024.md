## Abstract

Meridian Insurance Group, a USD 850M capital multiline property-and-casualty insurer with USD 420M of gross written premium across auto, homeowners, and small-commercial lines, entered Q1 2026 at the peak of a three-year soft market. The book carried a 98.4% combined ratio (1.6% underwriting profit), a solvency ratio of 186%, and the unresolved question of whether to match the 8% premium cut the two largest competitors had announced in December 2025. A known events schedule — soft market Q1, adverse selection Q2, Hurricane Eloise Q3 (industry base-case USD 180M gross on Meridian), hard market Q4 — would test five capital-at-risk dials: Premium Adjustment, Catastrophe Reinsurance, Reserve Approach, Investment Strategy, and Dividend. The case opens at the January 12 Underwriting Committee meeting where the new Chief Underwriting Officer, Elena Reyes, must recommend Quarter 1 settings against two senior voices — a CFO who wants to preserve short-term earnings and a Chief Risk Officer who wants to buy the catastrophe cover now, while it is still cheap.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate underwriting-cycle discipline at the soft-market peak, and articulate the mechanism by which pricing discipline today pays the catastrophe of two quarters from now.
2. Analyze the trade-offs among premium, reinsurance, reserves, investment strategy, and dividend as capital-at-risk dials in a regulated insurer.
3. Diagnose the interaction between reinsurance attachment points and balance-sheet solvency during a named-storm event.
4. Design a reserve approach (conservative / central / aggressive) appropriate to the combined-ratio regime and the capital cushion.
5. Recommend a defensible Quarter 1 package and articulate the events under which it loses.

On the afternoon of **January 12, 2026**, **Elena Reyes** sat in the Underwriting Committee room on the thirty-first floor of Meridian Insurance Group's Miami headquarters and read, for the third time, the one-line note the Chief Risk Officer had put at the top of her pre-read: *"Catastrophe reinsurance prices will never be lower than they are on Friday."* Below the note, in her own handwriting from the morning, she had added: *"Combined ratio at 98.4 is the last thing the competitors will match."* The committee would convene at 2:00 p.m.

---

## The Protagonist

Elena Reyes, 49, had been appointed Chief Underwriting Officer of Meridian on November 1, 2025. A mathematics major from the University of Puerto Rico and a Fellow of the Casualty Actuarial Society, she had spent nineteen years in reinsurance — ten at a global reinsurer's Bermuda office and nine at a Florida-based primary insurer as head of catastrophe modeling. She had been recruited to Meridian on the strength of two prior cycle calls: she had pushed her prior company to hold pricing in the 2019 soft market and to over-buy catastrophe cover in the summer of 2023, decisions that had preserved USD 220M of capital through a single named-storm event. Her contract carried a cycle-length vesting gate.

---

## The Decision

Reyes had to approve, by 3:30 p.m. on January 12, Quarter 1 settings on five dials the simulation would treat as her Round 1 input:

1. **Premium Adjustment (−15% to +15%)** — match the 8% cut, hold flat, or raise.
2. **Catastrophe Reinsurance (0–100%)** — share of the modeled 1-in-100-year loss ceded to reinsurers.
3. **Reserve Approach** — Conservative, Central, or Aggressive estimate for IBNR (incurred but not reported).
4. **Investment Strategy** — Bond (duration matched), Balanced, or Equity-tilted.
5. **Dividend (USD M)** — shareholder dividend payable end of Q1.

Her choices would compound across four quarters against a known events schedule and would be legible through three metrics: combined ratio, capital position, and solvency ratio. The CEO, Manuel Castillo, had told her in the Monday one-on-one: *"The board will forgive an expensive quarter. It will not forgive an expensive year. Pick the combination you can defend after Hurricane Eloise hits."*

---

## Company Background

Meridian Insurance Group had been founded in 1978 in Miami as a Florida homeowners insurer and had expanded over five decades into auto and small-commercial lines across eight U.S. states. 2025 gross written premium (GWP) was USD 420M (auto 48%, homeowners 34%, small-commercial 18%). Capital at start of 2026 was USD 850M. The solvency ratio (available capital to solvency capital requirement) was 186% — above the regulatory minimum of 100% and above Meridian's own board-set floor of 150%. The combined ratio had moved from 94.2% (2022, hard market peak) to 98.4% (2025), reflecting three years of soft-market premium compression and claim-inflation pressure.

Meridian was majority-owned by a family trust (58%) with the remainder public-float. The trust's 2024 mandate letter had specified two non-negotiable targets: a dividend floor of USD 30M per year and a solvency-ratio floor of 150%. Those two targets traded off in every event path Reyes had modeled.

The reinsurance program, placed in December 2025 for the 2026 year, provided proportional cover up to 60% of the modeled 1-in-100-year gross loss. The unplaced 40% layer — USD 60M of cover on top of the programmed USD 140M — remained available in the Bermuda spot market at a price the CRO estimated 20–30% below historical average. Reyes had five business days to add to the cover before the market re-priced on the first named storm.

## Industry and Market Context

The U.S. P&C market had entered 2026 at the peak of a soft cycle: eighteen months of aggressive premium competition, industry combined ratios at the 97–99% range, and reinsurance pricing that had softened through 2025 on abundant alternative-capital supply. Three empirical regularities from the post-2015 cycle literature were consequential for the decision. First, insurers that held pricing discipline through the soft peak (refusing to match competitor cuts) averaged a 4.2-point combined-ratio advantage in the subsequent hard market because they entered the hard market with a better book. Second, catastrophe reinsurance bought in the twelve months before a named-storm event carried, in spot terms, 18–28% lower pricing than cover bought in the same week as the event. Third, aggressive reserve approaches at the soft-market peak correlated strongly with adverse-development charges in the subsequent hard market, eroding trust with regulators and reinsurers.

Hurricane Eloise, modeled by two of the three major catastrophe-modeling vendors for a 55–65% probability of making U.S. landfall in the 2026 season with a gross-loss distribution centered at USD 180M for a portfolio with Meridian's footprint, was the single dominant event on the committee's radar.

---

## The Situation

### How we got here

Meridian had held pricing flat through 2024 against a soft market its competitors had chased. In 2025, under pressure from the board on GWP growth, the prior CUO had accepted 4% cuts on auto and 2% on homeowners. GWP had grown 3.1%; the combined ratio had drifted from 96.4% to 98.4%. The prior CUO had resigned in October 2025. Reyes had been appointed in November. Her November and December had been spent rebuilding the actuarial-indications file, re-quoting the reinsurance program, and modeling Q1–Q4 2026 events.

### What Reyes knew

The internal analytics team had modeled four scenarios across the five dials. Hold-pricing (+0%) with 60% reinsurance placed GWP at USD 406M (−3.3%) but preserved a 96.8% combined ratio. Match-pricing (−8%) with 60% reinsurance grew GWP to USD 445M (+6.0%) at a combined ratio of 101.2% and adverse selection in auto. Hold-pricing with 90% reinsurance cost an additional USD 8M of premium ceded but reduced the post-Eloise capital impact by USD 50M. Aggressive reserves at the soft peak reduced Q1 reported earnings by USD 14M but cut Q4 adverse-development risk by two-thirds.

### What she did not know

Whether Hurricane Eloise would land on the 55% probability or on the 65% probability, and whether it would track the Florida-east scenario or the Gulf scenario (the two scenarios carried materially different Meridian-portfolio losses). Whether the two largest competitors would deepen their cut from 8% to 12% in Q2, turning the market into a cut-chase. Whether the USD 60M spot-reinsurance layer would still be available on Friday at the price the CRO had quoted.

### What others were saying

**Sanjay Iyer**, CFO, argued for **Match and Release Capital**: *"We match eight percent. We grow GWP. We hold reserves at central. We pay the thirty-million dividend. We finance the soft market with the capital cushion. If Eloise comes, the reinsurance cover we have is enough."*

**Karen Whitfield**, Chief Risk Officer, argued for **Hold and Buy the Cover**: *"We hold pricing. We buy the additional forty-percent reinsurance layer. We move reserves to conservative. We cut the investment tail by two turns of duration. If Eloise hits the base case, the ninety-percent cover saves us fifty million of capital. If it hits the tail, it saves us a hundred."*

**Manuel Castillo**, CEO, argued for **Hold Pricing, Central Reserves, Prior Reinsurance, Dividend Flat**: *"Discipline on price. Do not over-engineer the back book. Pay the dividend. If Friday's reinsurance market is still open, revisit in Round 2."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Reyes had narrowed the space to three options.

- **Option A — Hold, Over-Insure, Conservative Reserves (Whitfield plan):** Premium flat; reinsurance to 90%; reserves conservative; investment bond-heavy; dividend USD 25M (below floor, trust conversation required). Projected Q4: combined ratio 92%, capital USD 835M, solvency 178%. Q1 earnings impacted by USD 18M.

- **Option B — Hold, Standard Reinsurance, Central Reserves (Castillo plan):** Premium flat; reinsurance 60%; reserves central; investment balanced; dividend USD 30M. Projected Q4: combined ratio 88%, capital USD 810M, solvency 172%. Eloise base case covered; Eloise tail case strains the covenant.

- **Option C — Match Price, Grow GWP (Iyer plan):** Premium −8%; reinsurance 60%; reserves central; investment balanced; dividend USD 30M. Projected Q4: combined ratio 96%, capital USD 790M, solvency 164%. Growth delivered; discipline compromised.

Reyes suspected Option A dominated on expected capital and lost on short-run earnings; Option B was optimal under the Eloise base case and painful under the tail; Option C was the option that would, in the CRO's formulation, "win the quarter and lose the cycle." At 1:47 p.m. she took the last sip of her coffee, picked up the binder, and walked to the committee room.
