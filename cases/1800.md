# The Lonely Chair

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1800 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1800 — *The Lonely Chair* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 5 October 2026, Elena Roig read the board's decision twice to be sure. Talea Digital Solutions had committed its largest client to a fixed go-live for the re-platformed core system, Project Atlas, on 30 November 2026 — eight weeks away, five weeks behind schedule, with no slack left in the critical path. Missing the date meant a EUR 1.8 million penalty and a EUR 4 million annual renewal at risk. The board had made the promise before Roig had a vote in it; she had been Managing Director of Enterprise Platforms for three weeks.

For six years before that, as delivery director, Roig had walked down the hall whenever something like this landed, to compare notes with whoever else carried a piece of it. That hallway no longer worked. The people she would have walked to were now eight direct reports, one of whom, Jordi Serra, had wanted her chair. Twenty-three decisions sat queued on her desk waiting on her signature, and nobody had flagged it as a problem yet.

## The Protagonist

Roig, 39, had spent eleven years at Talea, the last six as delivery director for the unit now called Enterprise Platforms. She had built her name delivering client engagements on schedule inside a company that grew fast on a flat, informal culture, where the founder set the tone in person and decisions got made over coffee, not hierarchy. When her predecessor left abruptly, the board promoted her from inside the unit she knew, over Serra, a team lead also in contention who now reported to her. The role carried an unstated assumption: someone who thrived as a peer would simply keep doing the same work from a different chair.

## The Decision

Roig's leadership group worked through four sessions, each closing before the next opened.

The first was diagnosis: what explained the missed approvals, the falling pulse score, and the flight risk among her senior engineers. One reading blamed the reporting structure itself — every decision now passed through one newly promoted person. A second held that Roig had not put in the hours the job required. A third located the fault in a team not yet ready for real authority. A fourth called it a scheduling problem, solvable by revising the project plan. Tied to that diagnosis was which relationship to rebuild first: a confidential sounding board such as a peer managing director or coach; the board sponsor alone, to manage upward and settle the rest; the client alone, ahead of internal dynamics; or no relationship work, in favor of pushing harder on the queue herself. A further dial, zero to a hundred, set how openly the group would name this to the team as structural rather than a question of effort.

The second session spent a pool of Roig's hours and a EUR 120,000 budget, neither renewable once committed (see Exhibit 2). Four arrangements were available: an executive coach paired with a chief of staff for delivery cadence; a confidential peer-MD board to pressure-test decisions; a chief of staff alone for process and cadence support; or no support investment, reinvesting the hours into the project. Beside it sat delegation: give Serra, the passed-over lead, a mandate over the legacy integration; hand the workstreams to two trusted lieutenants and route Serra around them; distribute authority to everyone holding a queued decision; or keep all twenty-three decisions herself. Whatever she delegated needed a protocol — written decision rights and escalation thresholds; a loose weekly check-in with nothing written down; or none at all. A final number protected a recovery block of zero to fourteen hours a week.

A cluster of live decisions arrived together the same week as the board update. The Atlas recovery path could renegotiate scope with the client to ship a reduced core on 30 November and phase the rest after; add contractors to the critical path and ask the existing team for more; or say nothing and plan to absorb the penalty if the date slipped. Serra had escalated, threatening to leave within two weeks with the legacy-integration knowledge — Roig could engage him directly and confirm his mandate, route around him and rely on others, or press him to commit or leave. The board sponsor's ten-day update needed a posture: honest amber, naming the risk with a recovery plan; confident green, projecting strength to buy time; or near silence until there was something definite to report. Running under all three was a dial for how hard to hold the line on the team's and her own wellbeing against the pressure to push through.

The fourth session set the ninety-day operating model: institutionalise what had been built into a fixed cadence, keep the cadence while dropping the coach and peer board now the crunch had passed, or revert to deciding alone, since the support had only been needed for the emergency. Serra's situation needed a long-term answer: retain and reposition him around the platform architecture with a real growth path, manage a clean exit with proper knowledge transfer, or leave him sidelined. The board update could be delivered as an honest trajectory naming the support model and its owners, as a narrative crediting Roig personally, or as vague reassurance avoiding hard questions. A last number asked how firmly to defend that sustainable leadership beat heroics.

## The Company and its Setting

Talea Digital Solutions, founded in Barcelona in 2009, built cloud platforms and managed services for mid-market clients in retail, insurance and logistics (see Exhibit 1). The firm employed 640 people and posted EUR 78 million in annual revenue at a 16 percent EBITDA margin. Enterprise Platforms, Roig's unit, accounted for 280 of those employees and 60 percent of group revenue — the largest unit in the company, now hers alone to run.

## The Situation

Three weeks into the job, Roig's own dashboard told a consistent story. The pulse-engagement score read 61, down from 74 before the leadership change. A leader-wellbeing index — sleep, support, perspective, recovery — sat at 42 out of 100. A decision-quality measure read 38 out of 100, with twenty-three decisions queued and an approval cycle stretched from two days to six. Atlas delivery confidence, the project's own estimate of hitting 30 November, stood at 30 out of 100. Roig was working more than seventy hours a week and had cancelled every one-to-one to keep pace.

Two camps had formed underneath her, unbidden: absorb the load herself until Atlas was safe, or hand off everything at once and trust the team to self-organize. Neither had asked which of the twenty-three decisions actually needed her. Serra had gone quiet in meetings and was known to be talking to other companies. The board sponsor wanted his ten-day answer on whether Atlas was on track and whether the new MD was coping.

If none of it changed, the arithmetic was plain: at the current cycle time the queue would keep growing faster than Roig could clear it, engagement would keep sliding as the team absorbed a widening bottleneck, and Atlas would reach its final month as short of runway as the day the board set the date.

## What Roig Controlled

Across the four sessions, Roig's leadership group committed a diagnosis, a support structure, a delegation and its protocol, a recovery block, a set of live calls under deadline pressure, and an operating model for the next ninety days — spending up to twenty hours of Roig's own time and EUR 120,000 of support budget, neither of which reset once spent. They were measured on four things: team engagement, leader-wellbeing, decision quality, and Atlas's delivery confidence against the fixed 30 November date.

## Closing

At six that evening, Roig closed her laptop and looked at the chair across from her desk, where she used to sit when the office belonged to someone else. The board update was ten days out. Twenty hours and a hundred and twenty thousand euros stood between where she was and whatever she decided to build. She had not decided anything yet.
