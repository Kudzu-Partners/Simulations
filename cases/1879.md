# IMD-Barcelona: Building the Ecosystem

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1879 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1879 — *Building the Ecosystem* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On 9 March 2026, Marta Bonet left the spring board meeting of Institut Mediterrani de Direcció — IMD-Barcelona — with a mandate and no plan for meeting it. The foundation chair had tabled an outside strategy review showing the school's fastest-growing market was no longer applying to it at all, and the board had ring-fenced €4,500,000 of capital to build something that would win part of it back, enrollable for the autumn 2027 intake. A second weak year, the chair had warned, would trigger a review of who was running the school.

Bonet had one budget cycle to decide where that money went, and to prove by launch that the choice was the right one.

## The Protagonist

Bonet, 51, had been Dean of IMD-Barcelona for a little over a year, promoted from director of the school's Executive MBA after two decades building its relationships with corporate clients. She had inherited a school still working toward the industry's most recognised accreditations, a €34,000,000 income statement that cleared a comfortable 8% surplus, and, in the same set of board papers, the news that neither achievement had yet to stop the school's newest and fastest-growing market from choosing somebody else.

## The Decision

The board had not asked Bonet to save the school's degree masters. It had asked her to decide how IMD would compete for a market it had assumed it already owned.

The first choice was how to answer the new competitor at all. She could commit fresh effort to building something separate and purpose-built for the segment the rival had captured; direct new investment into making the flagship full-time MBA still better, on the theory that a stronger core would eventually pull the market back; hold her position, on the view that reputation and accreditation would see off a new entrant in time; or meet the rival directly, cutting price to compete in the low-cost market it had created. Whichever way she leaned, she also had to choose the single group of learners the new programme would serve — the fast-growing mid-career professionals the rival had already signed, the new graduates who filled the school's traditional pipeline, the senior executives who paid its highest margins, or all three together, spreading the launch budget and faculty attention across all three audiences at once. And she had to set, on a scale of zero to a hundred, how rigorously she would stress-test the one assumption that, if wrong, would break whichever strategy she chose.

## The Company and its Setting

IMD-Barcelona occupied a campus on Avinguda Diagonal, teaching 1,900 students a year across a full-time MBA, an executive MBA, six specialised masters and a growing menu of open-enrolment executive programmes. Ninety-five core faculty carried that load, backed by adjuncts and practitioners drawn from industry. Revenue stood at €34,000,000 and the operating surplus at €2,720,000 — a margin healthy enough to have disguised, for two years running, how much ground the school was losing underneath it (Exhibit 1).

Two facts defined the exposure. Sixty-two percent of that revenue still came from the traditional degree masters, and those programmes were shrinking: applications had fallen from 2,250 to 2,000 in two years, an 11% decline, while yield — the share of admitted applicants who enrolled — had slipped from 41% to 36%. And the market the school had never quite decided to enter, degree-length mid-career reskilling, had just been claimed by somebody else.

## The Situation

SkillForge, a venture-funded micro-credential platform, had signed 14,000 mid-career learners in eighteen months at roughly a fifth of IMD's price, and was now courting two of the school's largest corporate clients. Its offer was narrower than a degree and far cheaper, and it was winning learners who had never been IMD applicants at all.

The board's ring-fenced €4,500,000 covered barely 60% of the roughly €7,200,000 that programme design, technology, faculty and marketing would together need to fund the launch properly. If Bonet spent the year defending the degree masters instead of answering SkillForge directly, the arithmetic pointed one way: applications would keep falling, SkillForge would keep signing the learners IMD never saw, and the board would be back within the year asking why the second weak year had arrived on schedule.

## What Bonet Controlled

Diagnosing the market and choosing a segment was the first of four linked decisions Bonet would make across the budget cycle, sketched in Exhibit 2. Next she would have to design the programme itself and choose how it was built: anchor it with three or four corporate employers in exchange for certain but lower-margin enrolment; scale fastest by handing delivery to a white-label technology partner in exchange for its own cut of curriculum control and 30–40% of revenue; slow the pace but bring in a public co-funding partner and its compliance obligations; blend a corporate anchor with selective technology delivery while keeping brand and curriculum in-house; or build the whole thing alone, at full control and margin but the slowest, most capital-hungry pace of all. She would set price against SkillForge's — a premium justified by outcomes and accreditation, a head-on match at the rival's own price, or the school's full legacy-programme price, positioned as elite — and allocate the €4,500,000 across content and faculty reskilling, technology, new faculty and practitioner hiring, and marketing, ideally without committing every last euro of it.

Then she would have to keep the school itself intact while she built the new thing. On capability, she could fund time and incentives to reskill the willing faculty while hiring selectively for genuine gaps; hire new faculty and adjuncts and leave the existing faculty as they were; mandate the new content for everyone with no protected time or incentive; or fund the programme design and assume the faculty would adapt on their own. If the technology partner asked for a say over curriculum as the price of scale, she could hold full control and accept slower growth, share control through a joint board with clear intellectual-property terms, or cede control in exchange for speed. If an anchor employer offered guaranteed seats in exchange for exclusivity, she could decline and keep the door open to other employers, or grant it and lock in the certainty. And she had to set, again from zero to a hundred, how hard she would fight to protect the degree programmes' quality and morale while attention and money moved toward the launch.

Finally she would have to convince the foundation chair the whole thing would last. She could commit to a scorecard combining early indicators of partnership value and pipeline with later ones — enrolment, contribution, revenue mix; report enrolment headcount alone each term; or build a dashboard for launch week and move attention elsewhere afterward. On financing beyond the seed, she could plan to carry the business to its projected break-even and reinvest the surplus; spend the full €4,500,000 immediately and leave the following year to itself; or assume a recurring public subsidy would close any gap. On what the school would give up to pay for what it was starting, she could sunset two of the smallest legacy masters and redirect their faculty and budget; stop nothing and run the new programme on top of the full existing portfolio; or cut the executive-education line that still funded 30% of the income the school needed. And she would state, in a final number from zero to a hundred, how firmly she was prepared to defend a slower, durable model over a fast, superficial win.

Four numbers would tell her, and the board, whether it had worked. A relevance score — the school's position in the segment it was chasing — opened at 12 out of 100 against a target of 70. A second score for the value the school's outside partnerships were actually creating opened at 10. A third, measuring whether the whole effort could survive past its seed funding, opened at 8. And a straightforward count of the students the new programme was projected to enrol in its first year opened at zero.

## Closing

Bonet closed the strategy review, opened a blank page, and drew three columns: response, segment, assumption. She had one budget cycle, €4,500,000 against a €7,200,000 wish list, and a blank page.
