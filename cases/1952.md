# Lumière Living: The Silver Play

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1952 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1952 — *Design for Longevity* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 9 March 2026, Mariana Sequeira read the user-test transcript for the third time and stopped in the same place. A 68-year-old woman from Matosinhos had turned the prototype over, set it down, said it was beautifully made, and added that it would be perfect for her mother. The object was Lumière Living's first product for the over-60 market — a table lamp with oversized controls and an emergency button — built to a brief Sequeira had written and signed. In a 90-person panel of 60- to 75-year-olds, 71% said it made them feel old, and purchase intent came back at 8% against the 25% Lumière required to fund a launch.

Twelve weeks from that morning Sequeira had to stand in front of the board and either commit the €4.0 million it had ring-fenced for the longevity market or hand it back. In between she had to account for why a product that worked had been refused, decide who the line was for, settle how much of the €4.0 million went into the design itself, and set a price. The autumn retail slot Lumière had booked would not hold for a second cycle.

## The Protagonist

Mariana Sequeira, 41, had led innovation at Lumière Living since 2024. She joined in 2016 from a Lisbon industrial-design consultancy and ran the lighting line for four years — the part of the catalogue that won most of the company's awards. It was Sequeira who took the longevity proposal to the board in the autumn of 2025, arguing that a house built on human-centred design was better placed to serve older buyers than the assistance-equipment firms in the category. The board agreed and funded her. The lamp had been developed under her, and the 8% belonged to her too.

## The Decision

Sequeira had twelve weeks to bring the board a redesigned line with the €4.0 million allocated, a price set and a buyer named. The board would read four numbers, and watch them move round by round as the plan took shape.

**Desirability** was purchase intent among older buyers, measured as the panel had measured it: 8% against the 25% hurdle. **Personal value** was a 0-to-100 assessment, from a standing panel of older co-designers, of how much better and more dignified the product made the owner's day; the lamp scored 20. **Societal value**, scored the same way, covered inclusion, dignity, and whether the object could be repaired and taken back at end of life; the lamp scored 15. **Payback** was the months the line needed to recover the launch outlay; the CFO would not sign above 30, and with no price set it could not yet be calculated. None of the €4.0 million had been committed.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumière Living, Lda., founded in Porto in 2009, sold lighting, furniture and household objects across Europe through retail partners and its direct channel. It turned over €38 million at a 9% operating margin and employed 160 people, 22 of them in an in-house design studio. Its reputation rested on objects people wanted to be seen owning; its buyers skewed to urban 30- to 45-year-olds.

The over-60 opportunity was worth €90 million a year in serviceable revenue, and it did not behave as one market (see **Exhibit 2**). Buyers between 60 and 70 were largely healthy and bought on self-image; buyers of 75 and over bought on function and safety and came through assistance channels. What satisfied one group read wrongly to the other: a healthy 63-year-old saw a hospital product, a frail 80-year-old found the same object too complicated.

A Scandinavian competitor was preparing a home line for the same market under a dignified-ageing banner. If Lumière missed the autumn slot it would arrive second into a category it could have defined.

## The Situation

The €4.0 million was ring-fenced and would not be topped up (see **Exhibit 1**). It had to cover the design and engineering of the object, whatever Lumière committed on accessibility, co-design and repairability, and the commercial launch — pricing support, channel and marketing. Every euro moved to one line came off another.

Inside that budget sat a hard step. Designing the line for a wide range of ability — adjustable controls, revised ergonomics, older users in the room while it was drawn — would cost about €1.1 million more in design and tooling than re-colouring and re-finishing the existing concept. The studio could not both spend it and fund the channel at the level the commercial team wanted.

The argument inside the company had not resolved. Engineering read the result as an incomplete product: fall detection, a larger emergency control, connectivity so a daughter in Lisbon could see the lamp was on. The commercial team read it as price and awareness: the price point untested, the launch unsupported, the category unexplained. The studio read it as what the object said about its owner: a lamp that announced a decline the buyer did not accept.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was plain. The concept sat at 8% purchase intent against a 25% hurdle, personal value at 20 and societal value at 15; none of the €4.0 million was committed; the autumn slot would pass; and the Scandinavian line would define what a dignified product for older buyers looked like.

## What Sequeira Controlled

Sequeira worked the cycle in four stages, committing at each before the next opened.

She had first to put a diagnosis to the board and set how hard she would argue it, from raising it as a possibility to staking the plan on it. Four readings of the 71% were open: that the panel refused what the object said about them; that it refused an incomplete product, thin on protective function; that it refused the price against what it delivered; or that it grasped the benefit poorly, the fault lying in the explaining. She had also to name what the redesign led with — dignity and desirability, and how the object looked to its owner; protection, monitoring and assistance; simplicity, fewer and larger controls; or connectivity, sensors, an app and remote visibility for family.

She had then to name the buyer and the promise. She could take the 60-to-70 group, the 75-and-over group, both at once across the whole €90 million, or the younger group first with the line extended to the older one later on one roadmap. The promise could be living well and still feeling oneself, staying safe and assisted, or clinical-grade care at home with the certifications that implied. She also set how tightly the line was built around one group's needs rather than averaged.

Third came the design call — the full redesign for a wide range of ability at the €1.1 million premium, a re-skin of the existing concept, or that same approach applied to selected touchpoints only, between the two on cost and depth. She split the €4.0 million across design and engineering, societal commitments and the commercial launch, and priced the lead product at €349, €229 or €149: the highest carried the widest margin per unit and the narrowest set of buyers, the lowest the widest reach, the thinnest margin and the longest recovery, the middle between them on all three.

Last, she had to tell the board where design had changed something — the object, the way it was bought and used, and the take-back arrangement behind it; the brand and the launch, with the object close to standard; or the framing of a launch that would otherwise have looked the same. She had to say how the three claims on the budget related: that dignity and co-design were what made the line wanted and therefore what made it pay; that both had been trimmed to protect payback; or that circularity and accessibility came first, with three years of losses. She had to name what beat the Scandinavian line — what the design said about its owner, more functions and sensors, or a lower price. Then: go, or no-go.

## Closing

Sequeira put the transcript face down and wrote two lines on the whiteboard: *who is this for*, and *what does it say about them*. The panel scores were pinned beside her desk — 8, 20, 15, and a fourth box she could not fill. Twelve weeks, four million euros, one slot.
