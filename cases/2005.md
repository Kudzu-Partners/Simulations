# Lumière Industrielle: The New CLO's First Board Cycle

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-2005 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2005 — *L&D Goes Strategic* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the second Tuesday of June 2026, Hélène Arnoux read two documents about her own function within an hour of each other. The first was the learning quarterly report she had inherited: 71% course completion, satisfaction 4.6 out of 5, the compliance catalogue refreshed on schedule. The second was a page from the group's chief financial officer proposing that the €4.2 million learning and development budget be cut by €1.4 million — one third — and the money redeployed to the commercial organisation.

Arnoux had been Chief Learning Officer of Lumière Industrielle SA for nine weeks. At the next board meeting, one budget cycle away, she would have three minutes to say what the function was for, which number it would move, and why €4.2 million was the right amount to spend on it. If the board was not persuaded, the cut would be voted and learning would open the following year at €2.8 million.

## The Protagonist

Hélène Arnoux, 46, had joined in April 2026 from a German building-systems manufacturer, where she had spent six years running commercial capability. She was not a career trainer: she had begun in field service, moved into key-account sales, and taken her first learning role at 38. What she had built there was small and specific — one programme, aimed at one product transition, measured against that product's order intake, defended twice at budget time and never cut.

At Lumière Industrielle she had inherited something larger and vaguer: 190 courses, four regional coordinators, a platform bought in 2023, and no line of her own in the operating review.

## The Decision

Arnoux had to decide, across one budget cycle, what the programme existed to do, how capability would be built, how she would prove it, and what she would say when the €1.4 million was pressed on her in the room.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumière Industrielle SA, headquartered in Lyon, designed, installed and serviced energy-efficiency and building-automation systems across France, Germany and Spain. Group revenue in 2025 was €680 million; the company employed 5,200 people, of whom roughly 1,400 sat in the commercial and field organisation — the people who quoted the work and delivered it.

For two years the group had been executing the shift the board called its first priority: away from equipment at a 14% gross margin, towards outcome-based service contracts in which Lumière Industrielle guaranteed an energy or availability result and was paid for holding it, at a 22% target margin. Finance's arithmetic put a 20% shift of group revenue onto outcome contracts at roughly €11 million a year in additional gross margin — more than twice the entire learning budget.

## The Situation

The shift was stalling in one place. Win rates on outcome-based proposals stood at 18% against a board target of 35%, and had not moved in three quarters. The contracts already won were over-running: engineers trained to install and repair equipment were scoping guarantees whose economics nobody had taught them to model, and salespeople who had quoted kit for fifteen years were being asked to sell a result. Arnoux's design lead put capability-building at roughly seven-tenths on the job, two-tenths through coaching and peers, one-tenth in a classroom.

Against that stood the report on Arnoux's desk (see **Exhibit 1**). Completion 71%. Satisfaction 4.6 out of 5. No entry anywhere connecting a euro of the €4.2 million to a euro of margin. The board secretariat's scorecard rated the function's strategic alignment — how tightly its programme was tied to one business metric — at 28 out of 100, and recorded that evaluation had never passed the first of four rungs. Rung one asked whether people liked the course; rung two, whether they had learned anything; rung three, whether they behaved differently in real deals; rung four, whether the business number moved. The CFO's standing description of the function, repeated at the last two operating reviews, was *sceptical*.

If nothing changed, the outcome was arithmetic rather than drama. The catalogue would run on at 71% completion, the €1.4 million would be voted out, the function would spend the following year at €2.8 million doing a smaller version of what it already did, and the win rate would stay at 18% — because nothing inside the €2.8 million was pointed at it.

## What Arnoux Controlled

**The goal.** She had to name one thing the programme existed to do. Four candidates: lift completion from 71% to 90%; raise satisfaction above 4.6; upskill the whole organisation, all 5,200 of them; or lift the services win rate from 18% toward the board's 35%. Two choices travelled with it — where to concentrate the programme (generic leadership and communication skills, compliance and product-feature training, or consultative selling, solution scoping and service economics for the 1,400), and what to do with a catalogue full of courses irrelevant to whichever goal she picked (keep it whole, add the new programme on top, or prune deliberately to release budget). She also had to put her name to a number: how many of the 17 percentage points between 18% and 35% she would close this cycle — none, all, or somewhere between.

**The design.** Where the bulk of capability got built was hers: three days of instructor-led classroom for all 1,400, at most of the budget and three selling days each; digital self-paced content, cheapest per learner and instantly scalable; or a blend weighted towards practice on live deals and manager reinforcement, with coaching above it and a thin layer of formal instruction. Coaching was the expensive, low-scale part: skip it, spread it evenly across all 1,400, or concentrate it on the front-line deal teams and scale the rest digitally. Then the line managers — ask nothing of them, send them a summary of what their people had been trained on, or require them to coach the deal reviews and own the application. And she had to size the commitment, anywhere from €1 million to €6 million against a €4.2 million budget.

**The evidence.** How far up the four rungs she would measure was a commitment, not an aspiration: whatever she promised, she would report. Beneath it sat the leading indicators she would publish quarterly, before the win rate could move — activity (courses delivered, hours, completion), reaction (satisfaction and net-promoter), or skill assessments and manager-observed behaviour in real deals. Then the question she expected from the CFO: how she knew any lift was the training. She could assert it, because the win rate rose after they trained; survey the sales force on how much it had helped; or compare a trained cohort against an untrained one. And she had to choose what return figure went in front of the board — none, on the view that learning impact could not be quantified; a headline "€20 per €1" from training hours and salary time saved; or programme cost against the share of the €11 million her evidence could claim.

**The room.** Three minutes. What she led with — activity, effort, or the business case. What she did when the €1.4 million was pressed: accept it, hand back half, defend the full €4.2 million on the return case, or argue for more. What she committed to and asked for: nothing beyond continuous improvement; quarterly completion and satisfaction dashboards with no asks; or quarterly business KPIs plus the executive sponsorship and manager release time the design required. And how firmly she was willing to state, in the room, that learning was an investment with a return rather than a discretionary cost.

She would be judged on five things, on the board's scorecard rather than hers (see **Exhibit 2**): the annual margin the board would credit to the programme, the alignment score, the evaluation rung reached, the budget standing at the end of the cycle, and the CFO's stated confidence. In June 2026 those stood at €0.0 million, 28, rung 1, €4.2 million, and sceptical.

## Closing

On the Friday, Arnoux cleared the whiteboard in her office and wrote two figures on it — €4.2 million on the left, €11 million on the right — and looked at the space between them. The board met in one cycle. The catalogue was still running, the win rate was still 18%, and the three minutes were not yet written.
