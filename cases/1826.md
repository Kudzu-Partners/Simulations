# Meridian Components: The Account No One Owned

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1826 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1826 — *OD Architect* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

---

On the morning of October 19, 2026, Grace Okafor read Atlas Manufacturing's termination letter to Meridian Components Corporation and knew at once it was not about parts. Atlas had bought precision components from Meridian for eleven years without returning one for a defect. It was leaving anyway, citing chronic order errors, replies that took days, and, in the departing buyer's own words, "no one who owns our problem." Atlas alone was worth twenty-two million dollars a year.

By lunchtime the CEO had called Okafor to his office. Three more customers, worth sixty million dollars between them, were due to renew within six months, and he did not intend to lose them as he had just lost Atlas. He handed her a one-time budget of one and a half million dollars and two quarters to close the gap in Customer Operations — with, as he put it on her way out, the freedom to spend it on the wrong things if she chose to.

## The Protagonist

Okafor, 44, had spent six years running continuous-improvement projects on Meridian's Cleveland shop floor before the CEO created a new role for her that spring: Director of Organizational Effectiveness, reporting to him directly, with a standing mandate to fix whichever part of the company was quietly costing it customers. She had never run a customer-facing unit, held a budget this size, or worked to a deadline this short, and her instinct was to trust a process map over a survey.

## The Decision

Okafor had to do four things in sequence and defend each to a CEO who had just watched one lost account cost him twenty-two million dollars: name what was actually broken; choose which interventions to fund inside a budget too small for everything on offer; decide the order to run them across six months without losing the plan when something went wrong mid-way; and land the change with 240 people and a skeptical CEO so it held after her project ended.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Components Corporation, founded in Cleveland, Ohio in 1971, made precision industrial components with 2,600 employees and 720 million dollars in annual revenue. Its Customer Operations unit — 240 people across order management, technical support, returns, and field service — was the only part of the company that touched a customer after a sale closed. In three years it had been reorganized twice, absorbed a bolt-on acquisition, and rolled out a new order-management system. Meridian's parts were rated among the best in the industry; its service around them was not, and the CEO had stopped calling that an HR problem the day Atlas cancelled.

## The Situation

The operational numbers were plain enough (Exhibit 1): an order-error rate of 6.8 percent, reply times customers had started to complain about, and an engagement survey on which only 51 percent of staff called themselves satisfied — the lowest score the company had recorded — against voluntary turnover the CEO now called unsustainable. What the numbers could not settle was why. Every error Okafor traced led somewhere different: to the two reorganizations, which had left roles overlapping and no one clearly accountable for a given account; to the order-to-resolution process and an order-management system configured for how the unit used to work rather than how it worked now; or to the unit's people — undertrained, unsupported, or simply making mistakes under pressure. The data was consistent with all three readings and ruled out none of them, and the budget would cover a serious response to only some of what she found. If the two quarters passed with no movement in the unit's performance, the three at-risk accounts would follow Atlas out the door, the CEO would lose patience, and a restructuring would be imposed over Okafor's head regardless of what she had recommended.

## What Okafor Controlled

Four numbers would judge her. A service-recovery score — error rate, response time, resolution speed, and satisfaction blended into one reading from zero to a hundred — opened at 25. An adoption score, separating real behavior change from compliance nobody would sustain once unwatched, opened at 12. A durability score, capturing whether her work would still stand after her project team dissolved, opened at 10. A dollar figure tracked how much of the $60 million at-risk revenue she actually kept.

Her first task was naming a cause: structural — unclear ownership and reporting lines left by the reorganizations; a broken process — the order-to-resolution workflow and the misconfigured system beneath it; a people problem — gaps in training and support across the unit; or individual employees making errors that firmer management could correct. Beneath the 6.8-percent figure specifically, she also had to name a reinforcing cause: an orphaned hand-off in returns each team assumed the other owned; the misconfigured system forcing manual workarounds; a skills gap across all 240 staff; or no deeper cause, the error rate itself being the thing to fix. A percentage set how rigorously her team would trace evidence to a root cause before naming a fix, rather than act on the first plausible story.

Next came the money: a costed menu of six interventions spanning three families — structural, process, and people (Exhibit 2) — of which she could fund up to four, spanning at least two families, inside the $1.5 million cap. She also had to state the logic behind the mix: that a multi-cause gap needed more than one cause addressed; that one well-chosen intervention would cascade and close the gap alone; or that the right move was whichever intervention moved the visible error rate fastest, regardless of family.

Sequencing came next: clarify structure first, then process, then capability; redesign the process first and settle ownership afterward; train the staff first on a process about to change under them; or launch every intervention at once. Separately, a communication-and-involvement budget of up to $300,000 would run across every intervention rather than inside any single one, and she had to decide whether to name measures and an owner per intervention before launch, track the four numbers monthly and assign owners later, or measure only at the end. Partway through, a key supervisor would resign and the vendor would raise its price for the reconfiguration work — a shock she could answer by holding her sequence, promoting someone internally, and drawing the cost from her lightest-touch intervention; by adding a fifth intervention to compensate; or by freezing the plan until the vacancy and the price were resolved.

Landing it was last. In a unit at 51-percent engagement, she could involve people in designing the change and coach the new role-holders; mandate the new way of working and audit compliance until the numbers moved; or announce it by email and a town hall and move on. What would make it hold after she left was a separate choice: embedding the new roles into job descriptions, the system, and incentive pay, each with a named owner; trusting the first quarter's momentum to carry the change forward without further reinforcement; or relaunching the same push every quarter, treating repetition itself as the mechanism that makes it stick. Asked whether her trajectory reflected real change or a number pushed to target, she could answer honestly from her leading measures; hit the target through audits and pressure; or point to an early dip in the error rate and call that the result. A final percentage set how firmly she would defend every dollar as protection of the at-risk revenue rather than as discretionary HR spending.

## Closing

At 8:15 the next morning, Okafor pinned Atlas's termination letter to the corkboard behind her desk, beside a blank sheet headed with the three words she still had to choose between. Two quarters had just become one hundred and eighty days. She had a budget, a mandate, and more ways to spend both badly than well.
