# Breaking the Ceiling: Marta Solé at Ibérica Materials Group

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1801 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1801 — *Breaking the Ceiling* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 28 September 2026, word reached Marta Solé that the informal slate to succeed Ibérica Materials Group's retiring Operations VP had already settled into an order, and that she was third on it. Two male peers were being discussed by the executives who would actually decide as the likely front-runners. The seat would open on 31 January 2027, and the board's nominations committee would settle the question in eight weeks. Marta had grown her division 22% and led the fastest-growing unit in the company. She was, by the numbers, the strongest of the three candidates. She was also, in the room where the decision would be made, running third.

One member of the nominations committee had already been quoted, secondhand, calling her "not quite ready." Marta had eight weeks to decide what to do about the gap between what she had delivered and what the committee currently believed about her, and, separately, whether to accept a stretch assignment that would come open within the same window and would have to be claimed or declined inside two weeks of its own.

## The Protagonist

Marta Solé, 43, ran Ibérica Materials Group's International Circular Materials division, the company's fastest-growing unit, grown 22% under her leadership. Colleagues and mentors spoke well of her; she had no shortage of advice. What she had less of were people willing to spend their own credibility arguing for her in rooms she did not enter. Her ties to the executives above her own function were uneven: some she had worked alongside for years, others she barely knew. In the past twelve months she had presented to IMG's full board once; each of her two peers on the succession slate had presented five and four times.

## The Decision

Marta's task, over the eight weeks before the nominations committee met, was to close the distance between her results and her standing. That meant, in sequence: diagnosing what was actually keeping her third; deciding how to spend a fixed, non-renewable pool of her own time and political capital; deciding what to do about a high-profile stretch assignment opening partway through the window; and, finally, deciding how to bring her case to the eleven people who would vote.

## The Company and its Setting

Ibérica Materials Group was a Madrid-headquartered industrial and circular-economy materials company with EUR 2.6 billion in revenue and 9,800 employees across Spain, Portugal, France, Mexico and Poland (see Exhibit 1). Its executive committee had eleven members, one of them a woman, even though women made up 44% of the workforce. IMG's chief executive had said publicly that the committee's composition would change within three years, and the board was watching whether this cycle moved that number or left it unchanged.

## The Situation

Marta's position going into the eight weeks was tracked on four numbers she could see throughout. Her perceived promotion readiness, meaning how ready the nominations committee judged her to be on a scale of 0 to 100, stood at 38; the seat required a reading of at least 65. Her sponsorship strength, a separate 0-to-10 measure of how many people would actively advocate for her as distinct from those who simply mentored her, stood at 3. Her network reach into the eleven-member committee stood at 40%, meaning most of the people who would vote did not know her well. A fourth measure, the quality of the terms of any stretch assignment she negotiated, started at zero, since none had yet been negotiated (see Exhibit 2).

If the weeks ahead brought more of the same — strong results, quiet mentorship, limited board exposure — the two peers already discussed as front-runners would remain front-runners, and Marta would be no closer to the seat when the committee met than she was on 28 September.

## What Marta Controlled

In the first stage, Marta had to name what was actually holding her back: that growing the division even faster would let her results carry the case; that the real shortfall was in who was willing to advocate for her in rooms she could not enter; that the timeline was fixed, making the next cycle the more realistic option; or that she needed to be more assertive in asking for the seat herself. She also had to decide how to read the comment that she was "not quite ready": as a fair assessment that she still had capability gaps to close, as personal friction with one executive rather than bias, as evidence of a broader pattern in how the committee weighed proven results against assumed potential, or as an offhand remark that carried no real signal. And she had to decide where to point the effort of mapping the committee: toward the specific executives who did not yet know her well, toward deepening relationships she already had within the committee, or toward trusting the formal promotion criteria to settle the matter. Underneath all three choices sat a fourth: how sharply, as a percentage, to separate what could change in eight weeks from what could not.

Marta then held a pool of 100 units of time and political capital to divide across four moves: turning a mentor into someone who would advocate for her directly inside committee discussions; securing a further, substantive board presentation; joining a steering group that crossed business lines; and building her reach toward committee members outside her current circle. Alongside that allocation, she had to decide how to frame credit for her division's growth: to claim it loudly and personally at every opportunity, to present it as the achievement of a team she led and could name, or to let it speak for itself without comment, and how visible to become overall, on a dial from a low profile to pursuing every available platform.

Partway through the eight weeks, a EUR 180 million mandate to integrate circular-economy operations across IMG's plants in Mexico and Poland came open, to be claimed or declined within two weeks. Marta had to decide whether to take it on exactly the terms offered, to decline it and pursue visibility another way, or to take it only after negotiating a more scoped and resourced version. If she took it, she still had to decide how hard to negotiate resources, authority and timeline; whether to accept a verbal understanding of success from her sponsor, insist on a written definition agreed by both her sponsor and the chief executive, or proceed without one, judging success by whatever results followed; and how to manage the geopolitical and operational risk in the integration, by assuming it would go smoothly, by delegating operational risk to the plant teams, or through a staged plan with contingencies built in.

In the final stage, Marta had to decide how her case would reach the nominations committee: as a results-only case built solely around her division's growth, through a series of private conversations with individual committee members, or as a single case combining her results, the stretch mandate, sponsor advocacy and network relationships. She had to decide what her sponsor would actually do when the committee met: mention her only if asked, name her and argue the case directly, or stay out of the discussion entirely, and what commitment, if any, she would make for the women below her in the pipeline: a structural sponsorship programme tied to the chief executive's own commitment, informal mentoring offered as opportunities happened to arise, or no separate pipeline commitment during this cycle. Last, she had to decide how much conviction to put into the three-minute pitch she would deliver herself, on a dial from restrained to maximal.

## Closing

That evening, Marta opened a blank page and wrote down four questions to answer before the committee met: what was actually keeping her third; how to spend a pool of capital she could not get back once spent; what to do about a mandate not yet offered but soon coming; and what case, exactly, eleven people would need to see to vote for her. She had results already behind her. She did not yet have a plan for the eight weeks ahead.
