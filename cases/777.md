# The Ledger: Five Briefs on the UK's External Accounts

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-777 · **Companion simulation:** Express 777 — *The Ledger: Balance of Payments & What It Really Means* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, September 7, 2026, at 07:50, Dr Clara Penhallow-Reid opened the external-accounts console at the Office for National Statistics (ONS) and found her week already decided for her. Sterling had fallen 10% since the spring. The Chancellor of the Exchequer was due to speak about the deficit the following Monday, and the Chancellor's private office had asked the ONS for its reading of the external accounts, and for a recommendation, by 17:00 on Friday, September 11.

Penhallow-Reid, the ONS's Chief Statistician, split the request into five briefs, one for each working day. Monday's was bookkeeping: five transactions to place in the ledger. Friday's was the recommendation itself: two policy levers and one judgment about what mattered most. Between them sat the trade figures, the question of how the deficit was being financed, and a forecast of what the cheaper pound would do.

## The Protagonist

Penhallow-Reid was forty-four. She had joined the ONS as a graduate national accountant and spent eleven years compiling the quarterly balance of payments, the record of every transaction between UK residents and the rest of the world, before taking over the methods team. She had been Chief Statistician since June. She knew the accounts line by line. She had never put the office's name to a policy recommendation, and several of her colleagues believed the office should not have been asked for one.

## The Decision

Five times in five days, Penhallow-Reid had to commit the office's reading to paper (see Exhibit 2). Each brief left the building on its own day. They were separate pieces of work, but the Treasury would read them together, and the last one would carry her signature into the Chancellor's office. The private office had been precise about it. It wanted two levers, no more and no fewer, and one factor named as the one that mattered most for whether the deficit could be sustained.

## The Company and its Setting

The ONS was the UK's largest producer of official statistics and worked at arm's length from ministers. Its balance-of-payments team recorded every cross-border transaction twice, once as the flow itself and once as the matching payment or claim, so that the two sides of the ledger summed to zero by construction. The current account carried four lines: goods, services, primary income and secondary income. The financial account carried four more: direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment and reserve assets. Where a transaction landed decided which story the headline figures told.

The UK had run a current-account deficit in most years for four decades. It sold more services abroad than it bought, bought more goods than it sold, and was home to one of the world's largest financial centres. Each year the gap had been financed by foreign money arriving in some form: companies building or buying in the UK, investors buying government bonds (gilts) and shares, banks lending across borders.

## The Situation

The figures on the console at 07:50 were the office's latest estimates (see Exhibit 1). Trade in goods showed a deficit of £150 billion. Trade in services showed a surplus of £90 billion. The current account as a whole was in deficit by 3.8% of gross domestic product (GDP). Of the money financing that deficit, 60% had arrived as portfolio flows, 25% as foreign direct investment and 15% as bank lending. The partner and sector tables from the same release were open on her second screen.

Sterling's 10% fall had made UK goods cheaper abroad and foreign goods dearer at home. The Treasury's covering note asked the ONS to say which way net trade would move three, twelve and eighteen months after the fall, and whether, over the long run, the fall would improve the trade balance at all.

By Monday morning every lever in front of the Chancellor had a sponsor. A City economist's note, copied to the Treasury, argued that the deficit might be benign if it stayed well financed, and was best left alone. The business department wanted supply-side reform to make exporters more competitive. The investment office wanted tax and regulatory changes to draw in more direct investment. A special adviser had written that the fall in sterling should be pushed further and faster. A group of backbench MPs had asked the Chancellor for tariffs on imports. A fiscal think tank had called for sharp austerity to bring domestic demand, and imports with it, down.

If Penhallow-Reid sent nothing, the Chancellor would speak on September 14 without the office's reading. The deficit would still stand at 3.8% of GDP, financed in the same proportions, and the depreciation was already in the prices.

Every brief went through the office's methods review before it left. Gareth Ainsley-Moore, who had succeeded her as head of methods, marked each brief out of 100 against the review sheet, counted how many of its items held up, and wrote a one-line comment beside the mark. He kept a running total for the week, out of 500, which placed the work on the office's four-rung grading ladder, from trainee through statistician and analyst to chief economist, and earned it up to three stars. On Monday morning the total stood at 0 and the grade at trainee.

## What Penhallow-Reid Controlled

On Monday she placed each of five transactions in one of the eight sub-accounts: on the current side, goods, services, primary income or secondary income; on the financial side, direct investment, portfolio investment, other investment or reserve assets. Kestwick Aero, a UK manufacturer, had exported an aircraft engine to Rimal Airways, an overseas carrier, for £50 million. A US firm had paid a London law firm £20 million for international legal advice. A UK pensioner had received £5 million in dividends on shares in a US technology company. The UK government had sent £15 million in development aid to Kenya. A German firm had bought 100% of a UK engineering company for £200 million.

On Tuesday she set three entries. The first was the net trade balance, goods and services together, in whole billions of pounds, with a minus sign for a deficit. The second was the sector she judged was carrying the services surplus: finance and professional services, manufacturing, tourism or agriculture. The third was the partner region behind the largest goods deficit: the European Union, the United States, China or Japan.

On Wednesday she tagged four kinds of financing as stable or volatile: inbound direct investment, meaning foreign firms building factories or buying UK businesses outright; portfolio inflows, meaning overseas purchases of gilts and listed shares; short-term lending between banks in London and their overseas branches; and long-term infrastructure bonds bought by foreign pension funds. She then gave the office's verdict on the deficit: robust, with a low risk that financing would stop suddenly; mixed, manageable but worth monitoring; or vulnerable, with portfolio money dominant and a sudden stop possible.

On Thursday she forecast the direction of net trade at month 3, month 12 and month 18 after the fall: improving, broadly unchanged or worsening. She also answered the Treasury's long-run question, yes or no: whether exports and imports, taken together, would respond strongly enough to the cheaper pound for the depreciation to improve the trade balance.

On Friday she chose two of six levers for the Chancellor: do nothing, on the view that a well-financed deficit might be benign; boost export competitiveness through supply-side reform; attract more direct investment through tax and regulatory reform; engineer a sharp depreciation of sterling; impose import tariffs; or launch sharp fiscal austerity. Alongside the two, she named the single factor she judged mattered most for whether the deficit could be sustained: its size, the composition of its financing, the level of the exchange rate, or the gap between UK and overseas interest rates.

## Closing

At 08:05 Penhallow-Reid pulled the five transactions onto the left of her screen and the blank ledger onto the right. The engine, the legal fee, the dividends, the aid and the acquisition sat in a column, each waiting for a line. Ainsley-Moore's review sheet was open in another window with the total at 0. The first brief was due at the Treasury by the end of the day, and the Chancellor's speech was a week away.
