# Pennine Drive Systems: Into the Deep End

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1821 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1821 — *Deep End* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 2 November 2026, Naomi Ashworth's first week as Managing Director of Pennine Drive Systems' Drivetrain Division produced two documents addressed to the same ninety days. The first was a cure notice from the division's anchor customer, a European rail original-equipment manufacturer with a £34 million, three-year contract on Pennine's electric-drivetrain programme: deliver one qualified production unit within ninety days, or the contract would be cancelled. The second was a note from the Pennine board, setting its own review of her strategic plan for the division on that same clock.

Ashworth had to produce both a plan the board would back and a unit the customer would accept, from a division that had already spent £26 million on the transition and missed two launch milestones before she arrived. Four decision points lay ahead over the ninety days — set a direction, choose a posture for the crisis, appoint someone to lead the qualification sprint, then deliver — and the customer's clock and the board's clock would not be negotiated separately.

## The Protagonist

Naomi Ashworth, 43, had spent most of her career at Pennine Drive Systems as a production and manufacturing engineer, rising to run engineering delivery on the company's established gearbox lines. The Drivetrain Division's managing director's chair was her first general-management appointment: her first time owning a board relationship, a P&L for a business still finding its market, and a customer relationship already in default. The division's previous managing director was gone, and Ashworth had inherited a 220-person team she barely knew in the same week the cure notice arrived.

## The Decision

Over the ninety days, tied to the same clock, Ashworth had to set a direction for the division and prove she could deliver against it.

The first decision was immediate: why had the drivetrain programme missed its launch twice — a missing skill (the senior power-electronics expertise the team had never hired), pace (a sound plan worked too slowly), people (the previous managing director and a handful of underperforming engineers, replaceable), or both the missing expertise and a focus spread too thin across competing priorities? From whichever diagnosis she chose, Ashworth drafted a one-line strategic intent: a targeted claim to build trusted, scalable electric drivetrains; a sweeping ambition to reinvent global mobility and decarbonise the planet; a financial target framed around the quarter's numbers and margin; or no statement of intent at all, until the contract itself was fixed. She named the single most important outcome for the next ninety days — the qualified unit alone, everything at once (the unit, a refreshed roadmap, a restructured team and lower costs), or rebuilding morale before the unit — and decided how decisively to stop lower-priority work, from barely touching it to stopping most of it, to free capacity for that outcome.

Ten days in, the cure notice forced a posture: ask the customer for time to co-develop a next-generation roadmap; lock scope and run a delivery sprint to the unit, with strategy to follow; or run that same sprint while presenting it, to customer and board alike, as the first step of the transition. Alongside the posture came the recovery budget — up to £3.5 million split across the missing external power-electronics expertise, a qualification test rig and accelerated testing, and engineering overtime and contractor hands — against a full, do-everything plan costing £6.2 million. And she decided what she told each audience: one consistent account to both; a promise of the full strategy to the board, an early unit to the customer and no overrun to either; or two separate accounts, kept apart.

Round three was the appointment: a managerial deputy with a solid delivery record who had never led anything this demanding, or a high-potential engineer with real ability and ambition who had never led a sprint at all. Whoever she chose, Ashworth decided how much real authority came with it — full control over scope, spend and the team inside clear guardrails; the title alone, with every decision still hers; or complete free rein with no involvement from her — how much support, from minimal to extensive, to build in mentoring, an escalation path and cover for their existing responsibilities, and what happened at a day-forty-five checkpoint: agreed warning signs and a plan to add support without changing leaders, no checkpoint at all, or a standing plan to swap in the deputy at the first sign of trouble.

By round four, sixty of the ninety days were gone and the board wanted a progress read. Ashworth decided how to recover whichever piece of the programme had fallen behind — redirect the external expertise and test rig onto it, leaving the rest of scope untouched; remove it from the unit's scope without raising the change with the customer; or spend beyond the £3.5 million authority and trigger the group's capital review. She decided how to work with whoever she had appointed if they were struggling — stay alongside them and let them lead the recovery, take direct control of it herself, or decide the appointment had not worked out and hand the sprint to the deputy. For the division's flight-risk engineers — the group Exhibit 1 had valued at £900,000 — she chose a targeted offer of role, growth and recognition tied to the transition story, a blanket pay counter-offer open to anyone who threatened to leave, or no specific retention move, attention staying on the unit alone. And she decided how to brief the board — stating the unit's status and the recovered work plainly, tied to the direction she had set; claiming complete success with a promise to deliver the rest of the roadmap next quarter too; or a delivery timeline alone, without reference to direction — and how firmly to argue, in that meeting, that a developed leader and a credible direction mattered more than a rescue would have.

## The Company and its Setting

Pennine Drive Systems Ltd was a Sheffield-based precision-gearbox manufacturer with group revenue of £210 million. Its Drivetrain Division — 220 people — carried the company's bet on the energy transition: an electric-drivetrain product line that had already burned £26 million and missed two launch milestones before Ashworth's appointment, with the division's engagement score down to 58 out of 100. The division's previous managing director was gone, and the board's patience for a third missed milestone was already thin.

## The Situation

Exhibit 1 set out the position Ashworth inherited. She could spend up to £3.5 million on recovery before triggering a group-level capital review — well short of the £6.2 million a plan addressing every problem at once would have cost. Three senior engineers, whose combined replacement cost ran to £900,000, were flight risks.

If nothing changed — no direction named, no recovery funded, no one appointed to lead the sprint — the cure notice's own terms made the consequence explicit: no qualified unit inside ninety days meant the anchor contract, and the transition bet built on it, would be cancelled. The board's review would land on the same calendar regardless of whether a plan existed to show it.

## What Ashworth Controlled

Four measures moved against her decisions every round (Exhibit 2): Strategy Clarity, a read on whether the division had a visionary direction the board could back, starting at 20 of 100; Execution Rate, progress toward the qualified unit, starting at 25; Talent Readiness, whether a future leader was being developed rather than drowned, starting at 30; and Stakeholder Confidence, board and customer belief in her mandate, starting at 40. None of the four could substitute for another — a division could score well on delivery and badly on direction, or the reverse, and the ninety days would judge both.

## Closing

At the end of her first Monday, Ashworth pinned the cure notice to the wall behind her desk, next to a blank sheet headed only "Direction." Both clocks — the customer's and the board's — read ninety days. She had a diagnosis to make before the day was out, a phone list with a proven deputy's extension and a high-potential engineer's extension on it, and £3.5 million she had not yet decided how to spend.
