# Logika Solutions: The Frozen Envelope

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1972 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1972 — *Reward Reset* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 12 January 2026, Mojca Hribar counted four resignation letters on her desk and recognised every name. All four were senior engineers. All four were going to the same Vienna firm, which paid an outcome-linked bonus and published a progression ladder. All four had given the same two reasons in their exit conversations: their pay did not track what they contributed, and they could not see where the next five years went.

Nine days earlier, Andrej Klemenčič, Logika Solutions' chief executive, had told the January board that the company would stop selling engineers by the hour and start selling products and platforms. The board's instruction to Hribar came out of the same meeting, and one clause in it was not negotiable: redesign pay and performance to fit the new strategy, and do it inside the existing compensation envelope of €49.5 million. She had until the April board to put the architecture in front of them.

## The Protagonist

Mojca Hribar, 44, had been HR Director of Logika Solutions since 2022. She had joined in 2016 as Compensation and Benefits Manager, from a Slovenian retail bank where reward had meant collective agreements and service increments. She had drawn Logika's current salary bands herself in 2018, and in the eight years since had signed off a long series of off-cycle raises, each granted to hold a particular person, that had pulled those bands out of shape. Whatever a pay audit found in the legacy data, it would find it in decisions carrying her signature. Her own bonus, like that of every director, was calculated on billable utilisation across the delivery organisation.

## The Decision

Hribar had to decide what Logika paid people for, and how much of a fixed €49.5 million sat behind each answer. She would present the architecture to the board in April, and return twelve months later with four numbers showing whether it had worked. Between those two dates the design would go live and an external pay audit would report.

## The Company and its Setting

Logika Solutions d.o.o. was a Ljubljana software and information-technology outsourcing firm, spun out of a state computing bureau and re-founded as a private company in 1994. Its delivery teams served banking, telecom and public-sector clients across Central Europe and the German-speaking markets. In 2025 it reported revenue of €96 million at an 11% operating margin, and employed 720 people, 540 of them engineers and consultants.

It still paid them broadly the way the bureau had. Only 8% of total compensation was variable, and the bonus pool that funded it was triggered by billable hours and tenure. Regional technology peers ran 18–22% of compensation as variable pay. The product-and-platform strategy Klemenčič had committed to needed ownership of outcomes, continuous upskilling and results the client could see — none of which the pay system measured.

## The Situation

The four board measures Hribar reported on all pointed the same way (see **Exhibit 1**). The engagement survey scored 58 out of 100 against a regional technology benchmark of about 72, and only 41% of staff agreed that their pay reflected their performance. Voluntary turnover among mid-level engineers was 21%; the finance team costed each regretted exit at €38,000 in recruitment, ramp-up and lost delivery, roughly €4.3 million a year at that rate. An internal review had found an unexplained gender pay gap of 11% at engineer grades, together with inconsistent pay for equal work built up over years of ad-hoc raises — a gap that European pay-transparency rules would make visible to every employee. The fourth measure was newer: a strategy-fit index the board had asked Hribar to maintain, a 0-to-100 read on how far what the pay system rewarded matched what the strategy required. It stood at 30.

Tanja Rupnik, the finance director, had put a number on inaction at the January board: on current attrition and current engagement, she projected three points of operating margin gone within twelve months. She had also closed the other exit. The envelope was €49.5 million and it was frozen (see **Exhibit 2**); anything Hribar added to variable pay or development had to come out of base.

Vlado Jereb, who chaired the works council, had already asked for the numbers. Roughly 130 people would be worse off on day one under any re-mix that moved money out of legacy base pay, and he wanted to know who they were before they did.

## What Hribar Controlled

Her first task was the diagnosis she would take to the board, and it turned on which reading of the problem she backed: that base salaries sat below the Vienna competitor's and had to be matched; that the pay system measured billable hours and tenure while the strategy needed ownership, upskilling and client outcomes; or that reward design was sound and the weakness lay in line managers. She had to choose how to frame the cost of doing nothing — as a normal cost of the sector, as lost billable hours from under-utilised leavers, or as Rupnik's €4.3 million and three margin points. She had to place the 11% gap somewhere in that diagnosis: as a precondition to fix regardless of strategy, as a question to revisit once the incentive work was done, or as legacy salaries best left untouched. And she had to decide how much of the 720-person workforce she actually segmented and analysed before deciding anything, from none of it to all of it.

The architecture itself was four settings. The variable share of the envelope could be set anywhere from its current 8% up to 30%. The metric set that triggered it could be individual billable hours, the measure already in place and cleanly counted; an individual ranking that paid top performers and rank-ordered the rest; or a combined set of team delivery quality, client outcomes, skill acquisition and company margin. Between €0 and €6 million of the envelope could be redirected into certifications, training and published progression paths. And base pay could rise 6% across the board to match Vienna, hold flat and be re-mixed within itself, or be re-mixed with differentiation — trimming over-market legacy salaries while protecting under-market and critical-skill ones.

Six months after go-live she would review it. The pay audit would report by then, and she could grandfather the legacy salaries, adjust only for new hires, or re-band to equal pay for equal work on a funded, phased plan. If the new awards had produced internal competition, she could read that as effort and leave the design alone, weight further toward individual awards, or re-weight toward team delivery and client outcomes. She had to settle how the change reached people — issued as a spreadsheet on a set date, with the works council taking it up after the fact; announced on day one with no transition period, and the new rules applying to everyone at once; or taken to Jereb in advance, with a transition for the 130 and a published account of gains and losses — and how much consultation time she built in, from none to a full programme.

What remained was the January 2027 board. Hribar would lead the scorecard with the envelope held, with whichever of the four numbers looked strongest, or with the system and the cause-and-effect logic behind all four. She would state a one-line reward philosophy: that cash was the lever that mattered and the other elements followed it, that reward was a cost to be held down and managed against the envelope, or that pay, metrics and development had to reinforce each other and the strategy on an equitable base. And Klemenčič would ask what she had taken away to fund what she added — a question she could leave unanswered in the meeting, attribute to the frozen envelope Rupnik had set, or answer by naming the re-mix and the transition built for the 130.

## Closing

Hribar cleared her desk and wrote the four segments of the envelope across a single sheet: base, variable, development, benefits. The April board was thirteen weeks out. Whatever she wrote above the line, the number at the bottom of the sheet would still read €49.5 million.
