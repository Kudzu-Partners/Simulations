## Abstract

EcoLitio S.A., a Chilean lithium developer backed by a consortium of Chilean and international investors, had been awarded, in March 2026, the exploration-to-development concession for Proyecto Salar Larache, a brine deposit of 380,000 tonnes LCE in the Tarapacá region of northern Chile. To convert exploration into a binding development agreement, EcoLitio had to secure alignment from four non-aligned stakeholders: CORMIN (the national mining regulator); the Larache indigenous community (140 families, ancestral claim on water); a syndicate of three international institutional investors representing USD 480M of the project's USD 820M capex; and an NGO coalition led by Agua Andina, which had signalled intent to seek a judicial injunction. Each stakeholder held a veto. The Chief Negotiator, Valentina Soria, had been given four rounds — roughly six months calendar — to move each stakeholder's trust index from its entry level (CORMIN 62, Community 28, Investors 55, NGOs 18) to the combined threshold that would permit a signable deal. The case stages Round 1 and the single question Soria must answer before she enters any room: *which table do I go to first, and with what?*

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the sequencing of multi-party negotiations and the downstream consequences of first-move choice.
2. Analyze how BATNA asymmetry shapes concession design across four non-aligned tables.
3. Design a coalition strategy that isolates veto-holding counterparts without alienating them.
4. Diagnose the interface between commercial, regulatory, and social-license instruments in a deal architecture.
5. Recommend a Round-1 stance for each of the four tables and articulate the implicit deal architecture that results.

On the evening of **April 14, 2026**, **Valentina Soria** sat in the Chief Negotiator's office of EcoLitio S.A., eleven floors above the Alameda in Santiago de Chile, and spread across her desk four folders colored differently — green, red, blue, and black. On each folder she had written, that morning, a single number. *CORMIN: 62. Community: 28. Investors: 55. NGOs: 18.* She had four rounds. She had one week to choose which folder to open first.

---

## The Protagonist

Valentina Soria, 43, had been named EcoLitio's Chief Negotiator in February 2026. Before EcoLitio she had been Deputy Minister for Mining Affairs in Chile for two years, and, prior to that, an M&A lawyer specializing in resource-sector cross-border transactions at a Santiago-based boutique. She held a law degree from the Pontificia Universidad Católica and an LL.M. from Columbia. She had been recruited on the strength of three priors: she had negotiated, as Deputy Minister, the consultation protocols that had underpinned two recent lithium development approvals; she had a personal relationship with the President of the Larache community's traditional council, dating to a 2021 interministerial visit; and she had been, before her time in government, the lead external counsel to Agua Andina.

Her contract carried a two-year performance gate tied to execution of a development agreement, subject to a signed community-consultation protocol and to a non-reopener by the regulator within twelve months of signature.

---

## The Decision

By **April 21, 2026**, Soria had to table Round-1 positions at four tables. At each table she would choose from a set of moves:

- **CORMIN (Regulator, trust 62):** pre-comply with an anticipated water-use guideline (high cost, high trust); propose a voluntary monitoring regime; offer nothing beyond the concession terms.
- **Larache Community (trust 28):** propose a benefit-sharing package framed in royalty points on LCE; propose a revenue-neutral equity position in a downstream JV; propose a water-rights co-management protocol; open with an apology for 2018 (a pre-award, adjacent-project conflict).
- **Investors (trust 55):** reaffirm the USD 820M capex envelope and timeline (signals discipline); propose a contingent-equity structure tied to community and NGO milestones (signals risk-sharing); request a USD 60M cost-overrun buffer (signals caution, risks confidence).
- **NGOs (trust 18):** open a bilateral technical dialogue; commission a third-party environmental-flow study at EcoLitio's expense; offer to publish baseline hydrological data; hold silence and litigate if sued.

Soria would, after Round 1, also declare a **deal architecture** that ran through all four tables: *Regulator-first (align CORMIN, drag the rest), Community-first (earn social license, drag CORMIN), Investor-first (secure money, pay out of it), or Broad Simultaneity (move all four in parallel).*

---

## Company Background

EcoLitio S.A. had been incorporated in 2023 as a Chilean development company with a single purpose: to develop the Salar Larache lithium resource under a concession awarded by CORMIN in March 2026. Its shareholding base was 60% Chilean (two local family offices and a pension-fund vehicle), 40% international (a Canadian specialty-metals fund, a U.S. strategic automotive off-taker, and a European development-finance institution). Its paid-in capital at concession-award date was USD 140M; its development-phase capex envelope stood at USD 820M over four years.

The Salar Larache resource had been independently estimated at 380,000 tonnes LCE, with a projected production run-rate of 28,000 tonnes per annum LCE from 2029 and a life of resource of roughly fourteen years. The project's lowest-cost brine-processing design required 42 liters per second of combined fresh and brine water over the life of the operation, of which approximately 9 liters per second came from an aquifer whose hydrogeological continuity with the Larache community's ancestral spring had not been independently established.

## Industry and Market Context

The global lithium market had, in the three years preceding April 2026, experienced two complete cycles: a price spike to USD 82,000/t LCE in 2022, a decline to USD 14,000/t by mid-2024, and a recovery to USD 26,000/t by Q1 2026. Long-term analyst consensus placed the 2028–2032 price in a USD 20,000–32,000/t band under a base-case electrification trajectory.

Chile held approximately 35% of global lithium reserves and was the second-largest producer. The Chilean regulatory regime had moved in 2024 from a concession framework to a public-private partnership framework for new resources, under which the state took a minimum equity participation of 25% through the Corporación Nacional del Cobre (CODELCO). Community-consultation protocols required under ILO Convention 169 had become binding for all lithium development approvals. Judicial injunctions, sought by environmental NGO coalitions, had materially slowed two of the six most recent lithium development projects and had been dispositive in one.

Comparable deals in 2024–2025 had, for projects of similar scale, closed at community royalty rates of 1.5–2.8%, water-rights co-management in two of three cases, and contingent equity structures tied to community and environmental milestones in three of three cases.

---

## The Situation

### How we got here

EcoLitio had been awarded the concession in a competitive process in March 2026, eleven months after its initial application. During the competitive phase, three decisions had constrained Soria's hand at the start of Round 1. First, EcoLitio had pledged, in its bid, to deliver first production by Q3 2029 — a timeline the internal operations team now viewed as contingent on a Q3 2026 development-agreement signature. Second, the investor syndicate's term sheet had been structured around a USD 820M envelope with a capex-overrun tolerance of 7.5%, beyond which an investor exit right activated. Third, the Larache community, in a formal response to the pre-award consultation, had noted that "no benefit-sharing offer has been made to this community by this company."

### What Soria knew

The internal negotiation team had mapped the four stakeholders' entry trust indices; their stated BATNAs (regulator: delay; community: injunction via NGO ally; investors: exit; NGOs: litigation); and their internal divisions (CORMIN: pro-development middle tier, cautious leadership; community: three factions inside the traditional council; investors: the DFI most exposed to reputational risk; NGOs: Agua Andina willing to dialogue, two coalition members willing to litigate directly).

### What she did not know

Whether the hydrogeological continuity study, which was in peer review, would find that the ancestral spring and the project aquifer were connected. Whether the Canadian fund would accept a contingent-equity structure. Whether the community's incoming council election, scheduled for July 2026, would return the current pro-dialogue president.

### What others were saying

**Patricio Roldán**, CEO, said: *"We need a signable deal by end of Q3. I do not care which table moves first. I care which table moves last."*

**Cecilia Alarcón**, President of the Larache Traditional Council, had told Soria on a call on April 10: *"I can talk. My three colleagues on the council cannot yet."*

**Tomas Ferrán**, Director, Agua Andina: *"We will not litigate a project that publishes its hydrology. We will litigate one that does not."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — Regulator-First:** Align CORMIN early on an expanded monitoring protocol; let CORMIN's endorsement drag the community and NGOs.
- **Option B — Community-First:** Table a benefit-sharing package and a water-rights co-management protocol with the Larache community in Round 1; let that social license unlock the regulator and constrain the NGOs.
- **Option C — Investor-First:** Secure the contingent-equity structure and the 7.5% overrun buffer; pay for community and NGO concessions from investor-approved contingency.
- **Option D — Broad Simultaneity:** Table moves at all four tables in Round 1, paced identically; forgo any one table's leverage to move the others.

Soria turned the four folders over, stacked them in the order she intended to open them, and wrote, at the top of her legal pad, a single word: *second.* She intended, she realized, to go to the community second, not first. She did not yet know whether she was right.
