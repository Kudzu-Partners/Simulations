# Nordic Home Essentials: Six Readings of Colombia

**Case Code:** STR-2026-635 · **Companion simulation:** Express 635 — *PESTEL Analysis Colombia* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Wednesday, April 15, 2026, Mette Kjær Andersen, head of strategy for Latin America at Nordic Home Essentials, opened the Colombia file in her Mexico City office for what the group's board had said would be the last time. The board wanted a recommendation on entering Colombia at its June meeting, sixty days away, and it wanted the case built country-first: what Colombia offered, what it threatened, and what the company would do about each.

Andersen had to work through the country one front at a time, political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal, and for each one decide how attractive it was, how risky, how hard the company should lean in, and how much of a USD 5 million pre-entry fund to commit to it. Whatever she spent on one front was no longer available for the next.

## The Protagonist

Andersen was forty-four and Danish. She had joined Nordic Home Essentials in 2012 in Copenhagen, moved to Mexico in 2019 to run the integration of a local acquisition, and had led the group's entries into Chile and Peru. She knew consumer goods and she knew the region. She had never lived in Colombia, and the two people she leaned on most for it were Lars Holm, the regional finance director, and Natalia Pineda, a Bogotá lawyer the group had retained for the assessment.

## The Decision

The assessment would move through six fronts in a fixed order (see **Exhibit 1**). On each, Andersen would rate the front's attractiveness to the company and its risk, choose the stance the entry plan would take on it, and allocate money from the fund to preparing for it and reducing its risk. The six readings would then be rolled into a single verdict for the board.

## The Company and its Setting

Nordic Home Essentials was a Copenhagen-based maker of household cleaning, personal care and packaged food products, founded in 1962 and present in thirty-eight countries. In Latin America it operated in Mexico, Brazil, Chile and Peru. Colombia had been on the group's list since 2022: a country of 52 million people, the fourth-largest economy in the region, with a young and increasingly urban population. Every regional plan since then had included it, and every plan had deferred it.

The board had approved a USD 5 million fund for the pre-entry phase. It covered the work that would have to be done before a single product reached a shelf: government and regulatory engagement, currency and pricing studies, consumer research, digital and payments groundwork, environmental permits and packaging compliance, and legal and labour due diligence.

## The Situation

The country file was thick and did not point one way (see **Exhibit 1**). On the political front, Colombia had strengthened its democratic institutions over two decades, promoted foreign direct investment actively, and held free trade agreements with the United States, the European Union and its Pacific Alliance partners. Regulation could change with each new administration, regional governance was uneven, and the country ranked 87th in the global corruption perception index.

On the economic front, growth had averaged 3–4% a year and inflation had settled at around 5–6% after recent highs. A middle class of more than 30% of the population was expanding. The Colombian peso moved sharply against the US dollar, which Holm flagged as the first number any board member would ask about.

The social picture offered a median age of 31 and an 82% urban population concentrated in Bogotá, Medellín, Cali and Barranquilla. Consumers leaned toward value-for-money brands, while demand for premium and international products was growing. More than three-quarters of the population used social media, and purchasing habits differed markedly from region to region.

Technologically, 75% of Colombians used the internet and 85% had mobile connectivity. E-commerce had grown by more than 30% a year and digital payments were spreading, but rural areas still had gaps, and Bogotá and Medellín had some of the region's most active start-up scenes.

On the environmental front, Colombia was among the most biodiverse countries in the world. Major projects required an environmental licence that could take six to twelve months. Water use was tightly regulated in some regions, packaging and waste rules were moving toward a circular economy, and urban consumers were paying closer attention to sustainability.

The legal front, Pineda's territory, combined a civil-law system with a labour code that protected employees through severance, social security contributions and limits on dismissal. Intellectual property was protected by international treaty, though enforcement varied. A company could be registered in ten to fifteen business days. The corporate income tax rate was 35%, transfer pricing followed the OECD guidelines, and commercial arbitration clauses were enforceable.

Holm's view was that the fund should be concentrated where the risk was highest. Pineda's view was that every front would need some of it before the lawyers would sign off. The working template her team had prepared set every rating at 5 on a scale of 1 to 10 and pencilled in USD 500,000 for each front. If Andersen accepted it as it stood, the board would receive six identical middle ratings and USD 3 million spread evenly across the six fronts, whatever the file said about each one.

## What Andersen Controlled

On each of the six fronts, Andersen set four things. The **attractiveness rating**, from 1 to 10: how much that front of the country favoured the company's entry. The **risk rating**, from 1 to 10: how exposed the company would be on that front. The **strategic response**: aggressive investment, a moderate approach, a cautious entry, or avoiding and deferring that part of the entry. And the **budget allocation**: how many dollars, in steps of USD 50,000 and up to USD 2 million on any one front, to commit from the fund to preparation and mitigation there. The allocation could never exceed what remained in the fund.

After each front, the assessment scorecard (see **Exhibit 2**) recorded the ratings, the stance, the money committed and a net score for that front, and the risk that remained once the committed money was taken into account. Four figures sat at the top of the scorecard throughout: an average market score across the fronts assessed so far, an average risk index after mitigation, the budget remaining in the fund, and an entry-readiness percentage. After the sixth front, the scorecard would resolve into one of four verdicts for the board, from not recommended to highly attractive with full entry recommended.

## Closing

At 18:40 Mexico City time, Holm's message arrived: "Political first. Remember the board will read the peso before it reads anything else." Andersen opened the political section, set the template aside, and looked at the fund balance: USD 5,000,000.
