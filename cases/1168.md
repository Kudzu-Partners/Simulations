# Mesa Constituyente del Aguarales: The Defender's Chair

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1168 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1168 — *Applied Political Theory: Liberalism, Republicanism, Deliberative Democracy* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 08:40 on Monday, June 22, 2026, Valeria Cuéllar-Ibarra took the third chair at the panel table of the Instituto de Estudios Políticos of the Universidad Nacional del Aguarales (UNA). In front of her lay a single form from the Mesa Constituyente's secretariat with three boxes on it, and a blank sheet headed *Artículo 87*. At the head of the table sat Doña Helena Robledo-Castaño, the Instituto's director, who would moderate the debate and judge it. Her standing question had already gone round the capital: *"Profesora, ¿cómo se traduce eso en un artículo constitucional?"*

Before Cuéllar-Ibarra spoke on the electoral system, she had to tick one box: the political tradition she would defend. Once she spoke, the choice held for the whole debate. Over three rounds she would file twelve draft articles, and the transcript would go to all 120 constituyentes before their binding vote on the Constitución's foundational architecture, three weeks away.

## The Protagonist

Cuéllar-Ibarra was forty-four, a professor of political theory in UNA's law faculty. For eleven years she had taught the country's three rival constitutional traditions side by side, in one seminar, to students who went on to staff all three coalitions. She had advised none of them. That was why Robledo-Castaño had asked her. The two other chairs went to colleagues who would defend whichever traditions she left, and who would cross-examine her in the second round.

## The Decision

Cuéllar-Ibarra had three things to settle. The first was which tradition to defend. The second, twelve times over, was what kind of article to put on the table. The third, each time, was whether to say on the record what that article left out or cost. Each draft would be judged as soon as it was read.

## The Company and its Setting

The República del Aguarales had about 14 million people. It had come out of thirty years of internal conflict through a 2024 peace accord that demobilised three regional armed groups. Eight indigenous nations, recognised in law, made up 22% of the population. About 40% of indigenous Aguareños lived in territories where indigenous law and ordinary law overlapped. The public sphere had been restricted for three decades, and in parts of the country the State had been absent for a generation (see Exhibit 1).

The Mesa Constituyente had been convened in June 2026 under a transitional law, with nine months to draft and approve a new Constitución. Its 120 elected members had formed three coalitions, and indigenous and rural sub-coalitions moved between them. Each coalition had tabled its own proposal, its *propuesta radicada*. The Mesa had asked the Instituto to argue out the disagreement in public before the coalitions locked their positions.

## The Situation

The three radicadas could not be merged (see Exhibit 2). Each rested on a different idea of what a constitution was for.

The liberal radicada put equal basic liberties first, beyond the reach of ordinary majorities. It enforced them through a strong Constitutional Court and treated social and economic rights as a second tier, realised over time. The republican radicada defined freedom as the absence of arbitrary power, public or private, whether held by the State, an employer, a household or a monopoly. It built institutions through which citizens could contest that power. The deliberative radicada located legitimacy in the procedure by which citizens reasoned together. It created permanent citizen bodies chosen by lot and sent contested moral questions to structured deliberation.

Robledo-Castaño had announced how she would judge each draft: internal consistency with the tradition defended, institutional realism in the Aguarales of 2026, and honesty about trade-offs. Each of the twelve drafts would receive three marks and a score for its turn, and all of them went into the transcript. The running totals stood at zero when the debate opened. At the close, the total would place the defender in one of four standings, from *Beginner* to *Mesa-ready jurist*.

The order was fixed. In the first round, Cuéllar-Ibarra would articulate her tradition in four articles: 87 on the electoral system, 134 on direct democracy, 56 on recognition of the eight nations, and 3 on the catalogue of inviolable liberties. In the second round, the other two panelists would press her on the same four. Some of their questions were already known. What would her Article 56 do if a community sentenced a member to corporal punishment under its own law tomorrow morning? What would her Article 3 say to a worker fired at will, a wife whose movements her husband controlled, or a tenant facing a monopoly landlord? In the third round she would lock a final text on each article. On Article 56, Robledo-Castaño had said she would not accept a single sentence.

If nothing changed, the outcome was plain. In three weeks the Mesa would vote with three incompatible radicadas in front of it and no worked text that turned any tradition into a clause a constituyente could amend. The transcript would reach the 120 members either way. A defender who answered in principles would leave nothing in it to vote on.

## What Cuéllar-Ibarra Controlled

**The tradition.** Liberal, republican or deliberative, chosen once on the secretariat's form and fixed for all twelve drafts.

**The kind of draft.** For each article in each round, her two research assistants had prepared four texts. One was faithful to the tradition's canonical position, close to the coalition's own radicada. One kept the tradition's signature but conceded to a post-conflict country: transitional clauses, staged thresholds, money for capacity in the zones the State had abandoned. One borrowed safeguards from the two rival traditions. One was a single sentence of principle that left the detail to later statute and to the Court.

Each kind had its advocates. Tomás Ibáñez-Rojo, a former doctoral student of hers who now advised one of the coalitions, told her to hold her ground: a defender who conceded on the first morning had nothing left to concede by the third. A constituyente from a rural sub-coalition told her that the Mesa did not need a seminar. It needed text that would survive a vote and work in a department with no judge. A retired clerk of the Corte argued that short declaratory articles had aged better in the region's constitutions than long ones. Some of her colleagues said a draft that borrowed from rivals could gather votes across the blocs. Others said it read like a defender who had stopped defending.

**What she would admit.** With each draft she could say openly what it externalised: whom it left unprotected, which question it postponed, or what it would cost to run. Saying so might earn the Mesa's trust. It would also show the panelists across the table exactly where to press, and give the coalition whips a quotation for the vote.

**What she would see.** After each draft, the transcript recorded the round, the article, the kind of draft and whether she had named its trade-off. It also showed the three marks and the score for that turn, while the three running totals grew beside her.

## Closing

At 08:58 Robledo-Castaño switched on her microphone and thanked the Mesa's observers for coming. The form still lay beside the blank sheet, its three boxes empty. Cuéllar-Ibarra picked up her pen.
