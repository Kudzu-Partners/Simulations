# Travessia Soluções Educacionais: Rescuing the Frontline Leadership Blend

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1797 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1797 — *Mode of Delivery* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 11 May 2026, Renata Almeida read the twelve-week completion report for "Frontline Leadership," the program Travessia Soluções Educacionais delivered for Logística Pampulha S.A., and found the number she had been dreading: 31 percent of the 1,200 shift-based supervisors enrolled were on track to finish, against the 75 percent completion the contract required. Learner satisfaction sat at 3.1 out of 5. Logística Pampulha's chief human-resources officer had called an emergency review that same morning and would decide, within two months, whether to renew Travessia's enterprise-wide learning contract — a BRL 9 million agreement worth roughly 16 percent of Travessia's annual delivery revenue. Almeida, Travessia's Educational Solutions Director, had four weeks to redesign the program and four more weeks of live delivery to show the redesign was working before that renewal decision was made.

## The Protagonist

Almeida, 41, had led Travessia's educational-solutions practice since 2022, after nine years designing corporate-learning programs for two of Brazil's largest retail banks. She had built her reputation on protecting delivery margin: Travessia's 34 designers, producers and engagement specialists were paid well by industry standard, and the unit's 28 percent operating margin depended on her holding delivery cost close to budget on every account. It had been Almeida who, the previous year, approved building the Frontline Leadership program at 80 percent self-paced asynchronous e-learning, a design chosen to fit the price Logística Pampulha had negotiated for 1,200 learners across nine sites. Her own compensation, like the unit's, was tied to completion and learning-effectiveness scores rather than enrolment — the measure Travessia sold to every client as the reason to pay it rather than a cheaper competitor.

## The Decision

The redesign was not one decision but a sequence of them, and the first was diagnostic. Several explanations for the 31 percent were already circulating inside Travessia: that the program's delivery design did not fit how the supervisors worked; that the supervisors themselves lacked the motivation to finish; that the e-learning modules were not engaging enough to hold attention; or that the learning platform was too difficult for the supervisors to use. The analytics could support more than one reading, too: a spike in drop-off after week three, alongside 82 percent completion in the handful of live cohorts that had run; a shortage of logins, answerable with more reminder emails; a split between mobile and desktop use suggesting the modules needed rebuilding for phones; or a data set too noisy to read at all. Her diagnosis memo to the CHRO would also carry a candor setting, from cautious to blunt, in how directly it named a design mismatch rather than a motivation shortfall.

Diagnosis settled, the modality map itself needed rebuilding, objective by objective. For the program's skill-practice and behavior modules — coaching conversations, feedback, on-the-spot decisions — three placements were available: synchronous live-online practice sessions with peers, full in-person workshops delivered at all nine sites, or the self-paced async track the modules already sat in. The knowledge and compliance modules — policy, safety frameworks, theory — carried the same three-way choice: leave them self-paced async, move them into the live sessions alongside the skill work, or teach them in person. Underneath both calls sat the arithmetic: how to split the 1,200 supervisors' program hours across self-paced async at roughly BRL 8 a learner-hour, live-online sessions at roughly BRL 35, and in-person delivery at roughly BRL 120 (Exhibit 2) — a single mix that had to add up to the whole program, inside a redesign budget ceiling of BRL 2.4 million.

A modality map was not yet a schedule, and a schedule was not yet commitment. The next layer of decisions covered how synchronous and asynchronous time related to each other — self-paced material preparing supervisors before they walked into live practice; live sessions delivering the core content first with self-paced drills to follow; or the two tracks run side by side with no connection between them — and how live sessions would be scheduled across nine sites of rotating shifts: multiple cohort times with regional facilitators, one fixed mandatory time for every supervisor, or every live session left optional. Two further commitments sat alongside the schedule: how many thousand reais, from zero up to 600, to invest in cohorts, nudges and manager check-ins, and what share of supervisors' rostered time, from none to all of it, Travessia could persuade the client to protect as dedicated learning time.

None of it would be decided once and left alone. Logística Pampulha's terms for the final stretch already included an incentive: an additional BRL 300,000, payable only if completion in the last four weeks of the program reached 70 percent — money Almeida could commit conditionally, accept without conditions, or decline outright. She also had to settle how she would respond if a scheduled cohort collided with a rotating shift or a facilitator became unavailable: move the session to a recorded-plus-live format with regional facilitators and multiple times, cancel it and return those hours to the async track, or keep the original single mandatory slot and count a missed session as non-completion. And at the end of the redesign sat the renewal pitch itself, delivered to the CHRO in person: whether to lead with cost per completing, competent learner; with how little the redesign had spent per seat; or with how much effort the redesign had taken — each a different answer to what Logística Pampulha was paying Travessia for — defended with a conviction level Almeida would also set, from tentative to unyielding.

## The Company and its Setting

Travessia Soluções Educacionais was the educational-solutions delivery unit of a major Brazilian business school, based in Belo Horizonte. It ran a learning-experience platform, a virtual-classroom stack and a content studio, and it earned BRL 58 million in delivery revenue at a 28 percent operating margin. Its distinguishing commercial fact was that clients paid it for completion and learning effectiveness, not enrolment — a promise that made the Frontline Leadership shortfall more than an operational embarrassment. Logística Pampulha S.A., the client, ran a dispersed logistics operation in which 1,200 supervisors worked rotating shifts across nine sites, with little desk time and little e-learning habit — the population the original 80 percent asynchronous design had been built for at the lowest achievable cost (Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

If the blend went unchanged, the trajectory pointed to a result very close to the 31 percent already reached: short of the 75 percent the contract required, satisfaction still near 3.1 out of 5, and a CHRO with grounds to decline, not renew, a BRL 9 million contract. Travessia would be judged going forward on four numbers: projected completion against the 75 percent target; a learning-effectiveness score, out of 100 and currently 34, measuring how well each objective actually fit its delivery format; an engagement-and-accountability score, also out of 100 and currently 20, covering cohorts, nudges, manager involvement and protected time; and cost per completing learner — not cost per seat — currently BRL 6,452 and judged against the BRL 2.4 million redesign ceiling (Exhibit 1).

## What Almeida Controlled

Round by round, Almeida controlled the diagnosis and its candor, the modality assignment and hour allocation for every objective, the sequencing and scheduling of live and self-paced time, the size of the engagement budget and the protected-time commitment, and — in the final round — whether to take the conditional funding, how to answer a scheduling or staffing gap, and how to frame and defend the pitch. She was measured on completion, learning effectiveness, engagement and accountability, and cost per completing learner, all inside the fixed ceiling in Exhibit 1.

## Closing

At her desk that afternoon, Almeida opened a blank redesign brief and ruled three columns across the top of the page — modality, sequence, accountability — with four weeks to fill them in before the supervisors would need to feel the difference, and four more before Logística Pampulha's CHRO decided the fate of nine million reais.
