# Foundry & Field: The Runway Bet

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1925 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1925 — *The Project Economy* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 3 February 2026, Aoife Brennan read the letter of intent twice and then opened the payroll file, because the two documents only made sense beside each other. Corrib Pay, a Dublin payments company in its third year of a fast scale-up, wanted Foundry & Field to build its new merchant-onboarding platform: EUR 1.2 million, six months, live on 1 September and not a day later, because that date belonged to the client's regulator. It was the largest contract the studio had been offered, and delivering it needed roughly twice the delivery capacity Foundry & Field had.

The payroll file supplied the other half. Fourteen permanent staff, EUR 145,000 leaving the bank every month, EUR 1.3 million still in it — nine months of runway, ending in November. Corrib Pay wanted a countersigned statement of work naming the delivery team by 20 February. Brennan had seventeen days to decide who would build the platform, and on what terms the studio would agree to keep them.

## The Protagonist

Aoife Brennan, 38, was co-founder and chief executive of Foundry & Field. She had spent nine years at a Dublin digital agency, the last three as its delivery director, before leaving in 2023 with Dara Nolan, the agency's principal engineer, to start a studio on one argument: that the agency carried too many salaried people between projects and priced its work to cover them. Thirty months later she had built the opposite — a small permanent team with specialists brought in around it, build by build. She had never run a project worth half a year's revenue, and she had never had to make anyone redundant.

## The Decision

Brennan had to settle how one project would be staffed, role by role, as employees or as contractors; how the money arriving from the client would be timed against the money leaving in payroll; and what she would do with the team if the demand behind the contract moved.

## The Company and its Setting

Foundry & Field Ltd. designed and built data and AI software for mid-market clients — dashboards, integrations, bespoke internal tools. Trailing twelve-month revenue was EUR 2.4 million. The fourteen permanent staff were engineers, designers and a delivery lead; around them a rotating bench of freelance specialists moved in and out by project. The studio was venture-backed and had never made a profit.

In Ireland a senior engineer hired permanently cost Foundry & Field about EUR 8,500 a month once salary, employer contributions, benefits and equipment were counted, and carried three to six months of notice and severance whenever the relationship ended. The same seniority contracting billed about EUR 11,500 a month, with no tail on either side and no obligation past the end of the engagement. Dublin's market for senior data and platform engineers was tight: contractors could usually be found within weeks; permanent hires took longer to find, and longer again to become useful on an unfamiliar codebase.

## The Situation

The build had been priced to leave 38% of the EUR 1.2 million after the cost of the people who built it, and it needed five senior people beyond the fourteen already on the payroll for its full six months (see **Exhibit 2**).

Brennan and Nolan reported four numbers to the board each month, and all four stood as set out in **Exhibit 1**. Runway — months of cash left at the current rate of spending — stood at 9.0; the board treated anything below five months as the point at which the studio would stop choosing and start reacting. Delivery confidence, a 0–100 reading Nolan maintained on whether a committed date would be met, stood at 55 for the build as scoped. Project margin was the 38% the contract had been priced to. The fourth had been added by the seed investor: a 0–100 judgement of how far the studio had shown that bringing in expert capacity project by project was a way to grow rather than a way to plug a hole. It stood at 40. Beneath all four sat effective monthly burn — everything leaving the account — at EUR 145,000 before a single extra person was added.

Behind the contract sat two smaller follow-on deals worth about EUR 500,000 each, pencilled into the third quarter; neither was signed, and neither would be before the build was well under way.

If Foundry & Field changed nothing, the arithmetic was undramatic: EUR 145,000 a month against EUR 1.3 million emptied the account in November, and nothing on the current book was large enough to change the slope. If the studio signed and built with the fourteen people it had, the September date belonged to a regulator and would not move for a delivery plan.

The investor had already put his question in writing ahead of the June board meeting: did the staffing plan extend the runway, or did it bet the company?

## What Brennan Controlled

She began with the reading of the job. She could size the extra capacity as a stretch the existing fourteen would absorb with overtime; as roughly double the delivery capacity for six months, ending when the build ended; or as a doubling the studio would keep, on the view that the pipeline behind the contract would hold it at the larger size. She then had to say what kind of demand the contract was: entirely temporary and gone when the build shipped; entirely durable and the leading edge of a bigger business; or mostly temporary with a durable core of ownership and support. And she had to set how binding the runway would be on everything that followed — one consideration among several, or the limit that overrode every other argument.

Then the five roles, each filled either with an employee or with a contractor: a platform architect, who would set the core design and answer for it through go-live and afterwards; a senior back-end engineer, on the services the platform would run on; a data and machine-learning specialist, for the risk-scoring components Corrib Pay had specified; a front-end engineer, for the merchant-facing screens; and a QA and integration engineer, whose work ran from the first integration to the day the platform shipped. She would also have to write down which single test had decided the mix: the lower monthly cost of an employee against a contractor; the absence of any permanent cost between projects; or the difference between owning a capability and renting it.

The money and the knowledge came next. She could invoice the full EUR 1.2 million once at go-live, split it into six equal monthly invoices, or negotiate a deposit and front-loaded milestones so that more of it arrived in the first half. She could ring-fence anywhere from nothing to EUR 600,000 of the existing cash against the gap between monthly payroll and client payment. And she had to decide how the platform knowledge would be held: by leaving it to the contractors' own habits and the notes they kept; by requiring a written handover pack before anyone rolled off; or by pairing every contractor with a permanent engineer and writing handover and intellectual-property terms into each contract.

Finally, at the investor's request, the plan had to state in advance what she would do if the third-quarter pipeline moved — hold the whole team, end the permanent surge contracts and pay out the notice, let the contractor engagements run down and keep the permanent team, or convert a contractor to a permanent role. It also had to name the single number she would put in front of the board in June — quarterly revenue, headcount, or months of runway remaining — and say how firmly she intended to argue it.

## Closing

At 9:15 Brennan wrote the five roles down the left-hand side of a page and drew two columns beside them, one headed *ours* and one headed *hired in*. Seventeen days to the statement of work. Two hundred and ten to the client's regulator.
