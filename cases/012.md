## Abstract

Solera Technologies, a USD 140M revenue B2B SaaS scale-up, had assigned a new squad lead, Daniel Ortiz, to a product team that had missed two consecutive launch deadlines and was twelve weeks from a third. The team of eight — four engineers, one product manager, one UX designer, one UX researcher, and one data analyst — entered the transition with a Psychological-Safety score of 3.1/10, a Trust index of 4.2/10, an innovation output of 2 ideas per month, and a predicted Launch-Success probability of 41%. Four of the eight were described by HR as "guarded," "defensive," "anxious," or "invisible." The outgoing squad lead had been moved laterally for reasons that had not been announced. Ortiz inherited the first kickoff meeting in fifteen minutes. The case sets Ortiz in the fifteen minutes before that meeting and in front of four meeting-design choices that will shape the twelve-week arc. It maps to the first round of the companion simulation.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Define psychological safety operationally and distinguish it from trust, cohesion, and comfort.
2. Analyze how small, early leadership behaviors compound into team-state trajectories.
3. Design a first-meeting intervention appropriate to a low-safety, high-pressure team.
4. Diagnose the interaction between launch-deadline pressure and candor-seeking behaviors.
5. Recommend a leadership posture that protects both innovation output and delivery probability.

At **08:47 a.m. on Monday, April 27, 2026**, **Daniel Ortiz** stood in the kitchenette on the third floor of Solera Technologies' Polanco office and read, on his phone, the last Slack message the outgoing squad lead had sent the team the previous Friday: *"It has been a privilege. Good luck with launch. — A."* There was no reply thread. In thirteen minutes, Ortiz would walk into a conference room, open his laptop, and meet his eight new direct reports for the first time.

---

## The Protagonist

Daniel Ortiz, 36, had joined Solera four weeks earlier, in a senior-manager role on the Platform team. He had been named interim squad lead on Friday afternoon, forty-three hours earlier, after the Chief Product Officer had moved the prior lead — referred to in the company only as "A." — to a horizontal role in Strategy. Ortiz had eleven years of engineering-management experience at two other SaaS companies, had led a high-performing team through a successful launch at his prior employer, and had earned a reputation internally at Solera for asking good questions in design reviews. He had no prior management history at Solera.

He had read, over the weekend, the performance reviews of the eight team members; the product's technical plan; the customer-advisory-board minutes from the two failed launches; and an HR engagement report that used, for four of the eight team members, the words *guarded, defensive, anxious,* and *invisible*.

---

## The Decision

In fifteen minutes Ortiz would open his first meeting as squad lead. Four meeting-design choices were on the table, each advocated by someone in his extended network, each consistent with a defensible model of team leadership, and none of them obviously right:

- **Option A — Run the inherited agenda as planned.** Dense status update. Preserves continuity; signals competence; risks reading as "another A."
- **Option B — Scrap the agenda for a getting-to-know-each-other session.** Invests in the relational foundation; signals new posture; risks reading, on a twelve-week launch clock, as a leader who does not take the deadline seriously.
- **Option C — Keep first 10 minutes of the agenda, then add a closing 15-minute structured round.** Hybrid; signals both continuity and change; risks diluting each message.
- **Option D — Send a Slack, now, asking each person to prepare one proud moment and one wish for how the team could work together.** Front-loads voice; makes the meeting the second act; risks reading as performative or as ducking the room.

Ortiz would, over the following eleven weeks, have three more formal meetings with the team that would carry similar choice-weight: a mid-sprint retrospective, a pre-launch go/no-go, and a post-launch team review. The companion simulation treats Round 1 as the fifteen-minute pre-kickoff choice.

## Company Background

Solera Technologies had been founded in 2017 by two former operators of a larger logistics platform and had grown, by 2025, to USD 140M ARR across 640 enterprise customers in the Americas and Western Europe. Its product, Solera Flow, was a supply-chain visibility platform used by mid-market manufacturers and consumer-goods brands. The company had raised a USD 70M Series D in Q4 2024 at a post-money valuation of USD 620M, with a growth expectation of 35% ARR year-over-year and a path to default-alive operating cash by end of 2026.

The squad Ortiz now led was the Insights squad — responsible for the analytics and alerting surface of Solera Flow. The squad's most recent major launch, in Q4 2024, had slipped by seven weeks and had shipped with a reduced feature scope. The prior launch, in Q1 2024, had slipped by eleven weeks and had, in the customer-advisory-board minutes, been described as "a tense release." The pending launch — a real-time anomaly detection module — was scheduled for July 20, 2026.

Solera's culture, by engagement-survey construction, sat in the 52nd percentile on a "safe-to-speak" scale, which the head of People had characterized as "below where a scale-up should be but not below where a scale-up often is." The Insights squad specifically scored in the 18th percentile.

## Industry and Market Context

The B2B SaaS mid-market had entered 2026 with a sharply divergent distribution of outcomes between high- and low-performing delivery teams: teams in the top quartile of psychological-safety scores shipped their launches on time 78% of the time; teams in the bottom quartile shipped on time 29% of the time. The research literature on team state had, since Edmondson's foundational 1999 study, converged on four propositions: safety preceded candor, candor preceded course-correction, course-correction preceded delivery, and early-leader behaviors compounded non-linearly. Post-2020 work on remote and hybrid teams had added a fifth: the first two meetings of a new manager were disproportionately diagnostic of the twelve-week trajectory.

For Solera specifically, investor pressure on the Q2 2026 launch meant that a third consecutive slip would, per the CFO, open a conversation about the pace of Platform-wide hiring. Ortiz knew this. The team almost certainly sensed it.

---

## The Situation

### How we got here

The two failed launches had, in the customer-advisory-board minutes, been attributed to (a) a specification that arrived too late from the PM, (b) a design pivot requested by a senior-engineer voice after the design review had closed, and (c) a quality-assurance finding that had surfaced nine days before code-freeze. No one on the team had, in the post-mortem, named a specific person; HR's read was that the team had internalized blame anyway.

### What Ortiz knew

The eight team members were Valentina (UX Designer, "guarded"), Diego (Backend Engineer, "defensive"), Sofia (Product Manager, "anxious"), Carlos (Data Analyst, "invisible"), Ana (QA Lead, "ignored"), Mateo (Frontend Engineer, "compliant"), Priya (DevOps Engineer, "withdrawing"), and Roberto (UX Researcher, "disengaged"). The HR characterizations were directional, not clinical. Ortiz had met only Sofia and Mateo, briefly, in the four weeks since his arrival.

### What he did not know

Which of the eight carried institutional memory of the two prior launches. Whether "A" had been removed for performance, for interpersonal reasons, or for reasons that were adjacent to HR investigation. Whether any of the eight had been, in the past six months, considering leaving the company.

### What others were saying

**Renata Gómez**, Chief Product Officer, had told him in a 6:04 p.m. Friday call: *"Ship in twelve weeks. Do not ship a different product. Do not lose the team. In that order."*

**Héctor Varela**, Head of People, had written in a Sunday-evening email: *"First meeting matters more than the next five. Do not mistake pace for clarity. Ask more than you tell."*

**A. himself** had sent, at 11:47 p.m. Sunday, an unsolicited two-line DM: *"Sofia carries the team. Diego carries the system. Ignore the rest at your peril."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

The four options the simulation surfaces are as enumerated under *The Decision*. Ortiz had, in the prior sixteen hours, stress-tested each against the HR state map and the twelve-week plan.

- **Option A (inherited agenda)** protected the launch plan, signalled competence, and was the default of every dysfunctional team he had encountered at his previous employer. It would, he suspected, accelerate further withdrawal in Carlos, Priya, and Roberto.

- **Option B (relational reset)** would likely be read as a positive signal by Valentina, Roberto, and Carlos, and as a risk to the launch by Sofia and Diego. It would lose three working hours.

- **Option C (hybrid)** was the option most of his prior managers had taken; it rarely failed and rarely created step-changes.

- **Option D (asynchronous pre-work)** front-loaded voice and made the meeting the second act; it risked reading as performative in a team that had, on the HR signal, already been performed at.

Ortiz's private bet, not yet articulated to anyone, was that the correct answer depended less on the option than on what he said aloud, in the first ninety seconds, about himself.

Ortiz put the phone back in his pocket, poured a second cup of coffee he would not drink, and walked the thirty-four meters to the conference room. Twelve weeks, one fifteen-minute choice, eight people he had read about and had not yet met. He opened the door.
