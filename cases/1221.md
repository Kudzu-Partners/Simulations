# Costaria and the Mango Tariff

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1221 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1221 — *Tariff War Simulator: Three Rounds of Retaliation* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 08:00 on Monday, March 2, 2026, the government of Boravia announced a 25% tariff on every mango imported from Costaria, effective in thirty days. By 10:30, Marisol Quintero-Abad, Prime Minister of Costaria, had her cabinet in the crisis room on the second floor of Government House. The Boravian notice called the tariff an anti-dumping measure. Nobody at the table read it that way.

Quintero-Abad had promised Parliament a statement by 15:00 on Thursday, March 5. It had to say two things: whether Costaria would put a tariff of its own on the Boravian software its banks, hospitals and ministries ran on, and what the government would do besides. That would be the first of six quarterly decisions. Whatever she chose, Boravia's cabinet would be choosing at the same time.

## The Protagonist

Quintero-Abad was fifty-four. She had spent fifteen years at the Central Bank of Costaria, the last four as deputy governor for external accounts, before her party drafted her as its candidate in 2023. She had won on a platform of steady growth and open markets, carrying two of the three mango provinces by fewer than four points. The next general election was due in early 2028, a few months after the eighteen-month horizon her advisers were now planning against.

She knew the corridor's arithmetic better than most of her ministers. She also knew who had voted for her.

## The Decision

Each quarter for six quarters, the cabinet would settle two things. The first was the tariff Costaria charged on Boravian software and IT services. The second was a single strategic line for the quarter: hold the current posture and wait, file a complaint at the World Trade Organization (WTO), open back-channel negotiations with Boravia, or pay an emergency subsidy to the mango sector. Each quarter the Cabinet Office would log the result, and Boravia's answer, before the cabinet met again.

## The Company and its Setting

Costaria was a tropical economy of 18 million people. Its mango sector employed 140,000 people directly, almost all of them in three northern provinces that had decided the last two elections. Boravia, its temperate neighbour of 22 million, was the natural buyer: Costaria sold it about US$850 million of mangoes a year.

The flow ran the other way in software. Costaria bought about US$1.0 billion a year of Boravian enterprise software and IT services. Its banks, hospitals, tax office and supermarket chains ran on Boravian systems that would take years to replace. That trade supported some 12,000 software jobs in two Boravian metropolitan districts. Costaria's own software sector amounted to about thirty small firms.

The formal Boravian complaint concerned a mango export-support programme that Costaria had introduced in 2024. In both capitals it was understood as cover. Boravia's software producers wanted leverage, and mangoes were the only Costarian export large and visible enough to supply it.

Others were watching. Software houses in India and Poland had been courting Costarian banks for years. Fruit exporters in Peru and Mexico wanted Boravian supermarket shelf space. Neither group had any part in the dispute.

## The Situation

The Finance Ministry's first estimate was blunt. At 25%, the Boravian tariff would cost Costarian exporters about US$210 million a year in lost revenue, a little over US$50 million a quarter. Nothing inside Costaria offset it: Costaria collected no tariff and protected no industry. Boravia would also lose, because Boravian families would pay more for mangoes, but by the Ministry's reckoning it would lose less than Costaria.

The cabinet dashboard opened at its baseline (see **Exhibit 1**). Cumulative net welfare stood at US$0 million. Net welfare was the dashboard's single money line. It added any tariff revenue the treasury collected and any gain to protected Costarian producers. It then subtracted the higher prices Costarian buyers paid, the trade that no longer happened at all, and the revenue Costarian exporters lost to Boravia's tariff. Mango employment stood at 140,000. Bilateral trade in the mango-software corridor was indexed at 100. A bilateral trust index, which tracked how investors priced the corridor's long-run stability, stood at 100. The share of corridor trade diverted to third countries stood at 0%. The cabinet's political-capital index stood at 100; the party's strategists treated anything under 50 as an election in trouble.

If the cabinet did nothing, the shape of the year was clear. The Boravian tariff would stay. Mango growers would sell less to their largest customer, and jobs in the three provinces would go with the unsold fruit. The growers' federation would hold the government responsible for a tariff left unanswered. Its president, Tomás Arriaga Velasco, had already called the morning's announcement "an act of economic aggression that requires an answer, not a seminar."

## What Quintero-Abad Controlled

Four people spoke at the table, and each pressed a different course (see **Exhibit 2**).

The **tariff on Boravian software** came first. Rodrigo Ferrán-Ibarra, the Trade Minister, wanted 25%, matching Boravia's rate. The schedule also allowed no tariff at all, 50% or 100%. A tariff would bring revenue to the treasury and some shelter to Costaria's thirty software firms. Costarian banks, hospitals and ministries would also pay more for systems they could not quickly replace, and some contracts would not be signed at all.

The **strategic line** came second, one per quarter. Lucía Montalvo-Reyes, the Finance Minister, wanted a WTO complaint and patience. Her own legal advisers put the time to a panel report at well over a year. They also noted that the WTO's Appellate Body had lacked a quorum since December 2019. Arriaga Velasco wanted an emergency subsidy for the mango sector, budgeted at US$180 million a year, paid by the treasury while the tariff lasted. Andrés Peñalver, the Foreign Minister, wanted to open a back-channel to Boravia for a managed-trade deal. He knew it would look weak at home, and he expected the federation to oppose it in public. The fourth course was to hold: keep the current posture and wait.

Quintero-Abad would be judged on the record the Cabinet Office kept, quarter by quarter. It showed both countries' tariffs, the cabinet's action, the quarter's net welfare effect in US$ millions, trade volume, the trust index, political capital and Boravia's response. The dashboard tracked the running totals: cumulative net welfare, mango employment and the share of trade lost to third countries. At the end of the sixth quarter the record would be reduced to one overall score out of 100 and a performance band.

## Closing

At 07:40 on Thursday, three memos sat on Quintero-Abad's desk, one each from Ferrán-Ibarra, Montalvo-Reyes and Peñalver. A fourth, from the growers' federation, had arrived by courier overnight. Outside, the first trucks from the northern provinces were parking along the avenue. The statement to Parliament was due at 15:00, and the Boravian cabinet would be meeting that same afternoon.
