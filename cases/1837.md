# Anadolu Çelik: From Power to Empowerment

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1837 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1837 — *From Power to Empowerment* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 8 June 2026, Zeynep Kaya read the quality escalation her operations director had forwarded overnight and understood what the next eight weeks would demand of her. Anadolu Çelik's largest export customer, a German automotive-parts buyer, had traced a defective shipment back to the plant floor in Gebze, and the audit blamed not a machine but a habit: a line worker had spotted the flaw early and said nothing, the nearest supervisor had not asked, and the fix had waited three days for a senior signature. The letter did not read like a first warning. It read like a last one. Kaya had eight weeks to show a credible plan for how Anadolu's people would work differently, or watch the customer withdraw 20 percent of its volume — ₺1.0 billion a year.

## The Protagonist

Kaya, 46, had taken the Country Manager role at Anadolu Çelik four months earlier, arriving with a board mandate to move the plant from a culture of command toward one of shared ownership, without denying what the plant actually was. She had spent the previous decade running operations for two multinational component makers, one in İzmir and one in Stuttgart, and had been hired, she suspected, for fluency in both a German auditor's language and a Kocaeli factory floor's. Part of her own compensation was tied to the customer relationship surviving the year.

## The Decision

Kaya's job over the next eight weeks was not to write a memo about culture. It was a running sequence of concrete calls: how much decision-making authority to hand down and to whom, how to describe the change to the people living through it, how to respond the first time someone tested the new authority and got it wrong, and what to tell the people who would decide whether Anadolu kept its biggest customer.

## The Company and its Setting

Anadolu Çelik Sanayi A.Ş. was a family-controlled steel-products and metal-components manufacturer in Gebze, on the industrial corridor east of Istanbul. The plant employed 1,150 people and generated ₺11.2 billion in annual revenue, with 45 percent of output exported to European automotive and white-goods customers who audited workforce practice as closely as tolerances. Authority at Anadolu had for years moved in one direction: up. A problem spotted on the line was something a worker mentioned to a supervisor, who mentioned it to someone above him, until whoever could authorize a fix did — sometimes days later.

## The Situation

The letter Kaya read that Monday came with numbers a board could not wave away. The plant's own Empowerment Index — a composite blending how much workers could decide for themselves, how freely they could raise a problem, and how visibly good judgment was rewarded — stood at 31 out of 100. A separate Cultural-Fit score, meant to flag whether a change was landing as respect or as disruption, sat at 60, above the floor of 50 past which supervisors were known to resist openly. Team engagement registered 38 out of 100, alongside technician attrition running at 18 percent a year, costing roughly ₺140 million annually in replacement hiring and lost productivity. Output quality sat at 42 out of 100, with quality failures running near ₺220 million a year and rising (Exhibit 1). Kaya had a ₺60 million change budget and about ten units of scarce management attention to spend alongside it. Left alone, the numbers suggested a plant that would keep training the customer, and its own best people, to expect exactly what they had always gotten.

## What Kaya Controlled

Her first task was diagnostic. She had to decide what had actually caused the shipment to fail: a gap in inspection tools and checklists, a gap in how workers had been trained to recognize the defect, plain carelessness by particular people on the line, or something more structural in how decisions and speech moved through the plant. Bound to that call was a second one — which existing expectation any change would have to work with, not against. She could import a flat, speak-freely model and remove a layer of supervision outright; treat higher pay as the lever that got people talking; lean on seniority, letting the most experienced worker decide; or build new latitude around Anadolu's existing culture of hierarchy and paternal care, framing it as something extended rather than surrendered. She also had to state, as a percentage, how clearly her own leadership team agreed on a single root cause before acting on it.

In the weeks that followed, Kaya had to decide how aggressively to move. She could launch every lever at once inside the first week; launch a single lever and hold the rest in reserve; or choose two and sequence them deliberately ahead of the customer's visit. Whatever she chose, she had to divide a fixed ₺60 million change budget across three named levers (Exhibit 2): delegation, the authority to halt the line over a quality concern, pushed down to the worker who saw the problem; voice, daily huddles and a channel for reporting near-misses without blame; and recognition, a visible way of rewarding people who raised a problem or made the right call unaided. Finally, she had to decide how to describe all of this to the floor and its supervisors — as a mandate to be enforced and audited, as a Western-style flattening in which no one outranked anyone, or as an extension of the paternal care the company had always claimed, in which the firm trusted workers enough to let them stop a bad part.

Not long after, one worker used the new stop-the-line authority over what proved a two-hour false alarm, and the plant's veteran Operations Lead — who ran forty percent of the floor — demanded the authority be withdrawn and order restored. Kaya had to decide whether to retract it, leave it in place on paper while quietly discouraging its use, or reinforce it publicly and treat the false alarm as the price of the lesson. She also had to decide how to handle the veteran himself: overrule him in front of the floor, work around him through his own team, or give him a visible role designing the very rules he was resisting. At the same time, the German customer asked for a plant visit in two weeks, and Kaya had to decide what it would show: a declaration that the culture was already transformed, a tour arranged to keep the harder shifts out of view, or a look at early behavior changes alongside a plan for what came next. Underneath all three calls sat a fourth: how much frontline and supervisor coaching time she would protect, on a scale from zero to a hundred percent, so people had time to actually do what was asked.

By the final weeks, Kaya had to prepare what she would tell the board chair and the customer's supplier-quality director. She had to decide what she would commit to measuring going forward — only lagging numbers like quarterly defect cost and attrition; a one-off launch event and a bump in an engagement survey; or real-time signals about huddles and caught defects alongside the slower-moving cost and attrition figures. She had to decide who would keep the change alive once her own attention moved on: herself, chairing every huddle indefinitely; no one in particular, trusting early momentum; or the supervisors themselves, coached into the role and written into onboarding and handover. And she had to decide, before two skeptical audiences, whether to promise a transformation complete in eight weeks, promise compliance enforced by mandate and audit, or say plainly that early behavior is real and rising while full culture change would take months — then state, on a scale from zero to a hundred, how firmly she would defend that answer.

## Closing

At 8:40 that morning, Kaya set the audit letter down beside a blank sheet headed with the four numbers she would be judged against, and began drafting the diagnosis she would have to defend to a floor that had spent decades learning that speaking up was not its job.
