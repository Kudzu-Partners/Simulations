# Meridian Infrastructure Group: A Question of Capacity

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1888 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1888 — *Complexity Coach* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of 28 May 2026, Meera Desai left a board sub-committee room in Melbourne with a verdict stated more plainly than expected. The sub-committee had just reviewed the collapse of Project Confluence, the integration of two newly acquired water utilities, in which two of Meridian Infrastructure Group's most respected senior leaders had escalated a contested regulatory-and-community trade-off straight to the executive committee rather than resolve it themselves. The escalation had cost six weeks and, the regulator made clear, some of its goodwill. The Chair's closing line was the one Desai carried out with her: "We do not have a strategy problem. We have a senior-leadership-complexity problem."

As Meridian's Leadership-Development Director, Desai now had a mandate, a number and a date. The number was A$2.4 million — the entire program budget for the 120 senior leaders below the executive committee, with no contingency behind it. The date was twelve months out: the board wanted a credible read-out on whether those leaders could now do the thing two of them had just failed to do.

## The Protagonist

Desai, 45, had run Meridian's leadership-development function for four years, having joined from a consulting practice that designed development programs for two other regulated infrastructure operators. She reported to the CHRO and worked alongside a coaching-faculty lead and a finance business partner who signed off on every dollar of the design. Her own standing was tied to the outcome: a weak read-out would put the program's budget, and her role, under review. She had never defended a spend this size to a board that had just watched two careers stall over one afternoon.

## The Decision

Desai's first task was to name precisely what had failed. Her team offered three readings: that the leaders had lacked specific techniques for negotiating ambiguity and needed teaching; that they had the techniques but had not been held accountable, and needed sharper incentives; or that they had not yet grown the capacity to hold two competing demands, staying steady while neither resolved. Layered onto that was whether her leaders lacked both the ability to hold competing frames and the composure that went with it, whether the frame-holding alone was missing, their composure never actually in question, or whether the real shortage was technical depth in regulation and community relations — and whether a segment nearest the live trade-offs needed something the rest did not, or whether all 120 needed exactly the same response. She also had to decide how sharply to define, before the program began, the behaviour that would count as proof any of it had changed.

With a diagnosis in hand, Desai had to build a program inside the A$2.4 million cap — roughly A$20,000 per leader, all-in. Four levers were available: action-learning assignments embedded in the live transformation program; a coaching pool combining executive coaching with peer circles; cohort workshops, 360-degree assessments and formal training; and seats in an external accelerated program a global business school had pitched at A$38,000 per leader (see Exhibit 2). Whichever mix she chose, she still had to resolve one question: a thin layer of development reaching every one of the 120 leaders, so no one was left out; intensive development concentrated on a smaller priority group nearest the live trade-offs, with a lighter touch for the rest; or as many leaders as the budget allowed routed through the externally branded program, whose name on a CV strengthened the retention case.

Executive coaching, the costliest lever per leader, covered only 40 of the 120 at the six-session depth known to change behaviour, at A$9,000 each. Desai had to set the rule for who received it: an equal split so no leader could say they had been overlooked; priority to the leaders with the strongest long-run potential, for Meridian's future bench; priority to the most business-critical leaders and the Confluence strugglers, where trade-offs were live now; or priority to whoever each sponsoring executive nominated, since sponsors knew their people best. She also had to decide whether to stretch the coaching money across all 120 at one or two sessions each, or hold the full six-session depth for the 40 the pool could support, and how much to spend reaching the other 80 through peer coaching. One sponsoring executive, whose cooperation the transformation needed, pushed to place three of their people into the pool regardless of the rule — and Desai had to decide whether to make the exception quietly to keep the relationship smooth, hold the rule as written for everyone alike, or revise it openly for every sponsor.

Finally, Desai had to decide what the twelve-month read-out would rest on: satisfaction scores and a self-rated confidence survey, managers' and peers' ratings of behaviour on real trade-offs, or completion and attendance records showing who had finished the program. She also had to decide whether early signals such as coaching uptake stood on their own, were set aside entirely until the twelve-month result, or served as an early read checked later against it — and what would keep the capability alive once the program ended: an annual refresher bringing the cohort back together, manager reinforcement built into real decisions afterward, or nothing beyond the program itself, trusting it would stick. Last, she had to decide how firmly to argue that behaviour mattered more than satisfaction, a stance some on her team thought needlessly risky given who controlled her budget.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Infrastructure Group Ltd, headquartered in Melbourne and founded in 2003, ran electricity distribution, water utilities and toll-road concessions across eastern Australia. The company employed 6,800 people, generated A$3.1 billion in annual revenue at a 31% EBITDA margin, and was majority-owned by a consortium of superannuation funds whose mandate was stable, long-horizon returns rather than short-term upside.

Meridian was mid-way through a once-in-a-generation change: decarbonising its energy assets, absorbing the two newly acquired water utilities, and standing up a single integrated control centre across businesses that had run separately until then — all at once. The board had concluded the binding constraint on that change was not strategy or capital, but the 120 senior leaders one layer below the executive committee — promoted for technical and operational excellence in a stable, regulated world, and now asked to hold contradiction and competing demands their prior experience had never required.

## The Situation

The board's own assessment of the 120 senior leaders, taken before Confluence collapsed, scored the cohort at 2.4 out of 5 on navigating ambiguity and competing demands — against a 12-month target of 3.6 (see Exhibit 1). A separate board-credibility score — trust in whatever read-out Desai delivered, on a 0-to-100 scale — stood at just 25. Every day a senior leader spent in a workshop or coaching session was a day away from a transformation already running at capacity; Desai read the business's tolerance at around twelve days per leader a year. Three of Meridian's strongest senior leaders were being courted by competitors, and losing any one mid-transformation was estimated to cost upward of A$600,000 — a number tying the investment to retention as much as to capability.

If the pattern behind Confluence went unaddressed, there was no reason to expect a different result the next time a senior leader met a trade-off with no clean answer: the same reflex to escalate rather than hold the tension, the same weeks lost to a decision that belonged one level down, and one more data point for the three leaders already being courted elsewhere.

## What Desai Controlled

Across the year, Desai controlled how the complexity gap was diagnosed and segmented, how the A$2.4 million was allocated across action-learning, coaching, training and the external program, who received the scarce depth of coaching and under what rule, how she responded when that rule was challenged, and what the program would measure and sustain. She was measured on four things: complexity-readiness against the 3.6 target, program spend against the A$2.4 million cap, average days per leader off the job against the business's tolerance, and the board-credibility score that had started the year at 25.

## Closing

Desai sat down that evening with the assessment data, the Confluence post-mortem, and a blank page headed "Program Design." Twelve months, by the board's own clock, started the moment she picked up the pen.
