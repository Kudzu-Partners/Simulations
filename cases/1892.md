# Northbridge Systems: Ninety Days on a Frozen Budget

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1892 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1892 — *Plan Meets Pipeline* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Tuesday, June 16, 2026, Sylvie Marchand watched Northbridge Systems' board freeze the commercial budget flat at CAD 9.6 million and hand her ninety days to spend it differently. The chair had put two numbers on the table first: annual recurring revenue was growing at 8%, against 22% three years earlier, and the win rate had slipped to 18%. Then he asked what a tenth million would buy that the first 9.6 had not.

Marchand had been Northbridge's marketing director for eleven days. By September 14 she owed the board one go-to-market plan that marketing and sales both signed — one set of priority segments, one shared definition of a lead worth working, one reallocated budget, and one way of counting whether any of it had worked. Reallocation was the only lever; there was no new money in the mandate.

## The Protagonist

Sylvie Marchand, 41, had arrived at Northbridge on June 5, 2026, from a Toronto industrial-automation vendor where she had run demand generation for four years. Marguerite Lavoie, who had become Northbridge's chief executive in January, hired her with one instruction: stop running marketing and sales as two departments. Marchand inherited a marketing team of fourteen that the company itself called the campaign factory: webinars, booths and content produced against a calendar rather than a plan. She had no authority over the 45-person direct sales force. Her own targets, for the first time in her career, were written in growth and win rate rather than in leads delivered.

## The Decision

Marchand had to decide, across four working sessions between June and September, what Northbridge's commercial plan said, who would execute it, how sales would be paid and managed to do so, and where the frozen CAD 9.6 million would sit when the year began.

## The Company and its Setting

Northbridge Systems Inc., founded in 2009 and headquartered in Mississauga, Ontario, sold maintenance- and asset-management software to mid-market manufacturers and utilities across Canada. It carried CAD 64 million of annual recurring revenue and employed 310 people, 45 of them direct sales representatives organised by region.

Reps still closed the deals they sourced themselves. What had changed was the distance between the plan marketing wrote and the pipeline sales worked. The prior year's plan had named three priority segments. Sales had spent roughly 70% of its time on a fourth, with shorter contracts and faster signatures, because commission was paid on the number of logos closed. Doug Merrick, vice-president of sales and nineteen years at Northbridge, had summarised marketing's leads at the June review in one word: "Tourists."

## The Situation

The funnel itself was not in dispute; both sides quoted the same report (see **Exhibit 1**). Marketing generated 2,000 leads a quarter. Sales worked 22% of them. The remaining 78% — about 1,560 leads every quarter — were never touched by a representative at all. Of the leads that were worked, 9% became opportunities, against a business-to-business software norm near 13%. Of those opportunities, 18% closed, against a norm near 22%.

Each side read the same page differently. Marketing's account was that it delivered pipeline and sales sat on it. Sales' account was that the leads were unqualified, that nobody had ever written down what qualified meant, and that a rep paid on logos would spend the day where logos came fastest.

Two composite measures had gone to the board with the funnel report, and Marchand would be reported against both. An alignment index scored, out of 100, how far marketing and sales operated as one system: shared definitions, shared commitments, shared numbers. It stood at 25. A conversion-health index scored, also out of 100, how much of the funnel actually moved through relative to the size of the leak. It stood at 20. Beside them sat the two figures the board tracked directly: projected growth in annual recurring revenue, modelled at 8.0%, and the annual revenue lift that projection implied against the CAD 64 million base, currently CAD 0.0 million.

If Marchand changed nothing, the arithmetic was flat rather than dramatic. The CAD 9.6 million would buy the same 8% growth, the same 1,560 untouched leads a quarter and the same 18% win rate.

## What Marchand Controlled

Her first session was diagnosis, and it had to end in a single answer, not a list. She could locate the largest leak at the top of the funnel, where 2,000 leads a quarter was too few to feed 45 reps; at the handoff from marketing to sales, where 78% were never touched; at the close, where an 18% win rate met a 22% norm; or in the product, where Northbridge lost on features rather than on go-to-market. She had to name a root cause with the same finality: that sales could not see the leads marketing already sent; that sales was not working them hard enough; that no shared definition of a qualified lead existed and commission pointed reps elsewhere; or that the market had softened and this was a cycle rather than a structural gap. And she had to decide how much of her ninety days to spend putting a dollar figure on the untouched leads before proposing anything.

The second session was the plan. She would declare how many of Northbridge's four segments were priority — all four, three, two or one — knowing that anything short of four meant telling a sales force which customers it was being asked to stop chasing. She would set what the value proposition led with: the platform's feature set, a lower price, or the chosen segment's own outcome. She would set the quarterly demand target at 3,000 leads, 2,000 or 1,400, each implying a different reading of whether the constraint was the size of the top of the funnel or what happened below it. And she would decide how much of marketing's existing off-plan activity to shut down to fund whatever she had chosen.

The third session belonged to sales, and to Merrick. She could leave the handoff where it stood, sending leads across and trusting reps to work the ones worth working; commit marketing to a firm quarterly lead volume that sales could plan its coverage and forecasting against; or write a two-way agreement carrying a shared definition of a qualified lead, a response-time commitment and a feedback loop both sides owned. She could keep commission on logo count, raise the quota against the same design, or pay accelerators on priority-segment wins and move coverage to match. She could have reps sell on price and the speed it bought, on the product demonstration and the platform's breadth, or on the customer's outcome and the plan's value proposition. And she had to decide how far to push Merrick from agreement to ownership, a negotiation that would cost days she could spend elsewhere.

The fourth session was the money. Every dollar of the frozen CAD 9.6 million had to be re-declared across four lines — trade shows and events, brand and content, digital demand generation, and sales-development capacity and tooling — with the people and systems inside each folded into the line they served (see **Exhibit 2**). The total could not rise, so at least one claimant would leave with less. She would state whether the split followed the segments she had chosen, followed the channels showing the strongest current returns, or held every line roughly where it sat. And she would tell the board which scorecard the two functions reported to: marketing on leads generated and sales on quota attainment, each function measured on the output it controlled, or one revenue-anchored scorecard on which both won or lost together.

Each session closed the same way. The four numbers moved, and the record gained a line — the round, one sentence describing what she had chosen, and where alignment, conversion, growth and revenue lift stood after it.

## Closing

Marchand left the boardroom with five numbers and ninety days: 8% growth, an 18% win rate, 2,000 leads a quarter, 1,560 of them untouched, and CAD 9.6 million that could not grow. Which number named the problem was hers to establish. The freeze was already in effect. Day 1 of 90 began the next morning.
