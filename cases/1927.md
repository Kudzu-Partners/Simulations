# Eastbrook Business School: Building the Mini-MBA

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1927 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1927 — *Build the Mini-MBA* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Saturday, March 14, 2026, Corinne Alvarez laid two documents side by side and read them as one. The first was the spring pre-registration report for Eastbrook Business School's Management Certificate: 165 confirmed seats against 280 enrolled the previous year, a fall of 41 percent, three weeks before the cohort was due to open. The second was a press release: LearnLadder, a venture-backed online platform, had launched a self-paced "Mini-MBA" at US$1,900 — one-fifth of the US$9,500 Eastbrook had charged for the same certificate for nine years.

Alvarez had six weeks. Eastbrook's replacement flagship, its own Mini-MBA, had to be designed, priced and on sale by the end of April to make the fall cohort. The provost had given her two numbers and called both binding: 300 enrollments, and US$1.5 million of contribution to the school after the university's 28 percent overhead. She had also given Alvarez 18 months to put the programme into Singapore and Shanghai, and no incremental budget for it.

## The Protagonist

Corinne Alvarez, 49, had been Associate Dean of Executive Education since 2021. She joined Eastbrook in 2014 to run custom corporate programmes after eleven years at a leadership-development firm in Boston, building the custom book from four clients to eleven before her predecessor retired. The open-enrollment portfolio came with the promotion. She had not redesigned the Management Certificate in the five years she had owned it; the corporate book grew every one of those years, and the certificate held its enrollment until the last two intakes. The provost reviewed her annually on enrollment and on the contribution the office returned to the school.

## The Decision

Alvarez had to decide what the Mini-MBA was, who it was for, what it cost to attend, and how it would reach Asia. The order mattered, because each answer narrowed the next. She had blocked out four working sessions over the six weeks, and each would close before the next opened.

## The Company and its Setting

Eastbrook Business School was the graduate-management unit of a private university in northern New Jersey, founded in 1923 and accredited by the Association to Advance Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB). Its Office of Executive Education was a self-funded auxiliary: no tuition subsidy, with faculty stipends, marketing and the university's 28 percent overhead all paid out of its own revenue. In the most recent fiscal year it earned US$6.8 million across open-enrollment courses, custom corporate programmes and the Management Certificate, and returned the same US$1.5 million of contribution — 22 percent — to the school (see **Exhibit 1**). Forty people staffed it, its courses taught by tenured professors on stipends and adjunct practitioners. Sixty-two percent of revenue came from a handful of repeat corporate clients.

The provost, appointed the previous summer, had told Alvarez that executive education was to become a growth engine and a bridge to the university's young campuses in Singapore and Shanghai. LearnLadder was eighteen months old and had raised venture funding twice; its Mini-MBA was recorded, self-paced and sold direct, with no cohort, no live faculty and no academic credit. Eastbrook's competitive set had not previously included anything of the kind.

## The Situation

The pre-registration number was the visible damage. The deeper exposure sat in the corporate book. The two anchor accounts paid Eastbrook US$2.6 million a year between them, 38 percent of office revenue, and in February both had asked why they should keep paying Eastbrook's price when LearnLadder was quoting theirs. Losing either would breach the US$1.5 million commitment on its own. Margin had been narrowing before LearnLadder arrived. The university had raised the overhead charge to 28 percent for the current year, and contribution margin per seat on the legacy certificate had fallen from 26 percent to 19 percent at the unchanged US$9,500 price.

The office tracked four numbers, and Alvarez expected to be asked about all four. Confirmed seats, against the institutional target of 300. Contribution in US dollars after the 28 percent overhead, against the US$1.5 million commitment. A differentiation index, scored 0 to 100 from buyer interviews and alumni surveys: what buyers said Eastbrook offered that a recorded platform did not, read by the office as its leading indicator of referral and repeat enrollment. It stood at 30. And a reach score, also 0 to 100, recording progress against the Singapore and Shanghai mandate; it stood at zero. Beside them ran a single cumulative figure for the marketing and design money committed.

If the certificate went on sale unchanged, the spring cohort would close at or near 165 seats at 19 percent margin, the flagship would not exist, and differentiation and reach would stay where they were. The provost had been explicit about a second consecutive miss against the 300-seat target: open enrollment would be contracted to an online programme manager that kept 60 percent of revenue and controlled the brand.

## What Alvarez Controlled

The first session settled two questions and one dial. What job the Mini-MBA did in the portfolio: a scalable front door feeding corporate accounts and the full-MBA pipeline; a lower-priced standalone product replacing the legacy certificate; a response pitched near LearnLadder's price and built to win on volume; or a small, high-priced programme aimed at a narrow senior audience. Then the one advantage a venture-funded platform could not replicate: live accredited faculty, a cohort that met, and a stackable credential; higher production quality than the platform's recorded lectures; a lower price, competing with the platform on cost; or none, on the reading that executive education was becoming a commodity. The dial, 0 to 100, set how sharply she committed to one kind of learner and one promise rather than keeping the programme broad.

The second session built the programme. Format came first: it fixed the fully loaded cost per seat, the seats a cohort could hold, and what the design offered that LearnLadder's did not. The choices were fully online and self-paced; hybrid, live online modules plus two in-person residencies; or high-touch in-person with fly-in faculty (see **Exhibit 2**). Then the module count, from four to twelve against a market convention of six to ten. Then the credential: stackable credit toward the full MBA, a non-credit executive certificate, or a digital badge. Then the spend on what was not modules — capstone, employer co-design, alumni access, faculty office hours — at US$0, US$0.3 million or US$0.6 million.

The third session set price and the enrollment plan. Four points were on the table: US$2,200, within reach of LearnLadder's US$1,900 and aimed at the same volume; US$3,500, a little over a third of the legacy price, leaning on volume to reach 300; US$5,900, the middle of the band the office had modelled; US$8,500, roughly a tenth below the legacy price, carrying the number on fewer seats. Alongside price she set what share of the 300 seats went to the two anchor corporate clients rather than individuals, from none to all; the volume discount those clients were offered, from zero to 50 percent; and the marketing and enrollment spend to fill a six-week funnel, at US$0.2 million, US$0.5 million or US$0.9 million.

The fourth session settled Asia and the scorecard. Four ways into Singapore and Shanghai: co-delivery with the university's Singapore campus under a revenue share; exporting the online build with light localization; flying faculty out for in-person Asian cohorts; or licensing the curriculum to a regional institution for a fee. She had to name the source of the money: the programme's own contribution, a further request to the provost, or a further domestic price cut covered out of added volume. She had to set the measures she would defend over 18 months — enrollment, net revenue, learner satisfaction and reach together; enrollment and net revenue, without satisfaction or reach; or enrollment alone, one number for the period. A second dial, 0 to 100, fixed how firmly she would hold the plan when the executive board pressed for a larger headline number.

## Closing

Alvarez wrote the two numbers at the top of a clean page — 300 seats, US$1.5 million — and under them the four sessions and their dates. The first ran on Monday. The fall catalogue closed at the end of April, and the provost had asked for the Asia plan and the scorecard in the same meeting as the price.
