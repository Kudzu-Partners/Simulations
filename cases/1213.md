# Banco Central de Costaria: Four Meetings from 3.50%

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1213 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1213 — *The Central Bank Junior — Setting the Rate When You Are Sixteen* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 08:30 on a Tuesday in mid-February of Year 3, Lucía Benavente-Orozco, three weeks into her term as Governor of the Banco Central de Costaria (BCC), opened the quarterly briefing pack at the head of the committee table. The policy rate stood at 3.50%. Gross domestic product (GDP) was growing at 4.2% a year, against a trend the staff put near 2.5%. Consumer price inflation had reached 3.1%, a tenth of a point above the top of the bank's 1–3% target band.

By 14:00 the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) had to announce two things: what it would do with the rate, and the tone of the one-page statement that would explain it. Benavente-Orozco would put both to the room. Markets would price them the next morning. The committee would then meet three more times, in the third quarter of Year 3 and in the first and third quarters of Year 4, before its tenure ended.

## The Protagonist

Benavente-Orozco was forty-one, the youngest Governor the bank had appointed. She had spent eleven years in its research department, the last four running the inflation-forecasting unit, and then two years at the finance ministry's debt office. She knew the forecasting models well. She had never voted on the rate.

Four of the committee's seven members, herself included, had been seated in the same month after the previous committee's terms expired together. Inside the bank the staff called them the junior committee. The decision belonged to the committee; the proposal and the statement belonged to her.

## The Decision

At each of the four meetings the committee settled two things together: the change in the policy rate and the tone of the statement. The rate could be cut by 50 or 25 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point), held, or raised by 25, 50, 75 or 100 basis points. Nothing else was on the table (see Exhibit 2).

Only the first meeting's data was in the room. The briefings for the other three would arrive with their quarters.

## The Company and its Setting

The BCC's statute gave it two goals: inflation between 1% and 3% over the medium term, and employment as high as that goal allowed. Its principal tool was the policy rate, the overnight rate at which it lent to and borrowed from Costaria's commercial banks. Its rate-setting meetings fell in the first and third quarters of each year, six months apart. Each decision was published at 14:00 with a one-page statement.

Costaria was a mid-sized economy with its own currency, the Costarian dollar (CO$). Its current expansion had begun two years earlier, and the staff counted the years of the cycle from that trough, which made this Year 3.

The morning after each decision, the reaction arrived in four places: the ten-year government bond yield, the CO$ exchange rate, the Costaria Equity Index and the newspaper headlines. Camila Ferrer-Lagos, head of the markets department, read that reaction to the committee at 09:00. Her staff also kept a credibility index, a market-trust score set at 100 when the new committee was seated. It rose or fell after every meeting as bond desks, analysts and the press judged both the move and the words. The running sum of those changes, the committee's cumulative score, stood at zero.

## The Situation

The briefing pack read as a boom. GDP growth at 4.2% was 1.7 points above trend, and the staff estimated the output gap, the distance between actual output and what the economy could sustain, at +1.2%. Unemployment had fallen to 4.8%, below the 5.5% the staff took as its structural level. Wages were growing at 4.5% a year and bank credit at 9.0%, and both were accelerating. Headline inflation was 3.1%. Core inflation, which left out food and fuel, was 3.0% (see Exhibit 1).

Not every number pointed the same way. At 3.50% the policy rate already sat half a point above the staff's 3.00% estimate of the neutral rate, the level that would neither push the economy forward nor hold it back. Some members read that gap as policy that was already slightly tight. Others noted that, with inflation at 3.1%, the rate stood only 0.4 points above prices. Core inflation was still inside the band. Rodrigo Salcedo-Ibarra, the bank's chief economist, reminded the committee that the neutral estimate carried a wide margin of error. He added that a move made in February would reach prices only months later. Some members used that point to argue for moving early, others for moving gently.

Outside the building the arguments were louder. A columnist in the largest business daily wrote that the bank was already late and should raise the rate by a full point. The employers' federation warned that dearer credit would stall an investment cycle that had barely started. The union confederation pointed out that real wages had only just begun to grow again.

If the committee did nothing, the position was plain. The rate would stay at 3.50%. The economy would go on running above trend, with wages and credit accelerating and inflation at the top of the band. The next meeting, six months later, would open on whatever that half-year had built.

## What Benavente-Orozco Controlled

She had two instruments at every meeting. The first was the size and direction of the rate move: seven settings in steps of 25 basis points, from a 50-point cut to a 100-point increase. The rate carried forward, so whatever the committee set in February became the starting point for the next meeting.

The second was the tone of the statement. The communications staff kept five standard framings, and each meeting required exactly one. A dovish statement tilted the risks toward a slowdown. A neutral statement presented a balanced outlook. A data-dependent statement described a calibrated move and promised to watch the coming figures closely. A look-through statement treated a price jump as a supply shock the bank would not chase, while promising to act if it spread into wages and other prices. A hawkish statement committed the bank to further tightening.

What she watched came in three layers. The dashboard showed the policy rate, headline and core inflation, GDP growth, unemployment, the output gap, wage growth, credit growth, the credibility index and the cumulative score. The morning after brought the bond yield, the exchange-rate move, the equity-index move and the headlines. The secretariat's meeting log recorded, for each meeting, the move in basis points, the new rate, the statement tone, the inflation print recorded once the decision was known, the change in the credibility index, and a one-line verdict from the markets staff. The verdicts ran from "on the curve" through "acceptable", "borderline" and "misjudged" to "shock to credibility".

## Closing

At 12:40 the committee broke for lunch with the vote still open. Salcedo-Ibarra's staff had drafted two statements and left the third paragraph of each blank. Ferrer-Lagos had the morning's bond prices on her tablet and no consensus among the desks she had called. Benavente-Orozco had eighty minutes to decide which number she would put to the room, and which words would carry it to the market the next morning.
