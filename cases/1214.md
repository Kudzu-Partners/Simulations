# The Costaria Cabinet: Thirty Billion and Three Audiences

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1214 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1214 — *Fiscal Policy Workshop — Tax & Spend in a Recession* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 14:40 on Tuesday, September 15, 2026, Ximena Arrieta-Valdés, Minister of Finance of the Republic of Costaria, carried one folder into the Cabinet crisis room. It held the draft of a CO$30 billion fiscal package, about a tenth of the country's annual output, and a one-page agenda with three items. Unemployment had reached 8.7%. The central bank's policy rate stood at 1.50%, close to its floor.

The President had asked the Cabinet to leave the room that evening with a package it could defend. The agenda would be taken in order, each item closed by a Cabinet vote before the next opened: how the CO$30 billion would be split across six uses, when it would be spent over the next two years, and what Arrieta-Valdés would say to the bond market, to voters and to the International Monetary Fund (IMF). The proposal on each item was hers to make.

## The Protagonist

Arrieta-Valdés was fifty-two. She had spent fourteen years in the Treasury's forecasting division and four as director of public debt, before the President brought her into the Cabinet eighteen months earlier. She knew how the bond desks that bought Costaria's paper read a budget. She had never designed a stimulus, and she had never defended one at a town hall.

## The Decision

The first decision was the composition: how much of the CO$30 billion went to each of six levers, with every billion placed and none left over. The second was the timing: whether to front-load the spending, split it evenly or back-load it across Year 1 and Year 2, and how much, if any, to hold back as a reserve for Year 2. The third was the message: one line of argument for each of three audiences, all drawn from the same package.

Each vote locked its item.

## The Company and its Setting

Costaria was a small open economy with its own currency, the Costarian dollar (CO$), and output of about CO$290 billion a year. A fuel-price shock had pushed inflation to 4.2% and then tipped the economy into recession. Output had fallen 2.8% over the last four quarters, and the Treasury put the output gap at −2.5%. Unemployment had risen from 5.5% to 8.7%. Inflation had fallen back to 2.4%, and consumer confidence was at a ten-year low (see Exhibit 1).

The Banco Central de Costaria had cut its policy rate from 4.25% to 1.50%. Bruno Lastra-Peña, the deputy governor who attended the session as the bank's liaison, reminded the room that the bank had little room left to cut. He did not speak for the bank's independent rate committee.

Public debt stood at 62% of gross domestic product (GDP), moderate by the standards of larger economies. Part of it, however, was held in foreign currency, and the bond desks that priced it had begun to ask how high the ratio would go. Government bonds yielded 3.50%. The welfare system was already supporting demand without any Cabinet decision: as incomes fell, benefit payments rose and tax receipts fell, worth about CO$8 billion over the two years.

## The Situation

The Treasury's briefing set out six levers (see Exhibit 2): a cut in personal income tax, a temporary cut in value-added tax (VAT), higher transfers and benefits, infrastructure spending, public-sector hiring, and retraining with other active labour-market programmes. For each, staff gave a range for the multiplier, the output generated per CO$ spent in a deep recession, along with a typical delay before the money reached the economy and the groups who would feel it first.

The draft in the folder was a placeholder of CO$5 billion on each lever. Nobody in the room had argued for it. Paula Senra-Ochoa, Minister of Infrastructure, wanted at least CO$15 billion for roads, schools, energy retrofits and broadband; the projects, she said, would still be standing when the recession was over. Óscar Villalba-Rey, Minister of Labour, wanted CO$9 billion for benefits and CO$6 billion for retraining, because the queue at his employment offices had doubled. Ignacio Prado-Lucena, Minister of the Economy and leader of the smaller coalition party, had promised voters relief and wanted CO$10 billion in income tax cuts and CO$8 billion off VAT. The three requests added up to CO$48 billion.

If the Cabinet changed nothing, the placeholder would go forward as drafted. Without any package at all, only the automatic support would be working, and the Treasury's baseline had unemployment easing only slowly from 8.7%. Most of the CO$30 billion would be borrowed, so the debt ratio would rise whatever the mix. How far it rose, and how the bond market read it, depended on choices still open.

## What Arrieta-Valdés Controlled

On composition, she proposed an amount for each of the six levers, in steps of half a billion, with the six adding to exactly CO$30 billion.

On timing, she had three spending profiles. Front-loading put CO$25 billion into Year 1 and CO$5 billion into Year 2. An even split put CO$15 billion into each year. Back-loading put CO$10 billion into Year 1 and CO$20 billion into Year 2. Villalba-Rey argued that the people losing their jobs that autumn could not wait for Year 2. The debt office, her former team, argued that a slower build-up of borrowing would be easier for the bond desks to absorb. Separately, she could hold back up to CO$10 billion as a contingency for Year 2, in case the recession deepened.

On the message, she would answer each audience once. A correspondent from the Vantrell wire service had asked how Costaria would stabilise its debt ratio. Her communications director wanted a short assurance that the government would be fiscally responsible, arguing that any number given to a wire service would be quoted back for years. The Treasury had drafted a glide path back to a lower deficit, set out with the package's arithmetic and anchored on the independent fiscal council. The debt office had prepared a detailed model of Costaria's borrowing costs.

At a televised town hall, voters would ask why the package was split the way it was. Treasury economists wanted her to walk the hall through the multiplier arithmetic, so that nobody could say the numbers had been hidden. The party's campaign staff wanted jobs, kilometres of road and retrofitted homes. Two ministers wanted a shorter answer: experts had built the package, and it could be trusted.

The IMF's regular review mission, due the following month, had asked for a written view on how the package interacted with monetary policy. She could argue that fiscal and monetary policy were separate domains with separate institutions, which was how Lastra-Peña's bank preferred to put it. She could make the case for coordinating the two while the central bank was near its floor. Or she could defend the package on grounds of national circumstance and sovereignty, the line the President's office favoured.

She would watch the Treasury dashboard: the package deployed; the average multiplier across the package; its estimated GDP impact in CO$ billions; jobs created; unemployment; the debt ratio; the bond yield; and a bond-market credibility reading out of 100, which would stay at zero until she had answered the markets. A chart traced unemployment, the bond yield and the debt ratio over the eight quarters, and the Cabinet secretary's log recorded one line for each vote.

The President's policy unit would score the afternoon out of 100 when the session closed. It would look at the output the package bought per CO$, the fall in unemployment by the end of Year 2, how the three audiences received her answers, and where the debt ratio ended. The score carried one of four grades, from Beginner to Cabinet-grade.

## Closing

At 14:55 Arrieta-Valdés laid the placeholder on the table. Senra-Ochoa had brought a map of projects ready to start. Villalba-Rey had brought the month's jobless claims. Prado-Lucena had brought the coalition agreement, with the tax pledge marked, and Lastra-Peña had brought the bank's last rate statement. The first vote was set for 15:30, and the Vantrell correspondent's question was already in her inbox.
