# Helio Verde: The Slate

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1505 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1505 — *CEO Succession at Helio Verde* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

At 7:48 on a Thursday evening in late October, Lucía Arenas-Beltrán was reading six executive profiles for the second time when the chair of Helio Verde's board called. Manuel Beltrán-Rojas kept it short. When she walked into the November board meeting in two weeks, he said, he would not ask her to recommend a successor. He would ask her to defend a slate. "The slate is the choice the board makes with you. The successor is the choice the board makes alone." He wanted three names she could defend and, if she chose, a fourth.

Arenas-Beltrán was not leaving; twenty-six months into her tenure as chief executive, she expected several more years in the seat. But the board had put a 24-month succession process on its calendar, and the first decision in it was hers: which of the six internal executives on her desk would go to the board as the slate, and what she would do about the chief financial officer's retention risk. When the line went quiet she wrote SLATE NOT SUCCESSOR on her legal pad and underlined it three times.

## The Protagonist

Arenas-Beltrán had been hired from outside the company. Her first hundred days had gone on diagnosis rather than announcements, and the diagnosis had become the strategy: Helio Verde's growth was constrained by its capability in modern retail, the supermarket chains that now set the terms of trade across the region. She had since pushed through a plant reconfiguration, hired a new chief financial officer and backed a new account with a major Mexican supermarket chain. The share price had risen 31% since her first day.

Her chief of staff, Tomás Ulloa, had drafted the six profiles, listed them alphabetically, ranked none of them and kept his own opinion off every page.

## The Decision

The succession calendar ran through four decision moments, named for the months remaining before the planned transition (see Exhibit 2): at T-24, now, a slate of three internal candidates with an optional fourth, and a plan for the chief financial officer; at T-15, one development move per slate candidate; at T-9, a recommendation on whether to open the process to external candidates; and at T-3, a written recommendation to the full board naming one finalist. The board, not the chief executive, would make the final choice.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde S.A. was a branded consumer-foods company headquartered in Escazú, Costa Rica, selling through neighbourhood stores and modern-retail chains across Central America, Mexico and the Caribbean, with revenue of roughly USD 1.1 billion in the last fiscal year. Its sharpest competitors were regional food groups expanding into its markets. Under Arenas-Beltrán it had built joint business planning with its largest retail customers: annual plans negotiated account by account.

Manuel chaired the board. Patricia, the lead independent director, chaired the nominating committee, which would oversee any external search.

## The Situation

Tomás's six profiles covered every internal executive the board could plausibly consider (see Exhibit 1).

Andrés Quiroga-Beltrán, 46, the chief commercial officer, had been promoted eighteen months earlier on Arenas-Beltrán's recommendation after running the Panama–Guatemala region. He had executed the modern-retail planning discipline and framed problems the way she did. He had never run an international profit-and-loss account (P&L).

Renata Coralles, 53, the chief operating officer, had spent twenty-five years at Helio Verde, nine of them running its largest plant; the operating reflexes the company ran on were largely hers. She had never given an earnings call. Patricia regarded her as the candidate of operational continuity.

Beatriz Solís, 50, the chief human resources officer, was known for her read of the company's culture. She had never run a P&L or managed a country. Six months earlier Manuel had asked Arenas-Beltrán to think about her "with more seriousness than the literature would tell you to."

Diego Ferraris, 44, the chief financial officer Arenas-Beltrán had hired, had built the financial case for the repositioning and was composed on earnings calls. He had received two approaches from executive-search firms in six months, had mentioned neither and had not asked for a retention conversation.

Eva Restrepo-Mendívil, 41, president of the international division, had joined eighteen months earlier and had built the Mexico supermarket account from a cold start. Two of her strategic calls had since been borne out. Directors described her as a candidate for the 2030s being discussed in a 2027 conversation.

Federico Ulate-Brenes, 48, the country head for Costa Rica, had ten years at the company. Before Arenas-Beltrán was hired he had been widely assumed to be the next chief executive. He was loyal and locally admired, and he had never had an international assignment. Their working relationship was professional and distant.

Manuel and Tomás had agreed to track the process on four readings, each scored from 0 to 100 and each starting at 50: ego discipline, how far the process resisted her own pull toward candidates who thought and worked as she did; slate robustness, whether the slate would still hold if the constraint on the company's growth shifted before the transition; board alignment, whether the chair and the lead independent director could stand behind the process and its outcome; and process integrity, whether she authored the slate and left the choice to the board.

If she arrived in November without a slate, the board would have to build one without her, the search firms' approaches to Diego would continue unanswered, and all four readings would remain at 50.

## What Arenas-Beltrán Controlled

For November, she chose three or four of the six names. For Diego she had three courses: open a retention conversation over the next 60 days, running in parallel with the slate; take no special action and wait to see whether he raised the approaches himself; or put him on the slate, giving him a visible stake in staying. The first addressed the risk at the price of naming it, the second avoided signalling anything to an executive who had asked for nothing, and the third made the slate partly an instrument of retention.

At T-15, each candidate on the slate would receive one development move: a twelve-month rotation to head the Mexico business, carrying an international P&L; external visibility through earnings calls, the analyst day and the press; a P&L stretch in a new Strategic Initiatives Office with a scaffolded P&L; a Corporate Strategy seat with exposure to the cabinet and the board's cadence; or no move, observing the candidate in the current role. Only one candidate could be at the leading edge at a time, and every move had to stand on its own as an operational decision. She would also tell the board whether at least one move exposed a candidate to a constraint other than the one the company faced today, or whether all of them sat inside the current operating reality.

At T-9, if the nominating committee's search firm surfaced outside names, she and Manuel could decline to open the process and keep the slate internal, interview only the external candidate whose experience bore on the growth constraint, or interview every external candidate the search firm put forward. Declining signalled confidence in the bench but gave the board no outside benchmark; one interview gave a single benchmark but some risk of a leak; the full list gave the widest comparison but the greatest risk of a leak. Word of any interview could reach the market and the internal candidates.

At T-3, the memo would name one finalist. It could be a six-paragraph narrative of about 700 words or a slide deck with parallel bullet points; Manuel had long preferred narrative for governance decisions, and most boards she knew received succession recommendations as decks. If a director preferred the alternate, the memo could address the alternate's case directly, leave that case out entirely, or counter it by listing the alternate's weaknesses.

## Closing

Arenas-Beltrán laid the six profiles in a row in Tomás's alphabetical order and pulled a clean sheet of the legal pad toward her. The November meeting was fourteen days away, and Manuel had said they would talk on the Sunday before.
