# Harrow & Vale Foods: The Inflection Point

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1817 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1817 — *Scaling the Venture* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of 13 January 2026, Priya Anand took a phone call in her office above the production floor in Banbury, Oxfordshire. Northgate Grocers, a national supermarket chain, wanted Harrow & Vale Foods to supply own-label chilled soup to 240 of its stores — a contract worth £6.2 million a year, against a business she had built to £9.4 million in revenue over the previous three years. Northgate wanted an answer inside ninety days.

The number was the easy part to like. Northgate also wanted volumes tripled within four months, would pay on 75-day terms, and had written service penalties of £8,000 into the contract for every major failure. Harrow & Vale's cash buffer stood at £0.45 million; the ramp itself was expected to cost £1.4 million before the first large invoice was paid. And Priya still personally signed off every recipe, every key customer relationship, and every item of spending above £2,000. Whatever she decided over the next ninety days, she would be deciding on four fronts at once: whether the business was actually ready, how hard to grow, what to stop doing herself, and how to pay for it.

## The Protagonist

Priya Anand had founded Harrow & Vale Foods and owned all of it. In three years she had taken the company from a small regional supplier to a £9.4 million business employing 78 people on one production site, at an 11% EBITDA margin — growth she credited to relationships she had built herself, with growers, with buyers, with the first accounts that had stocked her soups. The habit that had built the company was the same habit now in question: nothing of consequence moved through Harrow & Vale without passing across her desk first.

## The Decision

Before anyone discussed Northgate itself, Priya's leadership team had to agree on what would break the company. They had to rank, by danger, four candidate threats: a production site already at 82% of practical capacity with no room for tripled volume; the gap between a £1.4 million ramp requirement and a cash buffer of £0.45 million; every recipe and every meaningful spending decision still needing Priya's personal sign-off; and a single customer controlling 40% of revenue, with the pricing and exit leverage that came with it. They also had to name the dominant risk of scaling unprepared: Priya's own burnout, as every decision kept landing on her desk; service penalties piling up as the ramp outpaced the operation; the view that £6.2 million of new revenue would carry the business; or insolvency, staying profitable on paper while running out of cash. A quieter call sat underneath: how candidly to admit the business was not ready to scale as it stood.

Only then came the shape of the growth itself: a phased entry at 120 stores ramping over eight months; accepting all 240 Northgate stores in a single move; declining Northgate in favour of the existing online subscription channel; or a blended commitment splitting part of the Northgate volume with continued online growth. Whichever path she chose, she also had to create the capacity for it — stretch the existing site past its current 82% utilisation; commit £0.6 million to a new chilled production line; or outsource part of production to an outside partner — and decide how hard to negotiate volume caps, minimum terms and diversification commitments into the contract itself.

Breaking her own bottleneck was a decision in its own right. She could hand off spend authority, key accounts and hiring together while keeping recipe sign-off for herself; keep approving everything herself, with the ramp routing through her; or move recipes, accounts, spend and hiring out of her hands all at once. Whatever she handed over, something had to replace her judgement in those decisions: nothing formal, so the business kept moving on instinct; documenting decision rights, approval limits and quality standards; or trusting whoever inherited it to use their own judgement, without written thresholds. Layered on top was whether to bring in a £65,000 operations director ahead of the ramp — leave the existing team to absorb the extra load; wait until Northgate revenue arrived before adding the cost; or hire now, before the revenue arrived, building the capacity early — and a dial on how firmly to protect the recipe and brand standards she alone had built.

Last came the money, summarised in Exhibit 2: take a bank growth loan of £1.0 million at around 9%; sell 25% of the company to a regional growth fund; fund the ramp from cash flow and stretched supplier terms alone; or blend a smaller bank loan with stretched supplier terms. Timing was separate from the instrument — raise it before the ramp began, on terms available today; wait for early traction on the Northgate line and raise afterward, on a better valuation; or hold off raising anything until, and unless, the cash actually ran short. Two further calls sat alongside: what share of the funding gap the structure would actually cover, and how firmly to argue, in a four-minute presentation to her non-executive advisor, that it strengthened the business rather than merely funding it.

## The Company and its Setting

Harrow & Vale Foods Ltd made premium chilled food, soup chief among it, for UK grocers and for a fast-growing online subscription channel, from a single production site in Banbury, Oxfordshire. It employed 78 people, carried an 11% EBITDA margin on £9.4 million of revenue, and had never taken outside capital: Priya owned all of it.

## The Situation

Four numbers framed the ninety days (Exhibit 1). Cash on hand was £0.45 million. The ramp Northgate required was projected to cost £1.4 million before its first payment landed, 75 days after delivery began. The contract itself was worth £6.2 million a year, against a base of £9.4 million. And the production site already sat at 82% of practical capacity before any new volume arrived. Her non-executive advisor, briefed on the numbers, put the stakes plainly: taken well, Northgate could carry Harrow & Vale toward £20 million; taken badly, it could break the company outright.

Opinion inside the business was not settled. Some read the £6.2 million and treated the rest as a detail to manage after signing. Others worried about what tripled volume would do to Priya personally, given how much of the business still ran through her. Others pointed at the 75-day payment terms and the thin cash buffer and asked, plainly, where the money would come from in the meantime.

If the ninety days passed without a decision, Northgate would look elsewhere. A rival supplier would take the 240 stores, and Harrow & Vale would carry on as it was — a £9.4 million business, still growing on Priya's own energy, still signing off every recipe and every invoice over £2,000 herself, with £0.45 million between the company and its next emergency.

## What Priya Controlled

Across the ninety days, Priya's leadership team would set a ranked diagnosis of the business's binding constraint and the survival risk it posed; a growth path, a capacity plan and a level of contract protection; a delegation plan, its governance and a staffing decision; and a financing instrument, its timing and how much of the funding gap it actually closed. Four measures would sit against whatever they chose: how much of the company's scale the growth had achieved; a resilience-and-survival score, with a stated target of at least 60 out of 100; how many decisions that used to require Priya personally no longer did; and the cash runway itself, in pounds, where a figure below zero meant the company had gone insolvent mid-ramp.

## Closing

At her desk, Priya set the Northgate term sheet beside a single sheet of paper on which she had written four questions: what would break the business first, how hard to grow, what to stop doing herself, and how to pay for it. The clock behind her counted the ninety days down. Whenever she was ready, she would have four minutes to say all of it out loud to her own non-executive advisor.
