## Abstract

Nexum S.A., a mid-cap industrial-technology manufacturer headquartered in Turin, Italy, with 2025 revenue of USD 340M and EBITDA margin of 11.4%, entered 2026 with an R&D portfolio of eight named projects: three in Discovery, two in Development, two in Scale-Up, and one in Launch-Prep. The projects spanned the three Baghai-Coley-White horizons: Horizon 1 (core-business improvement) accounted for four projects; Horizon 2 (adjacent growth) for three; Horizon 3 (disruptive, transformative) for one. The incoming Chief Innovation Officer, Elena Trebbi, had been given an annual R&D budget of USD 18M — an increase from 2025's USD 14M but below the industry-peer benchmark of 6% of revenue (USD 20.4M). Trebbi had to allocate the budget across the eight projects, while holding Stage-Gate discipline, respecting the three-horizon balance, and acknowledging that at least one project carried a known Innovator's-Dilemma signal (a potential cannibalization of a 22%-margin core-product line by a lower-margin disruptive alternative). The case opens on the Sunday before her first portfolio-committee meeting and presents three internally coherent annual plans.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate R&D portfolio-allocation decisions under budget constraint, stage discipline, and horizon diversification.
2. Analyze the interaction between Stage-Gate criteria, Real Options thinking, and commercial potential.
3. Design a three-horizon allocation that balances near-term returns with long-run disruption protection.
4. Diagnose the Innovator's-Dilemma signal in a portfolio that cannibalizes a high-margin core line.
5. Recommend a Year-1 plan and articulate the kill-project discipline it implies.

On the night of **Sunday, April 12, 2026**, **Elena Trebbi** sat in the Chief Innovation Officer's glass-walled office in Nexum's Turin headquarters, the Mole Antonelliana visible in the distance, and laid the eight project folders — one per project — in a four-by-two grid on the long table against the back wall. Each folder had been tabbed by her predecessor's assistant with a stage marker, a horizon marker, and a single numerical score. On top of the grid sat a single sheet that read, in large print: *Annual Budget: USD 18M.*

---

## The Protagonist

Elena Trebbi, 48, had been named Nexum's first Chief Innovation Officer in March 2026, in a new role created by the board as part of a governance redesign that separated R&D from Operations. Trebbi had trained as a mechanical engineer at the Politecnico di Milano, holding a Ph.D. in materials science, and had spent twelve years in R&D leadership roles at a Swiss industrial group, where she had built an industry-recognized Stage-Gate process and had, famously, killed a USD 14M project at Stage 3 on the grounds that a small, unfunded adjacent experiment had produced a better signal. She had authored two industry-cited papers on Real Options in industrial R&D.

Her contract carried a four-year performance gate tied to the commercialization success rate of Nexum's pipeline and to the composition of revenue by horizon five years from entry.

---

## The Decision

By **April 15, 2026**, Trebbi had to submit, for portfolio-committee approval, a Year-1 allocation of USD 18M across eight projects:

- **P1 — Core pump-seal enhancement** (H1, Scale-Up, NPV USD 42M, risk-adjusted USD 28M, year-1 ask USD 2.2M)
- **P2 — Predictive-maintenance software for pumps** (H1, Development, NPV USD 31M, risk-adjusted USD 19M, ask USD 2.8M)
- **P3 — Low-energy motor line** (H1, Launch-Prep, NPV USD 56M, risk-adjusted USD 42M, ask USD 3.6M)
- **P4 — Industrial IoT platform** (H2, Development, NPV USD 72M, risk-adjusted USD 31M, ask USD 4.2M)
- **P5 — Industrial hydrogen compressor (adjacent)** (H2, Scale-Up, NPV USD 95M, risk-adjusted USD 38M, ask USD 5.2M)
- **P6 — Modular-electrolyzer component platform** (H2, Discovery, NPV USD 140M, risk-adjusted USD 18M, ask USD 1.6M)
- **P7 — Solid-state battery-cooling system** (H3, Discovery, NPV USD 320M, risk-adjusted USD 26M, ask USD 2.4M)
- **P8 — Software-defined pump-as-a-service** (H1/H2 hybrid, Discovery, NPV USD 180M, risk-adjusted USD 24M, ask USD 1.8M — **cannibalizes P1 and ~22% of core pump revenue at maturity**)

The total of the eight requests was USD 23.8M against a budget of USD 18M. Three projects would have to be de-funded or under-funded. In addition Trebbi would declare a portfolio stance: *Core-Defend (H1 priority), Adjacent-Lean (H2 priority), Disruption-Bet (H3 priority), Balanced-70/20/10.*

## Company Background

Nexum had been founded in 1954 in Turin as a pump-and-valve manufacturer and had, over seven decades, built itself into a mid-cap industrial-technology group across three product families: industrial pumps (52% of 2025 revenue), specialty motors (31%), and industrial software (17%). 2025 revenue was USD 340M, with an EBITDA margin of 11.4% and an R&D intensity of 4.1% (peer median 6.0%). The company's core pump line carried a gross margin of 28% and a contribution margin of 22%; its software line carried 58% gross margin and 39% contribution.

Nexum's customer base spanned 780 industrial accounts across Europe, the Middle East, and selectively in Asia, with the top 25 customers accounting for 44% of revenue. Its installed base of pumps — approximately 180,000 units in service — was both a competitive moat and a strategic exposure: the software-defined pump-as-a-service proposition (P8) would, over five to seven years, transition a meaningful share of the installed base from capex sales to service revenue, trading margin per unit for recurring revenue.

## Industry and Market Context

Industrial-technology manufacturing in 2026 sat inside three structural shifts. First, energy-efficiency and electrification regulation — particularly the EU's IE4/IE5 motor standards and the revised Energy Efficiency Directive — were moving from voluntary to binding across 2026–2028, which both created Horizon-1 obsolescence risk for Nexum's legacy motor line and created Horizon-2 demand pull for its low-energy motor and hydrogen compressor lines. Second, industrial software was moving from optional add-on to expected offering; predictive-maintenance and software-defined-service revenues across industrial peers had grown from 4% of revenue in 2020 to 11% in 2025. Third, disruptive technologies — solid-state cooling, modular electrolyzers, distributed-power — had matured enough that industrial incumbents were deploying modest Horizon-3 bets with five-to-ten-year payoff expectations.

Research on innovation portfolios had converged on four propositions. First, incumbent firms that under-invested in Horizon 3 underperformed on ten-year revenue resilience; firms that over-invested in Horizon 3 underperformed on near-term profitability and, at the extreme, on survival. Second, the commercialization success rate of projects that advanced through Stage-Gate with clean gate reviews was 3.8 times higher than the success rate of projects that were advanced despite weak gate signals. Third, Real-Options-informed discovery investments with optional follow-on capital created higher ROI than linear, front-loaded development programs. Fourth, the Innovator's-Dilemma effect — cannibalization avoidance leading to late Horizon-2/3 response — was empirically observable in 63% of incumbent competitive failures in cross-industry data.

## The Situation

### What Trebbi knew

Her predecessor had, over the past three years, allocated consistently 85/15/0 across the horizons. P8 had been initiated, quietly, by a small team in Nexum's Milan software unit, with a two-person effort and a USD 450K discovery budget, and had produced a functional prototype in early 2026 that was now generating commercial interest from two European water-utility customers. P6 had been placed on "slow" for two years and had a cohort of three technical hires already in place. P3 had passed its Stage 3 gate but was at risk of missing a Q3 launch window without the full USD 3.6M.

### What she did not know

Whether the board's risk-tolerance envelope — new, and not yet expressed in writing — would absorb a 70/20/10 balanced plan or would require a 85/15/0 carryover. Whether the head of the Pumps business unit, whose bonus was tied to pump-business EBITDA, would formally protest P8's funding. Whether the IE4/IE5 regulatory timeline would slip by six to twelve months.

### What others were saying

**Matteo Bianchi**, CFO, argued for **Core-Defend**. *"Hold to 85/15/0. Fund P1, P2, P3 in full. Fund P4 at two-thirds. Do not fund P8 until it clears the first commercial-interest milestone with a third customer. Put P6 and P7 on discovery-hold."*

**Anna Ferri**, Head of Pumps Business Unit, argued for **aggressive Core-Defend with P8 block**. *"P8 cannibalizes the pump EBITDA that funds everything. Do not fund P8 this year. Fund P1–P3 at full. Use the saving for P5 (hydrogen)."*

**Paolo Scaglia**, Head of Software Business Unit, argued for **Disruption-Lean**. *"P8 is the future of the pump line. Fund it at the full ask. Fund P4 and P7 at the full ask. Under-fund P1 and P2. If Pumps's EBITDA drops 1 point in 2027, that is the investment we are making."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — Core-Defend (Bianchi plan, 85/15/0):** P1 2.2; P2 2.8; P3 3.6; P4 2.8; P5 3.6; P6 0; P7 0; P8 0. Unfunded: P6, P7, P8. Near-term EBITDA protected; Horizon-3 absent.

- **Option B — Balanced-70/20/10 (Trebbi draft):** P1 2.2; P2 2.4; P3 3.6; P4 3.2; P5 3.6; P6 1.0; P7 1.2; P8 1.0 (kept alive at discovery). No full de-funds; three under-funds.

- **Option C — Disruption-Lean (Scaglia plan, 50/30/20):** P1 1.2; P2 1.8; P3 3.6; P4 4.2; P5 3.2; P6 1.2; P7 1.0; P8 1.8. P1 and P2 materially under-funded; P8 advanced to development.

Trebbi looked at the eight folders, picked up P8, and placed it, alone, at the top of the grid. Under it she wrote, in pencil: *1.0, conditional.* The portfolio committee would convene at 10:00 a.m. the next morning. She walked to her office window and, across the Piazza, watched the Mole blink on for the evening.
