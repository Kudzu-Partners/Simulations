# Northwind Mobility: The Aurora Car-Seat Decision

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1833 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1833 — *Character Under Pressure* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 October, at Northwind Mobility's design centre in London, Ontario, Meera Kapoor — three weeks into her appointment as Division President — heard the Head of Engineering & Quality walk her through the pre-launch test file for Aurora, the next-generation car seat carrying 40% of the division's projected growth for the year. The seat had passed every mandatory regulatory sled test. It had also failed Northwind's own internal margin-of-safety standard in three of forty high-temperature trials, all at the upper end of the range. A late-stage substitution of cheaper side-impact foam, made to hit the board's margin target, had saved CA$1.8 million and narrowed that margin. The seat was legal to sell exactly as built.

Kapoor had six weeks before Aurora's committed on-shelf date of 25 November, and CA$62 million of first-year revenue riding on it, to decide what to do about a product that cleared the law but fell short of her own engineers' standard. Fixing it meant a new foam supplier and re-tooling: CA$4.2 million, and a seven-week slip past the holiday shelf window. Whatever she chose, she would have to explain it to a board that had installed her three weeks earlier specifically to protect margin.

## The Protagonist

Kapoor, 46, had spent eleven years running supply operations at a mid-sized appliance manufacturer before Northwind's holding-company owner recruited her to run the car-seat and stroller division. She had a reputation for hitting delivery dates without cutting shop-floor corners, which was why the board wanted her in the seat. She had never run a product-safety call of this size, had not worked closely with the Head of Engineering & Quality who had put the sled-test results in writing rather than raise them quietly, and had no track record yet with a board whose patience under pressure was untested.

## The Decision

The easy version of the question was "ship or delay." Kapoor's harder problem was that the numbers alone did not resolve it: a lawful product, a broken internal promise, a launch date already sold to retailers, and a board whose stated priority was margin. She had to decide not only what to do, but which considerations counted and how she would defend that weighting to people who had not been in the room when the foam was chosen.

## The Company and its Setting

Northwind Mobility Inc., founded in 2004 and based in London, Ontario, designed and manufactured car seats, strollers and child-restraint systems across Canada and the northern United States, built as the safety-first challenger to larger global brands. It generated CA$310 million in annual revenue at a 9% EBIT margin (CA$27.9 million), across a design centre and two assembly plants employing 1,400 people. Eighteen months earlier, a value-oriented holding company had acquired Northwind and installed a cost-reduction programme, with a board now impatient for margin expansion. Aurora was the year's flagship launch precisely because it was meant to prove the acquisition case, without touching the safety promise the brand was built on.

## The Situation

If Kapoor changed nothing beyond confirming the existing plan, Aurora would ship on 25 November as promised, at the same narrowed margin the internal tests had just flagged. Three big-box retailers had already allocated end-cap space and taken pre-orders against that date. Missing the window instead — the cost of the fix — was estimated at CA$22 million in lost first-year sales, plus the likely loss of an end-cap slot to a competitor (see Exhibit 2). Carrying the narrowed margin into the field instead risked a recall-and-reputation event Northwind's finance team sized at CA$30–80 million, enough to test a brand built on "beyond the minimum." Four measures, each zero to one hundred, tracked her position entering the decision: balance at 30 (whether any single consideration was already dominating her thinking); decision quality at 25 (how sound and explainable her reasoning was, independent of outcome); stakeholder trust at 40 (the board, the quality team, the retail buyer and the customer combined); and risk at 35 (deadline and safety exposure carried forward, lower being better). A remediation-and-mitigation budget of CA$6.0 million sat uncommitted (see Exhibit 1).

## What Kapoor Controlled

Her first choice was how to name the problem: financial (weigh CA$22 million of lost sales against the CA$4.2 million repair cost); legal (the seat had passed the mandatory test); political (weigh how the board would read her choice in week three); or all three considerations weighed together, with the legal test treated as one input rather than the whole answer. Layered on that was which instinct was most likely to take over unnamed under the board's margin pressure — the pull of the deadline, deferring to whoever pushed hardest, a caution so complete no decision got made, or consulting so widely the window closed before anyone committed — and which instinct was most likely to go missing under the same pressure: the nerve to own the foam call personally and deliver an unwelcome verdict, the drive to hit the date regardless, fairness extended to the board but not the engineer who raised it, or none of that. Threaded through both was how explicit and nameable she made her own reasoning, from barely articulated to fully spelled out.

Next came the action itself: delay, absorbing CA$4.2 million and a seven-week slip to change the foam supplier and re-tool; ship on schedule, on the basis that the seat had already passed the mandatory test; a phased release — a verified sub-batch shipped now, the end-cap arrangement renegotiated, the remaining units brought to standard on a rolling basis; or ship on the original date without documenting the failed internal results anywhere formal. Whichever she chose, she had to state how much weight it put on four things at once — courage in delivering the news upward, restraint against the deadline and the margin number, accountability for owning the foam decision personally, and collaboration in using engineering's full data — with none of the four allowed to swamp the rest. And she had to name exactly what the choice cost: this quarter's margin and the holiday window, nothing at all, or the internal safety margin itself.

Once committed, three people would test the decision at close range. The board chair, demanding margin and the date be protected, could be met with the reasoning and the data, accommodated by reversing course, or confronted head-on with no data offered. The Head of Quality who raised the flag was watching whether Kapoor would credit that courage publicly, acknowledge it only privately, or make clear that concerns this close to a launch were unwelcome. The retail buyer threatening to pull the end-cap could be told the truth and offered a credible new date, promised the original date regardless of whether it held, or given what was asked at the standard's expense. And if engineering found the fix could run in five weeks rather than seven, she had to decide how far that evidence moved an already-committed plan, from not at all to completely.

Further out, after the quarter closed, she had to decide how to read what happened: soundness treated as separate from the result, judged only by the number, or credited entirely to her own foresight. A journalist asking about the foam substitution could get a full account of the standard, the data and the fix, a narrower answer confirming only what was asked, or a flat statement that a legal product left nothing to discuss. And for the next such moment, she had to name which capacity most needed building — courage, temperance and accountability under pressure; more drive to hit deadlines; or none — and say, in a figure she set herself, how firmly she would commit to a standing practice built around it.

## Closing

By nine that morning the sled-test file sat on Kapoor's desk, the CA$4.2 million and the seven-week number confirmed by engineering, and the calendar read six weeks to 25 November. The board chair's assistant had already asked for fifteen minutes at Thursday's meeting. Kapoor closed the file and started drafting what she would say.
