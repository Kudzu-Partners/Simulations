# AgroSensa: Two Clocks and One Budget

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1921 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1921 — *Tech Pivot* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 11 May 2026, Valentina Ospina laid two documents side by side on the table in AgroSensa's engineering room in Medellín. They had reached her four days apart. The first was a press release. A Brazilian agritech backed by venture capital — the largest competitor AgroSensa S.A.S. had — announced it was entering Colombia with a cheaper soil sensor and a plan to buy share. Its distributors would be selling in roughly five months. The second was a draft term sheet from a Spanish agricultural distributor offering AgroSensa exclusive distribution across the European Union, conditional on a CE-certified, EU-compliant product delivered within nine months.

Before the end of May, Ospina had to give the two founders a funded plan: which of four candidate projects the fixed COP$4,200-million research budget (written COP$4.200 millones in Colombian usage, where the dot marks thousands) would pay for, whether and how AgroSensa would cross into Europe, and in what order the work would reach customers. Priced by her own engineers, the four projects came to about COP$7.600 millones (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Protagonist

Valentina Ospina, 38, had been AgroSensa's Innovation Manager since 2023. She had joined in 2019 as a firmware engineer on the low-power sensor that became the company's first granted patent, and took the role when the founders decided that choosing what to build had become a bigger job than building it. She ran the research group inside a company of 78 people and reported to Iván Restrepo, co-founder and chief executive, and to the board. Both patents carried her name among the inventors. The national innovation award the company won two years earlier had brought approaches from distributors in Mexico, Peru and Spain; the Spanish one had turned into paper.

## The Decision

Ospina had to decide what AgroSensa would fund, what it would defer, and what it would stop — and to defend the answer to the founders and the board at the end of the month. Three commitments were live at once and the money covered fewer than all of them: defending the domestic base against a cheaper competitor, building the next generation of the product, and opening a market abroad. Neither of the outside dates was hers to move.

## The Company and its Setting

AgroSensa S.A.S. was founded in Medellín in 2017 by two engineering graduates. It built soil and micro-climate sensors and sold them with a crop-analytics subscription that helped mid-size farms schedule irrigation and inputs. By 2025 it served roughly 430 farm customers across Colombia and booked COP$18.500 millones of revenue at a 6% EBITDA margin. About 60% of that revenue was recurring subscription income, and that line paid for research. It held two granted patents on its low-power soil-moisture sensor.

Its cash position was thin: COP$2.400 millones in the bank and COP$1.500 millones of undrawn credit on an innovation facility. The annual research budget was COP$4.200 millones and the founders had refused to raise it. It was also spoken for — six projects were live, and between them they used it.

Below AgroSensa's core customer sat a price-sensitive segment served with the same hardware, and the entrant had said publicly that it would price under the incumbents.

## The Situation

Nobody disputed the facts, only what followed from them. Restrepo read the Spanish term sheet as the return on nine years of work and wanted certification started at once. The commercial director wanted the whole budget aimed at the domestic base and the Spanish letter answered with a polite delay. The controller wanted to know how many months of payroll the plan left standing.

Ospina's own numbers were few (see **Exhibit 1**); the founders had asked her to report four each period. **Home Defence**, on a scale of 0 to 100, was how much of the domestic base the company held against the Brazilian entrant; it stood at 16. **EU Foothold**, on the same scale, was progress toward a certified European entry, at 3. **Innovation ROI** was the value the research budget returned per peso committed, at 10. **Cash Runway** was months of solvency, at 6.0; below six months, the controller's standing note said, payroll came into question.

Each candidate carried the engineering group's figure for what it would cost to reach customers on time rather than late (see **Exhibit 2**): about COP$2.000 millones for the next-generation sensor answering the Brazilian product; about COP$1.800 millones for CE certification and the hardware adaptation the European directive required; about COP$1.800 millones for an artificial-intelligence yield-prediction module that raised revenue per existing customer on the subscription line; and about COP$2.000 millones for a low-cost "lite" sensor for the price-sensitive segment.

If Ospina changed nothing, the outcome was arithmetic. The six live projects would consume the budget, none of the four candidates would start, the Brazilian sensor would arrive in five months against the product AgroSensa already sold, the Spanish window would close unused at nine, and the COP$2.400 millones in the bank would cover payroll for six months at the current burn.

## What Ospina Controlled

She had to state, first, how she read the position. Four framings circulated in the building: fund the few projects that could win their races and defer or kill the others; fund all four thinly so that nothing was abandoned; put the company's weight behind the European opening; or spend everything on holding the domestic base. She had to commit to a figure for what the Brazilian would take if home went undefended — 15% to 20% of domestic share inside two quarters; something slower and smaller, on the argument that farms switched suppliers reluctantly; or nothing, on the argument that the patents and the award held the customers. And she had to say, in front of the founders, how far her team accepted that the three fronts could not all be run at once, on a scale from full denial to full acceptance.

Then the money. She divided the COP$4.200 millones across the four candidates in any proportion she chose, including zero, and named what would be paused among the six live projects to free cash and engineering time: two legacy projects the group rated lowest, nothing at all, or the recurring platform's maintenance.

Europe was a separate set of choices. AgroSensa could sell through the Spanish distributor under the exclusive arrangement on the table — fastest to market, least capital, least control over price and brand, thinnest margin. It could form a joint venture with the same partner — shared capital, shared risk, a middle level of control, and a commitment Ospina could not verify. It could open a wholly-owned subsidiary at roughly COP$5.000 millones — control of price, brand and margin, and the longest to stand up. Or it could make no European move this year, spend nothing abroad, and let the term sheet lapse. She had to time whichever she chose: secure the domestic base first and run a small European pilot before scaling, commit fully and immediately, or hold the move until home was secure. And she had to fix how tightly European spending was tied to a stated go/no-go test — certification achieved and a first European order booked — from none at all to complete.

Last, the order of the work and the cash behind it. She could ship a simpler product early against the five-month clock and improve it afterwards; hold the five-month clock at home and the nine-month certification window at the same time; or fund the most capable build her engineers could specify, which they put at fourteen months. She had to write the rule for what was cut first if the work slipped or the Brazilian moved faster — certification, the domestic defence, or nothing. She had to set the share of available cash held back against a spend that landed two quarters before the revenue did. And she had to decide how much of the board pitch she spent defending the runway rather than the strategy.

## Closing

At 7:20 that morning, Ospina wrote the four project names down the left margin of a sheet and the two dates across the top. The pitch to the founders and the board was at the end of the month. She had COP$4.200 millones, four projects that wanted COP$7.600 millones, six months of payroll in the bank, and two clocks already running.
