# Phoenix Cascadia FC: The Argentine Ten

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1723 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1723 — *Phoenix Cascadia FC: FSL Expansion Operating Model* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the Monday before Halloween 2025, three things reached Marcus Reilly-Chen's inbox inside forty-eight hours, and all three were waiting for him on the same Tuesday-morning agenda. Season-ticket-membership pacing had missed plan for a third straight month — 9,840 confirmed against a 14,000 target, five months before the season opener. The sporting director's marquee target, a 28-year-old attacking midfielder out of the Argentine Primera known around the front office simply as the Argentine 10, needed a signed answer by 15 November, ten days after the meeting. And the Corvista TV production schedule had landed with nine deliverables due by 1 February that no one had yet been assigned to own.

Camila Restrepo-Salas, Phoenix Cascadia FC's principal owner, had scheduled ninety minutes at 7:00 a.m. to work through all three, starting with the Designated Player question. Marcus had until then to decide how he would frame it for her.

## The Protagonist

Marcus Reilly-Chen, 41, was Chief Operating Officer of Phoenix Cascadia FC, one of FSL's newest expansion clubs. He had been hired eighteen months earlier after four years as SVP of Business Operations at Chattahoochee SC, a stint running ticketing and gameday at the Bayou Ironworks FC before that, and a five-year run at Basepath Properties out of business school at Huron Lakes. Camila was his principal owner-of-record on the league's board and, most mornings, his first phone call of the day.

## The Decision

Marcus had to decide, before the 7:00 a.m. meeting, how to frame the Designated Player question for Camila — sign, wait, or pass (Exhibit 2) — knowing the answer would set the tone for the decision windows still ahead of him. **Frame A** meant signing the Argentine 10 now, before the 15 November deadline, at 14 million dollars a year in cash — a Designated Player slot committed immediately, without waiting to see whether General Allocation Money could have bought the charge down first. **Frame B** meant waiting for General Allocation Money to free up through other roster moves first, so the slot would open on Phoenix Cascadia's own schedule rather than the selling club's, trading certainty that the player would still be available for the chance to lower the effective cost of the slot. **Frame C** meant passing on the individual signing altogether and redirecting that annual commitment toward the academy and the training-facility build already under way, forgoing a marquee face for the inaugural season in exchange for keeping the sporting budget uncommitted.

## The Company and its Setting

Phoenix Cascadia FC operated out of a construction trailer at the edge of the future Sonoran Arena, a soccer-specific stadium going up in the Phoenix–Tucson corridor, ironworkers still bolting the roof canopy into place outside the window. The club had paid a 500 million dollar expansion fee to join the league, arriving in the same fee tier as Coronado Tide FC's 2025 launch. Camila's family, longtime minority investors in a Latin American energy group, had put in the largest share of that fee; a Phoenix real-estate developer and a Tucson hospital-system investor had supplied the rest. The first regular-season match was set for 26 April 2026.

## The Situation

Marcus's dashboard read the same way every morning that autumn (Exhibit 1). Operating cash stood at 105 million dollars against the year-one budget. Season-ticket memberships sat at 9,840 against the 14,000 target. A sporting index, tracking roster quality and coaching fit, stood at 60 out of 100. A brand index, tracking sponsor and media traction, stood at 50 out of 100. Gameday readiness — staffing, ingress, rehearsal — stood at 30 out of 100, with the stadium itself still under construction. The front office's own five-year valuation estimate for the franchise sat near 600 million dollars. If none of the three pending items moved before 15 November, the season-ticket gap would keep compounding, the nine Corvista deliverables would stay unowned heading into their first due date, and the Argentine 10 would be free to sign somewhere else.

## What Marcus Controlled

Between then and opening night, Marcus would face eight such decision windows. This one was the Designated Player frame. The ones still ahead were already on his own project list: how deep to build the bottom six of the senior roster once the Designated Player slot was set, how many events to run on the soft-opening calendar before the stadium doors opened for real, how much of the Corvista TV production tier to commit to, how to approach the Sonoran Front supporters' section taking shape outside the west stand, what pricing posture to take if the season-ticket gap persisted, and how hard to spend on opening night itself. Independent of which of those choices he made each time, he also set three standing dials every window: how much to spend chasing season-ticket conversions, up to two million dollars; how much to spend building gameday readiness through rehearsals and dry-runs, up to a million and a half; and how much to spend on Corvista production quality — documentary access, multi-language commentary, behind-the-scenes inventory — up to a million.

## Closing

At 6:45 a.m., Marcus printed the three frames onto a single page and walked toward the trailer's conference room, where Camila's espresso was already cooling. The deadline was ten days out. The meeting was fifteen minutes out. He had not yet written down which way he was leaning.
