# Cascade Industrial Holdings: Ninety Days to the Capital Allocation Day

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1916 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1916 — *Your Corporate Theory* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, May 12, 2026, Marisol Aguirre read Meridian Point's open letter to the Cascade Industrial Holdings board for the second time. The fund owned 6.4% of the stock. The letter said Cascade "lacks any coherent logic for what it owns," called the company an industrial grab-bag, and put its discount to the sum of its four segments at 22%. It demanded a Capital Allocation Day within 90 days and, failing that, gave notice of a proxy contest for two board seats.

Aguirre had been chief executive officer for ten weeks. Four investment proposals sat on her desk, worth US$1.45 billion against roughly US$1.0 billion she could commit that year. By August 10 she would have to stand in front of Meridian Point and her own directors and say, in one sentence, why Cascade's four businesses were worth more under a single owner than apart — and then show a capital plan that matched the sentence.

## The Protagonist

Marisol Aguirre, 49, had become chief executive officer on March 2, 2026, promoted from president of Flow Control, the valves-and-pumps segment she had run for six years and the largest single contributor to company operating profit. She had joined Cascade in 2011 with a pump maker it acquired and had spent her career inside one segment rather than above four. The board appointed her on a specific promise: restart growth without breaking the company up. Her equity award vested on total shareholder return measured over three years against a diversified-industrials peer group, so the share price was not an abstraction to her.

## The Decision

Aguirre had to decide, by August 10, what if anything Cascade's four businesses had in common and where the year's capital would go. The four proposals in **Exhibit 2** came to US$1.45 billion against roughly US$1.0 billion of capacity, so at least one would have to be refused, and the refusal explained. Four moves stood between the letter and the podium — the diagnosis, the sentence, the money and the day itself — and the order mattered: each narrowed the next.

## The Company and its Setting

Cascade Industrial Holdings, Inc., headquartered in Salt Lake City and listed on the New York Stock Exchange, was a diversified industrial-technology company with US$3.4 billion of revenue, US$510 million of operating profit and 11,000 employees across four segments: Flow Control, Industrial Sensing, Water Treatment Systems, and a small Software & Analytics unit. For two decades it had compounded value the same way: buying undermanaged industrial component makers and improving their operations.

Organic growth had since slowed to 2%. The shares had lagged the diversified-industrials peer group by 18 points. Analysts covering the stock had told investor relations that a narrow, credible account of why the segments belonged together could close about half the 22% discount — roughly US$370 million of equity value (see **Exhibit 1**).

## The Situation

Two instruments told Aguirre where she stood. The first, maintained by investor relations, scored how consistently sell-side analysts could state a single value-adding logic for Cascade's portfolio; it read 30 out of 100. The second was the board's own quarterly reading of growth momentum, at 35 out of 100 and unmoved in a year.

The balance sheet gave her room and a limit. Net debt stood at 1.8 times earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation against a covenant ceiling of 3.0 times; the finance committee had set 2.5 times as the practical limit for new commitments. Peter Lindqvist, the chief financial officer, had put that limit in writing, alongside the roughly US$1.0 billion of new-investment capacity for the year.

Inside the company the argument had hardened. Renata Cho, who ran corporate development, had a full pipeline and a mandate to use it. Howard Teague, the lead independent director, had asked at Aguirre's first board meeting why Cascade owned sensing and water treatment together, and had not been satisfied. Meridian Point's letter closed by observing that a company able to justify its portfolio would have done so already.

If Aguirre changed nothing, the arithmetic was plain. Organic growth would stay near 2%, the discount near 22%, and on August 10 she would appear with a pipeline and no argument — which was what Meridian Point had said she would do. A plan that committed no capital at all would concede the letter's point as surely as a break-up.

## What Aguirre Controlled

**The diagnosis.** Her strategy office had tabled four answers to what, if anything, connected the segments. One was the sensing hardware embedded across Cascade's flow and water equipment, capturing data end to end. One was the company's twenty-year record of buying good businesses and running them well. One was the shared corporate brand and balance sheet behind all four units. One was nothing at all — four unrelated businesses under one roof. The same exercise asked which part of the portfolio had the weakest claim to belong: anything in warehouse robotics, which touched no Cascade customer or plant; the Software & Analytics unit, too small to matter; Water Treatment, sitting awkwardly beside Flow Control; or none of them, the discount being market noise. And it asked what the 22% discount measured — the absence of a value-adding reason for common ownership, a failure to tell the story to investors, or a structural penalty on companies of Cascade's size. She also set how much of the ninety days went into the diagnosis before the team ranked the proposals.

**The sentence.** Four drafts had come back. One said Cascade turned embedded sensing across industrial flow and water systems into mission-critical analytics. One said Cascade created value through operational excellence and disciplined capital allocation. One said Cascade was a high-growth industrial platform pursuing the largest addressable markets available. One said Cascade had no shared logic and should separate its segments. Whichever she chose, she had to divide one hundred points of emphasis across three headings ruled on her strategy chief's whiteboard: where industrial markets were heading, what Cascade distinctively owned, and which outside assets would be worth more under Cascade than under anyone else. She then had to state the boundary it drew — no purchase without sensing-to-systems overlap, however large its market; no low-margin business, but anything profitable kept; or nothing off-limits at all.

**The money.** She could fund any combination of the four proposals. She also had to say how she justified whatever she turned down: on the logic of the portfolio, on the lowest internal rate of return, or not at all, funding everything that cleared the hurdle rate. And she had to set the order of the approved commitments — cross-segment investments first, earnings-accretive bolt-ons first, or the largest growth deal first.

**The day.** She expected the board to press for the biggest growth deal. If it did, she could hold the portfolio and present her rejection as evidence that the logic bound her hands, add the robotics deal to keep the directors onside, or concede the point and propose a separation. She had to put a number on what coherence was worth: the equity value released by closing the discount, the near-term earnings accretion from the bolt-ons, or the combined addressable market of everything Cascade might one day buy. And she had to offer, or withhold, a test that could prove her wrong inside eighteen months — a named metric and date for cross-segment analytics revenue and attach rate, a qualitative judgement of portfolio quality over time, or no test at all. How hard she pressed the argument was hers to choose.

She would be measured on four things: the analyst reading of portfolio logic she started at 30 out of 100; the conglomerate discount she started at 22%; the board's growth-momentum reading, at 35; and how much of the US$1.0 billion she had committed, alongside the leverage that commitment implied.

## Closing

At 7:20 a.m. Aguirre wrote the four proposals down the left-hand side of a legal pad and left the right-hand side blank. Ninety days ran to August 10. The letter was already on the wires, the board wanted growth, and she had US$1.45 billion of proposals and US$1.0 billion to spend.
