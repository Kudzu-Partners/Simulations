# Helventer Industrial Group: The Frozen Portfolio

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1911 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1911 — *Elevating L&D* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Monday, 8 June 2026, at the second-quarter strategy review in Utrecht, Annelies Roskam watched the chief financial officer put one slide on the screen and leave it there for the rest of the session. It reported an internal audit of the previous year's training spend. Of EUR 14.6 million, EUR 5.0 million could be traced to one of the five capability priorities named in the Helventer 2030 strategy. The remaining EUR 9.6 million could be traced to none of them. Roskam had been chief learning officer for six weeks.

Dorine Klaassen, the chief executive, froze the budget where it stood — EUR 14.6 million, no increase — and gave Roskam until the board's next session on 6 September 2026 to present a redesigned learning portfolio tied to the strategy and with its effect on the business shown. If the board was not persuaded, it would cut the function by 30%, or EUR 4.4 million: two regional academies closed, 11 of 40 roles removed, and whatever survived folded into a shared-services centre.

## The Protagonist

Roskam, 44, had spent fourteen years at Helventer before anyone put the word "learning" in her title. She had joined as a field service engineer in Rotterdam, run the Benelux service organisation, and then built the commercial team that sold the company's first uptime contracts — the business Helventer 2030 was written to scale. She had argued more than once, in writing, that the training function was disconnected from that shift. In April 2026 the executive committee gave her the job. She had inherited the catalogue on the CFO's slide; she had not built it.

## The Decision

Roskam had to decide what Helventer's EUR 14.6 million bought, and prove the answer to a board that had stopped believing the previous one. Within ninety days she had to classify 312 courses against the five 2030 priorities, decide what to retire and what to build, choose how each capability was delivered, commit the function to measures it did not collect, and put the result to Klaassen and the chief financial officer, Pieter Havinga. Every euro spent on a new capability had to come out of something she stopped doing.

## The Company and its Setting

Helventer Industrial Group N.V., founded in 1961 and headquartered in Utrecht, made precision pumps, valves and flow-control systems for the water, energy and process industries. It employed 9,400 people and had reported EUR 2.1 billion of revenue at an 11% margin on earnings before interest and tax.

Helventer 2030 was the pivot away from selling hardware and towards selling outcomes — uptime, efficiency and remote monitoring, sold as multi-year service contracts. The engineering was largely in hand; the workforce was not. The strategy required roughly 2,800 employees to be reskilled in digital-service, data and consultative-selling work, skills the company had never hired for at volume and that its salespeople, who had spent their careers quoting equipment against a specification, did not have.

## The Situation

The audit put numbers on something that had been visible for years (see **Exhibit 1**). The largest single block of spend in the 312-course catalogue was legacy compliance refreshers, vendor-run product courses and leadership workshops that filled every time they were offered and mapped to none of the five priorities: EUR 9.6 million.

The programmes that did serve the priority capabilities reached 380 employees a year, against the 2,800 a year the strategy required. Delivery ran through instructor-led classroom teaching at the regional academies, at a blended cost of EUR 1,180 per learner; digital and blended delivery cost EUR 190 per learner (see **Exhibit 2**).

Measurement was the part Klaassen had been most direct about. L&D reported completion rates and end-of-course satisfaction and nothing else; none of the 312 courses carried a measure of what it changed in the business. On an internal scale from 0 to 100, the function's own reading of the evidence it could produce beyond those two stood at 5. Klaassen said satisfaction scores would not be accepted in September.

If Roskam changed nothing, the arithmetic was not in dispute: the share of spend tied to a 2030 priority would stay at 34%, the priority programmes would keep reaching 380 people a year against a requirement of 2,800, and she would arrive on 6 September with the completion reports the board had just rejected.

## What Roskam Controlled

The diagnosis came first. She could trace every euro to a named 2030 capability and reclassify well-attended but unmapped workshops as unaligned; count leadership and well-liked workshops as partially aligned, on the argument that general capability serves any strategy; rank the catalogue by completions and satisfaction, on the argument that the programmes people chose were the ones that worked; or present the prior year's spend unclassified and promise a fuller review after September. She had to name one source of misalignment for Havinga — the EUR 9.6 million of inherited spend, the EUR 1,180 classroom cost per learner, the small number of people reached, or no single source at all — and decide how much evidence to put behind the classification, from an assertion to a documented trace of every course.

Then the money. Any amount from nothing to the whole EUR 9.6 million could move out of the unaligned spend and into the capability gap, with nothing added from outside the freeze. She had to set the share of priority-programme delivery that ran digital or blended, from none of it to all of it, with classroom teaching making up the remainder. And she had to decide whether coached delivery was held for the relational, judgement-heavy capabilities — consultative selling and leadership — with only scalable foundational content digitised; whether those capabilities ran mostly self-paced with some live practice; or whether everything moved to the cheapest digital format to put the largest number of people through.

Then the evidence. For three flagship programmes she had to commit to what the function would report: business outcomes, in behaviour change and leading operational indicators — service-contract attach rate, mean time to resolution — feeding a business-impact index; learning, in test scores and knowledge checks taken at the end of each programme; or activity, in the completion and satisfaction data already collected at no added cost. She had to say how the chain from a programme to that index was defined: as a baseline, a named owner and a named data source for each measure, with the weakest link stated openly; as a direct claim that the programmes had moved the commercial metric, the chain itself taken as evident; or as a single headline index, on the argument that the board wanted the result and not the derivation. And she had to decide how much of the budget went into data, owners and dashboards, from nothing to EUR 1.5 million.

Then September. She could put the before and after on all four measures in front of Klaassen and Havinga with an explicit list of what she had retired to fund the shift, inside EUR 14.6 million; lead with the case for a learning organisation and leave the figures in an appendix; or argue that the freeze made the transition impossible and ask for EUR 18 million. She had to name a cadence — a standing quarterly review that re-scored alignment and rebalanced what was built, retired and sustained; an annual review at budget time; or none, the redesign itself being the deliverable. And when Havinga tested one assumption in the room, she could concede the weakest link in her chain and show the evidence behind the rest, hold every assumption, or point to how highly the programmes were rated.

The board would judge the redesign on four numbers: the share of spend tied to a 2030 priority, the employees reached each year in the priority capabilities, the blended cost per learner, and the reading of the evidence the function could produce.

## Closing

Roskam left the review with the audit slide printed and four numbers written along the top of it: 34%, 380, EUR 1,180, and 5. Below them she wrote the two dates, 8 June and 6 September, and the figure the board had put beside the second, EUR 4.4 million. The catalogue ran to 312 lines. She had ninety days and not one euro more than she had walked in with.
