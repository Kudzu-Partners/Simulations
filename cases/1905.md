# Vossberg Mess- und Regeltechnik: Adapt or Anchor

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1905 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1905 — *Adapt or Anchor* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Tuesday, April 21, 2026, Ines Brenner read the loss debrief on Rheinaue Wasserwerke twice, then read the margin page underneath it. Rheinaue had been Vossberg's largest customer for nineteen years: EUR 43 million a year, 6% of the business unit's revenue, and the reference account on the first slide of every tender. It had gone to a competitor whose sensors were, by Vossberg's own laboratory measurements, less accurate and cheaper — sold with a subscription that told the utility which pumps were going to fail. The margin page said the hardware business had held 38% gross margin in the quarter, after giving up two to three points a year for three years.

Brenner had two quarters. At the end of them she would present an adaptation plan to Dr. Georg Halbach, chief executive of the Kirchner engineering group, and to the group board. Halbach had attached a EUR 60 million investment envelope for the following two years and one condition: there would be no more of either the money or the time.

## The Protagonist

Ines Brenner, 49, had been business-unit head of Vossberg since January 2023. She had joined in 2011 to run the flow-meter line, and from 2018 to 2022 she had run calibration services — certifying instruments already installed in customers' plants — which she grew from a warranty obligation into the unit's steadiest revenue. Her bonus, like that of every senior manager at Vossberg, moved with the hardware profit-and-loss account: its operating profit and its order intake. The engineers she rated most highly worked on sensor accuracy, and they reported to her through the core.

## The Decision

Brenner had to divide EUR 60 million, and her own attention, between the business Vossberg had and the business the market appeared to be building around it — then decide where in the organisation each half would sit, who would be paid for what, and how fast the shift would show in the revenue mix. Halbach wanted the plan in two quarters. The group board had told him it would form its own view of Vossberg within eighteen months.

## The Company and its Setting

Vossberg Mess- und Regeltechnik AG, founded in Stuttgart in 1962 and part of the Kirchner group since 1998, made industrial sensors, flow meters and process-control hardware for chemical, food and water-treatment plants. It employed 3,100 people and closed 2025 with EUR 720 million of revenue and EUR 115 million of operating profit — a 16% margin (see **Exhibit 1**). 84% of that revenue came from the hardware core: instruments sold once, installed for a decade or more, and serviced under calibration contracts.

The line between the instrument and the software reading it was dissolving. Software-native firms — Talvi Systems was the one Vossberg met most often — bought commodity sensors from contract manufacturers, sold them near cost, and earned their margin on analytics subscriptions running at 60% to 75% gross. Vossberg's hardware gross margin was 38%. Customers who had once specified accuracy to four decimal places were writing tenders that specified uptime.

Digital Ventures — twenty-eight people under Marek Sattler, on an annual budget of EUR 9 million — already built analytics on top of Vossberg instruments. It reported into the hardware profit-and-loss account, where its spending was a cost line and its results were reviewed on hardware measures: gross margin, order intake, units shipped.

## The Situation

Two further large accounts were evaluating the same competitor. Hanne Ostermann, who ran the sensor and flow-meter core, wanted the money spent on taking cost out of the instrument, holding price where accuracy justified it, and selling calibration deeper into the installed base: work that paid inside a year and that Vossberg knew how to do. Sattler wanted it spent on the analytics layer, where the recurring margin sat and where the installed base and twenty years of calibration data were an advantage no software firm could buy. Both were claims on the same EUR 60 million.

Halbach would judge the plan on four measures and had given Brenner the reading on each (see **Exhibit 2**). Profitability was the operating profit the plan projected against the EUR 115 million the unit earned now. Innovation pipeline, scored 0 to 100, was the credible future value of whatever new business she funded; the planning office put Vossberg at 20. Organisational fit, on the same scale, was whether her proposed structure would let a new business survive inside a company built around the old one: 55. Adaptation speed was the pace of her shift measured against the pace of the industry: 15. A fifth line tracked euros committed against the EUR 60 million cap.

Nothing in the current year forced her hand. Order intake was steady, calibration renewals ran at their usual rate, and the 16% margin would survive the year on the installed base alone. Left as it was, the core gave up two to three points of gross margin a year, the two evaluations would resolve on their own timetable, and the board's eighteen-month horizon was the only fixed date other than her own.

## What Brenner Controlled

**The reading.** She had to state where the value in her industry was moving. Four readings were live in the building: to the analytics and subscription layer, where recurring gross margins of 60% to 75% sat while sensors commoditised; nowhere yet, the instrument being genuinely differentiated and the 16% margin the evidence; into a price war, with rivals buying share at discounts they would have to stop; or into calibration and field service, since installed instruments would always need maintaining. She had to say how much of the 16% margin was profit earned on an installed base at risk of churning — a large share, very little, or none — and put a figure on how fast the shift was running, from zero, meaning years away, to one hundred, meaning the window was closing now.

**The money.** The EUR 60 million had to be committed in full across three lines: taking cost out of the hardware, defending its price and growing calibration services; building the analytics and predictive-maintenance platform inside Vossberg; and acquiring a small software firm or hiring software engineers. She also had to state the payoff horizon assumed for the second and third — staged over two to three years, crediting value as milestones were met; inside a single year, booking the upside at once; or an unspecified long term with no date.

**The reporting lines.** Digital Ventures could stay inside the hardware profit-and-loss account, sharing its resources and overhead; become a separate unit with its own account, measures and incentives, reporting to Brenner; or hold its own account while sharing a technology platform and a sales force with the core. She had to specify what it shared: the installed base, sales relationships and calibration data, while keeping its own measures; nothing at all, on a clean break; or everything, measures and bonus pool included. And she had to set how far incentives moved, from zero — hardware bonuses untouched — to one hundred, venture measures fully ring-fenced.

**The clock.** She had to name the share of revenue she would target on subscription within two years, from none of it to 60%, and say how each further tranche would be released: against revenue, retention and capability milestones; in full at the outset; or with no formal gate, reviewed annually with the board. She had to decide in advance how she would answer competitive news arriving during the two quarters — pulling investment forward where milestones were met and re-basing the gates, holding the plan as written, or moving the remaining money into the new business at once and announcing a 50% subscription target. And she had to decide how firmly to hold the plan in front of the board, from zero to one hundred, against the pull of a louder headline.

## Closing

Brenner laid the Rheinaue debrief and the margin page side by side and drew a line down a clean sheet between them. On the left she wrote what Vossberg earned; on the right, what it did not yet sell. The board met at the end of the third quarter. She had EUR 60 million, and no version of the plan in which both columns got what they had asked for.
