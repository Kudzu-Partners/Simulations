# Open or Closed?

On the morning of 8 June 2026, Livia Conti opened two folders her patent counsel had left on her desk. The first was Brennaro Sistemi's annual patent-portfolio review, flagging three families covering a silicon-carbide inverter-control architecture — two years ahead of any rival, earning almost nothing, unused outside one low-volume product line. The second was a letter from a Japanese automotive supplier offering nine million euros up front, plus a running royalty, to license that architecture. The offer expired in six weeks.

Conti, Brennaro Sistemi's Chief Technology and R&D Officer, had twelve months and a budget of twenty-four million euros to decide what to license outward, what to build inside the company, and what to bring in from an outside partner — three choices that could not be made separately, since each closed off part of what the others could afford.

## The Protagonist

Livia Conti, 46, had run Brennaro Sistemi's research organisation since 2021, after seven years leading its power-electronics laboratory in Turin. She had joined the company in 2014 from a semiconductor supplier, and had spent much of her tenure arguing that a firm Brennaro's size protected its advantage by inventing and patenting everything itself. That instinct had built the silicon-carbide architecture in the first place — and left it idle for three years while a licensing offer sat unanswered on someone else's desk before it reached hers.

## The Decision

The board had asked Conti to bring the same discipline to opening the company's technology up that she had always brought to closing it down, against a budget that would not cover every option at once. Over the year she had to classify Brennaro's technologies by how central each was to its advantage and how well the company could develop it alone; design the licence-out deal for the silicon-carbide architecture; choose whether to build, buy, or partner for what Brennaro lacked; and bring the result — a blended return, a defensible technology position, a reconciled budget — to a board that would not accept three separate stories.

## The Company and its Setting

Brennaro Sistemi S.p.A., headquartered in Milan since 1991, designed and manufactured precision electric-drive and power-electronics systems for electric mobility, robotics, and industrial automation. The company reported annual revenue of 720 million euros, spent 11% of that on research and development — about 79 million euros — and employed 3,400 people, 480 of them in research. Its operating margin was 9%, and its patent portfolio ran to 210 active families, of which the three silicon-carbide families were, by revenue, worth about as much as the paper they were printed on (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

Brennaro's board had put a number on what was at stake: between 40 and 55 million euros of value over three years, across all three decisions. Each was time-limited in its own way. A German competitor was rumoured to be nine months from a rival 800-volt drive platform. Brennaro's own version could be built in-house for 16 million euros — two-thirds of the discretionary budget — reaching market in roughly 18 months, nine months behind the rumoured rival. A third technology, managing heat inside the platform, was one Brennaro lacked and could not build quickly; closing it by partnership or acquisition would cost between 6 million and 30 million euros, depending on the control Conti wanted (see Exhibit 2). If Conti changed nothing, the silicon-carbide patents would keep earning nothing while they aged, the platform would arrive after the German launch, and the nine million euros would still be on someone else's table.

## What Conti Controlled

For each technology, Conti weighed how central it was to Brennaro's advantage against how well the company could build it alone. The silicon-carbide architecture was either strong intellectual property Brennaro could not fully exploit itself, a candidate to license outward; the company's crown jewel, too central to let out of the building; or intellectual property with no buyer or licensee currently lined up, best simply left to lapse. The 800-volt platform was either a platform better handed whole to an outside contract design house instead; central to the company's own competitive advantage, built well in-house, and best kept closed; or a market not worth chasing. The heat-management gap was either core enough to build from scratch in-house, however long that took; a gap small enough that customers would likely never even notice it; or a capability the company could not build fast enough alone, better sourced from a partner. She also set a number, zero to a hundred, for how firmly her team would commit to opening the portfolio rather than guarding everything itself.

On the silicon-carbide licence, Conti's first choice was to decline it and keep the architecture closed, or accept it and put the dormant asset to work. If she licensed it, she set the deal's boundaries: carve out robotics and e-mobility, Brennaro's strongest ground, and license only outside them; grant rights with no limit on where the licensee could use the technology, for the largest possible upfront payment; or write the licence so narrowly, restricted to one specific application and nothing more. She also set its exclusivity and payment structure: full worldwide exclusivity, with no territorial or field-of-use restriction on the licensee; non-exclusive rights paired with a guaranteed royalty floor; or nine million up front with the running royalty waived. She then set what share of that cash, zero to a hundred, to ring-fence for what came next.

On the platform, Conti could commit sixteen million to build it in-house, for full ownership and control, or skip the in-house build and find an outside partner to supply it instead. On the heat-management gap, she chose among an outright acquisition of that outside technology at thirty million; building the capability from scratch inside Brennaro, at no cost up front; a minority equity stake with technology access at eleven million; or a co-development alliance with its outside developer at six million. She also set governance terms for any partner: a standard non-disclosure agreement, moving quickly and settling ownership later, at no added cost; no governance question at all, since Brennaro was building everything itself, with no outside partner involved; or joint-ownership clauses, firewalls around each side's existing intellectual property, and defined exit rights, for two million. She set a second number, zero to a hundred, weighting speed to market against the control she kept over the work.

At year's end, Conti brought the three decisions to the board as one account: three reasonable deals that did not obviously connect to one another, each defensible taken alone; a single plan in which the licence cash funded the build and the partnership closed the remaining gap; or a decision to reverse course entirely and own every technology the company touched. She also had to say what remained of Brennaro's defensible position: everything in the portfolio still held and owned outright; a narrow, protected core of power-electronics control technology with everything else opened up around it; or a statement that after three deals, no single clear core stood out. On risk and budget, she could bring a general risk summary without reconciling any of it back to the spending; a named list of risk in each decision, reconciled line by line against the twenty-four-million cap; or a pitch leading with the upside and leaving the risks and the arithmetic off the page. Alongside it, she set the blended return, zero to a hundred, she was ready to defend as what the year had earned.

She would be judged on four things: the blended value the decisions were projected to capture over three years, scored zero to a hundred against a target of seventy or more; how defensible Brennaro's position remained, on the same scale; how many months stood between her decisions and a working platform, against the rival's nine; and how much of the twenty-four-million cap she had committed, since licence cash stretched the ceiling but never removed it.

## Closing

At 9:15 that morning, Conti called her IP counsel, her head of corporate development, and her lead platform engineer into her office, and set both folders on the table. The licence offer had six weeks left. The board wanted the year's plan in outline within the week.
