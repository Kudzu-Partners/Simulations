# The Alliance Architect: Structuring the EV-Cell India Joint Venture

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1853 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1853 — *The Alliance Architect* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of 12 May 2026, Kavita Rao, Strategy Director of Sundara Mobility Ltd., laid Voltaic Cells GmbH's signed term sheet on her desk beside a one-page timeline. Sundara's board had given her sixty days to turn that term sheet — a joint venture called EV-Cell India, fixed at a $300 million enterprise valuation — into binding heads of agreement. If she failed, Voltaic was free to sign the same deal with Sundara's largest domestic rival instead, handing that rival the solid-state battery technology Sundara needed and, within two years, an estimated three to four points of Sundara's share of India's electric three-wheeler market.

## The Protagonist

Rao, 41, had joined Sundara in 2014 as a manufacturing planner and moved into strategy in 2019 — the year a foreign licensing partner quietly used Sundara's shared product roadmap to launch a competing three-wheeler in Sundara's own cities. She had watched that failure from the operations side and carried it into every partnership conversation since. Sundara's board had approved exploring the Voltaic venture only if whatever structure emerged could not be undone by a repeat of it. Rao reported to the chief executive, and the final recommendation would carry her name.

## The Decision

Over sixty days, Rao would move through four connected choices: how to read what Voltaic actually needed and how to value Sundara's own contribution; how to architect the equity split and the governance beneath it; how to design the exit terms that would govern the partnership's end before it properly began; and, when a complication landed ten days from the deadline, how to close without losing what the first three choices had built.

## The Company and its Setting

Sundara Mobility Ltd., founded in 2009 and based in Bengaluru, manufactured electric two- and three-wheelers and battery systems. It generated roughly ₹8,200 crore in annual revenue, held an 11 percent share of India's electric three-wheeler market, employed 6,400 people across two plants, and ran a battery-management-system and software team built in-house (see Exhibit 1). The company was profitable, at a 12 percent EBITDA margin, but lacked the solid-state cell chemistry its next vehicles needed and the capital and years to develop it alone.

Voltaic Cells GmbH, a German battery maker, held that chemistry and wanted a manufacturing base, distribution network and regulatory standing inside India — all of which Sundara could offer. Neither firm could get where it wanted without the other, which was why the two sides had signed a term sheet.

## The Situation

The enterprise valuation of EV-Cell India was fixed at $300 million, agreed by both sides already, meaning the sixty days ahead were about how the pie would be split, governed and unwound, not its size. Voltaic's contribution was set: $170 million in cell technology plus $40 million in cash, a combined $210 million. Sundara's contribution — manufacturing capacity, distribution network, regulatory relationships and software team — carried no fixed number; independent valuations put it between $90 million and $130 million, depending on how the software was counted (see Exhibit 2).

A partner-trust index Sundara had tracked since 2019 stood at 55 out of 100, a level both sides' advisors called wary rather than warm. If nothing changed and the sixty days lapsed, Voltaic remained free to sign with Sundara's rival instead.

## What Kavita Controlled

Rao first had three questions to settle. One was what Voltaic actually needed behind its preference for a majority stake: simply wanting the larger equity number, a preference a straightforward 60 percent stake would satisfy; chiefly the size of its dividend share, in which case board seats mattered less; protecting its cell chemistry from leaking to a rival; or board seats matched arithmetically to its equity share. The second was a number — where inside the $90 million to $130 million range to place Sundara's own contribution. The third was which single decision, if lost, came closest to repeating 2019: how much the venture could spend on capital projects each year without a partner's sign-off; who the two boards would accept as chief executive, and whose judgment that person carried; who set the day-to-day price list for what the venture sold; or who could use the shared roadmap once it sat inside the venture, and on what terms.

Next was the structure itself. The equity menu ran from a clear Voltaic majority, 40 Sundara to 60 Voltaic, the fastest structure to sign; a narrower Voltaic majority at 45 to 55, trading speed for a larger Sundara share; an even fifty-fifty split, equal on the register whatever the governance beneath it turned out to be; a Sundara minority at 49 to Voltaic's 51, protected through reserved matters rather than the ownership line; or a Sundara majority at 51 to 49, with Voltaic's protection carried the same way. Layered onto whichever split she chose was how many categories of decision — pricing, capital spending, hiring the chief executive, licensing the roadmap back to either parent — would need both partners' sign-off; how the software would sit inside the venture, licensed in under a clawback returning it to Sundara if the partnership ended, licensed in without a clawback and its fate on a breakup unspecified, or transferred into the venture outright as a jointly held asset; and how a future disagreement between the boards would be resolved — a casting vote held permanently by one partner's chair; an escalation clause to the two chairs and then a forced buy-sell; or no formal mechanism, trusting goodwill to carry the partnership through.

Before the structure was finished, Rao had to design how the partnership would end, long before either side expected to need it. The exit menu offered a targeted package — a lock-in, a right of first refusal and a buy-sell clause — covering the scenarios that mattered most; a stacked package adding tag-along and drag-along rights, a non-compete and a change-of-control trigger; or a light package limited to a short lock-in, leaving every other scenario to the general partnership terms. Attached to it was a lock-in period, in years, before either side could sell its stake, plus a choice on the software if the partnership broke up: an explicit clawback returning it to Sundara with a focused non-compete; joint ownership surviving the breakup, with usage rights split by the terms already in place; or no specific term, leaving its fate to the general clauses. Each choice was information Voltaic would read, so Rao also had to decide how carefully she sequenced and explained every demand, rather than presenting them all at once.

Ten days from the deadline, a complication arrived: Voltaic said it was also talking to Sundara's rival, and asked for a larger licensing royalty back to itself. Rao's response could take one of three forms: concede quickly, on the view that testing a threat this close to the deadline was too dangerous; price the increase in dollars a year against the three-to-four share points a walk would cost, and concede only the smaller number; or hold every term as written, on the view that the terms were already sound, and let Voltaic decide whether walking was worth it. She then had to decide what to flex and what to leave untouched: some of the reserved matters, for softer cash terms, on the logic that speed mattered more now than the veto list; the software clawback, since it had been the hardest term for Voltaic's side to accept; or the royalty, since it was time-limited and reversible, while holding the IP protections and the reserved-matters list where they stood. The close itself was a final choice: present the agreement with the value-capture and risk numbers stated plainly, walk-away included; present the same agreement but soften the protective language, to avoid seeming adversarial; or decline to close until Voltaic withdrew the royalty demand in full. Behind whichever recommendation she made sat one more number: how firmly her final position was backed by that priced walk-away rather than by instinct.

## Closing

At 9 a.m. on 12 May, with fifty-nine days remaining and a partner-trust reading of 55 already logged, Rao opened a blank term sheet on her laptop and began with the first line to fill in: the equity split. Voltaic's counsel was expecting a call by Friday.
