# Chimiq Distribution: The Plan Nobody Owns

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1829 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1829 — Open Strategy · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 16 March 2026, Camille Aubert read the Q1 results from Chimiq Distribution's four hubs a second time, because it did not seem like it could be right. Three of the four hubs had missed their EBIT targets, and when asked why, the hub directors had not blamed the market. They had blamed the plan. Two of them, independently, used the same phrase: the targets had been "handed down, not agreed."

Aubert had been Chimiq's Chief Financial Officer for five months. She had four weeks to redesign how the company made strategy and to present that redesign, together with a new performance-measurement dashboard, to the board. A refinancing term sheet was due to sign in roughly 75 days, and whatever she proposed had to survive that fact intact.

## The Protagonist

Aubert, 44, had spent eleven years in finance roles at a European industrial-materials group before Chimiq's private-equity investor recruited her for exactly what she had done there: helped that company move, over three years, from a strategy set by a handful of executives to one built with the people who ran its plants and sold to its customers. She had taken the Chimiq job knowing the culture she was entering was the opposite of the one she had left. Her mandate from the investor was explicit on one point and silent on everything else: fix the growth problem before the next board cycle.

## The Decision

Over the next four weeks, Aubert had to decide how far to open a strategy process that had run the same way for thirty years: diagnose what was broken about a plan six people wrote behind a closed door; design who would participate in the next one and what information each part of the company would be allowed to see; build the measurement system to run the reopened strategy day to day; then present it to a board that had never trusted a wider circle with anything — and hold the design together the moment it was tested.

## The Company and its Setting

Chimiq Distribution SA, founded in 1991 and family-controlled until a 2021 minority investment brought in its private-equity partner, distributed coatings, adhesives and industrial additives to manufacturers across France and the Benelux from four hubs in Lyon. It employed 1,300 people, including a technical-sales force, and had generated EUR 480 million in revenue at a 7.5% EBIT margin — EUR 36 million — in the most recent year. For three years running, revenue had grown 1–2% a year against a market moving at roughly 4%; the private-equity fund's own investment thesis had assumed faster growth. Strategy at Chimiq had always been set by the founder-CEO and five other executives; the rest of the company received the resulting numbers to execute, not to shape.

## The Situation

The Q1 miss was only the most visible symptom. A pulse survey that quarter found just 24% of managers said they understood how their daily work connected to the strategy, and only 19% believed that year's targets were achievable at all. Separately, the prior year's bottom-up forecasts, once reconciled against what hubs actually delivered, had run 11% above the outcome — numbers submitted with no shared logic and no way to check them against one another before they locked in. None of it could be fixed however Aubert liked: the private-equity board's confidentiality directive, issued after a competitor poached two accounts, and a refinancing covenant of Net Debt to EBITDA at 2.9 times against a 3.25-times limit both bounded how far she could go. If nothing changed, the arithmetic was simple — a fourth flat year, a widening gap to the fund's own growth thesis, and a board and a bank with less reason each quarter to believe Chimiq's numbers (Exhibit 1).

## What Aubert Controlled

The redesign turned on two questions Aubert kept separating in her own notes: who got to help make strategy, and who got to see the information behind it. She could conclude the real gap was inclusion — the people closest to customers and costs had no hand in shaping the plan; that it was transparency — people could not see enough to trust what they were given; that both were true and the fix was to open everything to everyone on both dimensions at once; or that neither diagnosis held and the six-person process was fine, needing only a clearer explanation. She then had to name what the closed process had actually cost Chimiq — an ownership deficit, a forecast bias, lost decisiveness, or lost confidentiality — and what it had genuinely protected that any opening still had to guard: the confidentiality the governance directive required, nothing worth protecting, or the executives' own authority over strategy. A linked dial set how candidly her diagnosis would name the participation failure, from a bare acknowledgment to a blunt one.

From there, Aubert had to design who would participate in the next cycle — a standing forum of hub directors, a large but bounded exercise inviting hundreds of employees onto selected topics, a fully open exercise putting every question to all 1,300 people, or the existing six-person circle with a clearer cascade attached — funded from a transformation budget capped at EUR 600,000. Alongside it sat three classes of information the closed process had always kept in the same six hands: hub-level cost and growth data, profitability by named customer, and the pipeline of acquisitions under review. Each had to be placed, independently, as open to every employee, gated to managers and the forum, or closed to the executive circle alone (Exhibit 2).

The dashboard to run the reopened strategy was its own four-way decision: a system that cascades objectives down from the board, a system of commitments built up from the hubs, a hybrid of both, or an extensive set of board-facing metrics with no equivalent facing the other way. Within it sat a choice of control emphasis — shared measures the hub directors could steer by alongside board oversight, board-facing diagnostics alone with no shared steering, or mission and values with no hard measures or owners — and how many of a planned six to eight measures would be shared widely rather than held to the board. A final rule had to counter the 11% forecast bias: peer review and reference-class comparison before targets locked, a top-down correction to the plan figure, or no correction at all.

What happened after the board saw it was still hers to answer. If the plan leaked first, she could trace the one gate that had failed and close only that, issue a statement treating the leak as a communications matter, or close the whole process back down. If the bottom-up targets landed short of the refinancing plan, she could work the gap with the hub directors, impose the shortfall on them as a corrected number, or accept the lower figure. Facing a board chair who feared leaks and a founder-CEO who feared losing control, she could keep the design, shift to the chair's view and close it back down, or shift to the CEO's view and leave strategy where it had always sat — with a final dial on how firmly she held whichever line she chose.

She would be judged on four numbers the board could watch move over the four weeks: Strategic Alignment, a 0–100 read on whether plans fit reality and were actually owned, starting at 22; Employee Inclusion, tracking the same question the pulse survey asked, starting at 19; Control Effectiveness, the board's and the bank's confidence in the process, starting at 40; and Target Attainment, how far the plan would close on what the refinancing required, starting at 28 — alongside a separate leak-risk read that stood at "Low," and however much of the EUR 600,000 transformation budget she had spent getting there.

## Closing

At her desk that evening, Aubert opened a blank page and wrote two words at the top: Inclusion. Transparency. Beneath them she drew a line for what Chimiq lacked, a line for what it had to keep, and a line for what the board would need to hear in four weeks that it had never been asked to accept before. The term sheet would sign in about 75 days. The six-person door was still, for the moment, closed.
