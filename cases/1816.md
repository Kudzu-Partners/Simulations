# Whitfield Learning Partners: The Brackenmoor Mandate

On the morning of 2 February 2026, Helena Marsh opened the letter that had arrived overnight from Brackenmoor Industrials plc and read it twice before picking up the phone. Brackenmoor, a GBP 4.1 billion engineering group and Whitfield Learning Partners' largest client, had just absorbed a profit warning. Its newly promoted layer of roughly 180 mid-level operations leaders was escalating almost every decision upward, and its safety-incident and project-overrun rates were both climbing. The chief executive's own diagnosis was a "leadership capability gap."

Marsh had ninety days to get a programme contracted, and nine months after that to show Brackenmoor's board it had worked. Brackenmoor would fund GBP 1.8 million to develop the 180 leaders — about GBP 10,000 a head, well below the GBP 13,500 Whitfield normally charged for its flagship design — with GBP 360,000 of its own fee contingent on results Whitfield had not yet agreed to measure. Brackenmoor's HR director had also made it known that two rival providers were already circling with a guaranteed-return pitch of their own.

## The Protagonist

Helena Marsh, 46, had been a Programme Director at Whitfield Learning Partners for eight years, after a decade in leadership-curriculum design for a UK retailer. She had built her name on the accounts other directors found difficult — clients who wanted proof, not goodwill, by contract's end. Brackenmoor was the biggest of those accounts by far, worth 22% of Whitfield's GBP 18 million annual turnover, and she had held the relationship since it was signed. Whitfield's commercial model rested on one unusual promise: a fifth of every programme fee was contingent on agreed impact metrics, verified six months after delivery — and Marsh had spent eight years learning how it could go wrong.

## The Decision

Marsh had to decide, in stages and against a closing clock, what Whitfield would tell Brackenmoor the real problem was, how a programme for 180 leaders would be built inside a fixed budget, which numbers the contingent fee would ride on, and what she would say when a sceptical board pushed back.

## The Company and its Setting

Whitfield Learning Partners Ltd was the custom-programme arm of a UK business school in Bedford, with 64 staff across design, client delivery and operations. It had built its reputation over two decades on bespoke leadership work for large corporate clients, including an industry-shortlisted global programme for an automotive manufacturer — none of which would matter to Brackenmoor's board if the metrics did not move. The engineering group's mid-level leaders had been promoted over the previous two years into roles with wide spans of control but, according to Brackenmoor's own exit-interview and engagement data, no clear mandate to decide on their own and a culture that punished an honest mistake about as readily as a careless one.

## The Situation

The brief gave Marsh a hard set of numbers to design against (see **Exhibit 1**): a fixed GBP 1.8 million ceiling, a fixed GBP 360,000 contingent fee, a ninety-day contracting window and a nine-month deadline for proof. What nobody at Brackenmoor had settled for her was what the 180 leaders actually needed, what a programme built to that budget could plausibly change in them, and which operating numbers — decision speed, safety incidents, project overruns — a programme could honestly claim credit for moving. If Marsh designed for the wrong problem, or built something that impressed in the workshop and evaporated back on site, the board would see no movement at six months, the fee would be gone, and a rival's guaranteed number would likely take the account.

## What Marsh Controlled

Marsh's work fell into four stages (see **Exhibit 2**). First, she had to say what she believed was wrong with Brackenmoor's leaders. It might be a motivation problem, on the theory that the leaders had every tool they needed and simply were not using it. It might be a leadership-skills gap, as the chief executive had framed it, calling for a curriculum built around faster, more confident decisions. It might be a mixed problem, some capability but mostly structural: no mandate to delegate, unclear decision rights, and a culture that punished any admitted mistake. Or it might be a selection error from two years earlier — the possibility that the wrong 180 people had been promoted into the roles. Alongside that call, she had to say what share of the gap a programme could close alone, and how candidly to name the part no classroom could touch.

Second, she had to architect the programme inside the GBP 1.8 million ceiling. She could hold that full depth for only the top 110, telling Brackenmoor that 70 would go undeveloped this round. She could build one blended design for all 180 leaders, depth calibrated to fit the ceiling rather than Whitfield's usual flagship standard. She could build to the full depth of that flagship standard for all 180, matching Whitfield's premium work. Or she could build a light, fully digital programme reaching all 180 cheaply, with no in-person time at all. She then split her design effort across on-the-job transfer — the leader's own manager staying involved, application projects on live problems, and accountability for what was learned; classroom workshops and case teaching, delivered in person by Whitfield's own faculty; and self-paced digital content and microlearning leaders could complete on their own schedule — and committed a spend against the ceiling.

Third, she had to decide how impact would be measured, and what the GBP 360,000 rode on. She could stop two steps short, at reactions and a knowledge test. She could stop at reactions alone, a same-day sheet handed out as each workshop closed. Or she could measure all the way from reactions and learning through to on-the-job behaviour and business results. She then chose the metric the fee rode on: a 30% fall in project overruns within nine months, the headline figure the board wanted; a fall in the group's safety-incident rate, the number the chief executive raised first; a 90% satisfaction score gathered at the end of each workshop; or a leading indicator of decision speed and delegation, tied closely to the leaders themselves. She also had to say how she would credit any change to the programme rather than to everything else at Brackenmoor: with before-and-after measurement alone and no comparison group to rule out other causes, with a matched comparison group tracked alongside before-and-after measurement, or by pointing to the trend and letting the board draw its own conclusion — and how much of the committed budget to spend baselining before it started.

Finally, she had four minutes to put the whole case to Brackenmoor's chief executive and HR director, starting with the return to promise: a single confident figure with no method stated behind it; no figure at all, letting the experience speak for itself; or a range stated openly alongside the method used to isolate the effect. Challenged on scope, she could keep the conversation on the curriculum's quality and leave the structural question unanswered, name plainly what the programme could and could not fix alone, or promise instead it would move every operational number the board cared about. When the HR director raised the two rivals' guaranteed-return pitch, she could match whatever figure it took to win the mandate, cut the design until Whitfield was the cheapest of the three bids, or differentiate on a credible, defensible impact model rather than a promised number — and, whichever way she went, decide how firmly to hold that position once challenged.

Whitfield would judge her, stage by stage, on three things: how well she had found the real problem, how much of what the leaders learned would change their behaviour back on site, and how convincingly the results could be proven and credited to the programme — and, hanging over all three, whether the GBP 360,000 stayed with Whitfield or went back to Brackenmoor.

## Closing

At half past eight, Marsh set the letter down beside a blank sheet of paper and drew three columns across it: what was actually wrong, what GBP 1.8 million could build, and what she was willing to stake GBP 360,000 on. The ninety-day clock had started. She had not yet written a word in any of the three columns.
