## Abstract

CRECE Distribuciones, a USD 32M consumer-goods distributor serving convenience and mid-format retail across central Mexico, signed a master supply agreement with MAXMART — the country's third-largest grocery chain — on February 14, 2026. MAXMART's contract would lift CRECE's 2026 revenue by approximately 43% on prior-year base. MAXMART paid at ninety days. CRECE's customer base had, until MAXMART, paid at fifty-two days on average. The CFO, Ramiro Cervantes, had a cash balance of MXN 4.2M, a Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) of 68 days, and twelve months to survive the surge without a capital raise. Four monthly-reset dials — Collection Effort, Supplier Payment Target, Target Inventory, Receivables Factoring — would drive the CCC, the cash, and the supplier-relationship score across the year. The case opens on the morning of March 1, 2026, as Cervantes prepares the first set of dial settings and the Month-1 treasury recommendation to the CEO.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Diagnose the mechanics of a working-capital crunch induced by a step-change in revenue with a mismatched collection profile.
2. Evaluate the trade-offs among collection effort, supplier-payment terms, inventory days, and receivables factoring as monthly levers.
3. Analyze the cost of factoring relative to the cost of alternative financing and the cost of default.
4. Design a supplier-communication strategy that preserves the relationship score while extending payables.
5. Recommend a Month-1 dial package and specify the conditions under which it will need to be re-tuned.

On the morning of **March 1, 2026**, **Ramiro Cervantes** sat in the CFO office at CRECE Distribuciones' distribution center in Tultepec and read, for the fifth time, the one-page treasury brief his controller had left on the desk at 6:32 a.m. The brief carried six numbers: *Cash MXN 4,200K. DSO 52 days. DIO 34 days. DPO 18 days. CCC 68 days. Current Ratio 1.24x.* Below those numbers, in the controller's handwriting: *"MAXMART's first PO lands April 10. It will be for MXN 820K and they will pay on July 9."* Cervantes had eight working days until the CEO's Monday board call.

---

## The Protagonist

Ramiro Cervantes, 43, had been Chief Financial Officer of CRECE Distribuciones since 2021. An accountant by training (public university, Instituto Mexicano de Contadores Públicos) with an MBA from a Mexican business school, he had spent seven years at a Big-Four audit practice before moving into industry — first as a treasury manager at a Mexican FMCG company, then as controller at a mid-market pharmacy distributor, then to CRECE. He had led two prior working-capital programs: one in 2019 at his previous company that had reduced CCC by 21 days without a write-down, and one in 2023 at CRECE that had brought CCC from 84 days to 68. The new contract with MAXMART had undone, on the signature page, half of the 2023 gain.

---

## The Decision

Cervantes had to set, by 9:00 a.m. on March 10 (the Monday board call), Month-1 values on four working-capital dials the simulation would treat as his Round 1 input:

1. **Collection Effort (1–10)** — weighting of accounts-receivable collection activity: dunning cadence, executive calls, cash-discount offerings, stop-ship triggers.
2. **Supplier Payment Target (15–60 days)** — the target DPO value to negotiate or impose on the supplier base across the top 20 supplier relationships.
3. **Target Inventory (15–45 days)** — the target DIO for finished-goods inventory, which drives replenishment policy and safety-stock levels.
4. **Receivables Factoring (0–100%)** — the proportion of MAXMART and other large-customer receivables sold to a factoring partner at a discount.

His settings would compound across twelve months against a fixed MAXMART ramp schedule and would be legible to the CEO and to the three-bank financing syndicate through three numbers: cash balance, CCC, and financing cost as a percentage of revenue.

---

## Company Background

CRECE Distribuciones had been founded in 2008 by its current CEO, María Elena Ruvalcaba, as a regional consumer-goods distributor serving convenience stores and mid-format grocers across the State of Mexico, Hidalgo, and Puebla. 2025 revenue was MXN 640M (approximately USD 32M), with a gross margin of 14.2% and an operating margin of 3.8%. CRECE carried two distribution centers, a fleet of 42 delivery vehicles, 2,400 active retail customer accounts, and relationships with 186 suppliers (85% of spend concentrated in the top 20). Average customer basket was MXN 4,800 per delivery. Prior to MAXMART, customer concentration had been modest: the largest customer represented 4.2% of revenue.

CRECE's financing structure was a revolving credit facility of MXN 18M (undrawn MXN 11M at start of March) with a three-bank syndicate at TIIE + 350 basis points, and a factoring facility with a Mexican fintech capable of advancing 85% against invoice face value at a discount rate of 1.8% per 30 days (roughly 22% annualized). The credit facility was subject to a current-ratio covenant of 1.15x and a CCC ceiling of 75 days; the CCC ceiling had been informally relaxed to 85 days at the December 2025 banker review, but the current-ratio covenant was hard.

## Industry and Market Context

The Mexican consumer-goods distribution sector had in 2022–2025 experienced two structural changes in payment behavior. First, the large modern-trade chains — CRECE's new customer among them — had extended payment terms from 60 to 90 days on average, passing working-capital requirements from themselves to their distributor base. Second, the mid-market factoring market in Mexico had matured materially, with a group of domestic fintechs offering rapid onboarding and 48-hour advances at rates 200–400 basis points below comparable U.S. benchmarks. Third, supplier-payment discipline at the top-20-supplier tier had, in the distributor segment, empirically correlated with future purchase-price advantages: distributors that paid at the median DPO (32 days) of the segment captured pricing roughly 1.4% better than distributors that paid at 55 days.

Three findings from the post-2020 working-capital literature were consequential for Cervantes's decision. First, factoring more than 50% of receivables at the highest customer concentration produced, on average, a 210-basis-point financing-cost drag but eliminated the default-driven insolvency risk that had killed 18% of mid-market distributors facing a concentration jump above 15%. Second, supplier-payment extension paired with proactive supplier communication (a written extension request and a payment-plan agreement) preserved 90% of the supplier-relationship score; the same extension executed silently destroyed 60% of it. Third, collection-effort levels of 8+ on the 1–10 scale reduced DSO by 6–9 days but damaged 4–6% of marginal customers' repeat-order probability.

## The Situation

### How we got here

CRECE's CEO, María Elena Ruvalcaba, had pursued MAXMART through three product categories and eighteen months of qualification meetings. The February 14 master agreement was a strategic win: MAXMART would purchase five categories across 340 stores, with an expected volume ramp from MXN 820K in April (first order) to a run-rate of roughly MXN 4.8M per month by October. The gross margin on MAXMART volume would be 12.6% — roughly 1.6 percentage points below CRECE's portfolio average, in exchange for volume and for strategic-retailer logo status. The payment term was a take-it-or-leave-it ninety days.

### What Cervantes knew

The internal treasury team had modeled four Month-1 dial packages. A conservative package (Collection 6, Payment 35, Inventory 25, Factoring 20%) projected CCC of 54 days by end of Month 1 and a cash balance of MXN 4,100K — holding flat. An aggressive package (Collection 8, Payment 45, Inventory 22, Factoring 40%) projected CCC of 47 days and a cash balance of MXN 4,600K, at a financing-cost drag of 0.5% of revenue. A defensive package (Collection 5, Payment 30, Inventory 30, Factoring 10%) projected CCC of 62 days and a cash balance of MXN 3,800K, drifting toward the covenant. A crisis-ready package (Collection 9, Payment 50, Inventory 20, Factoring 60%) projected CCC of 41 days and a cash balance of MXN 5,100K, at a financing-cost drag of 1.4% of revenue.

### What he did not know

Whether the top-three suppliers, if pushed to 45-day payment terms, would accept in writing or would quietly derate CRECE's order priority. Whether MAXMART's payment performance would honor the ninety days or drift to one hundred and five, as three peer distributors had reported in December 2025. Whether the fintech factoring partner's discount rate would hold at 1.8% per 30 days once the invoice concentration against MAXMART exceeded 40% of the book.

### What others were saying

**Laura Molinari**, Controller, argued for the **Conservative Package**: *"Hold. Do not spook suppliers. Factor only the MAXMART invoices. Watch the first payment and re-tune at Month 2."*

**Enrique Ruiz**, Commercial Director, argued for the **Aggressive-Defensive Supplier Split**: *"Pay the top eight suppliers at thirty days; push the bottom twelve to forty-five. Pair the extension with a supplier breakfast. Do not factor more than thirty percent."*

**María Elena Ruvalcaba**, CEO, argued for the **Crisis-Ready Package**: *"The board will ask what happens at Month 4 when MAXMART is a million and a half pesos past due. Build the answer into Month 1. Factor sixty percent. Collect at nine. Pay at fifty with written agreements. The financing drag is the price of being alive at Month 4."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Cervantes had narrowed the space to three options.

- **Option A — Conservative Hold (Molinari plan):** Collection 6, Payment 35, Inventory 25, Factoring 20%. End-M1 CCC 54 days; cash MXN 4,100K; financing drag 0.3% of revenue. Supplier score preserved. M4 covenant risk moderate.

- **Option B — Aggressive-Defensive Split (Ruiz plan):** Collection 8, Payment differentiated (top-8 at 30, bottom-12 at 45), Inventory 22, Factoring 30%. End-M1 CCC 49 days; cash MXN 4,400K; financing drag 0.6%. Supplier score holds if breakfast is delivered; falls if it is not.

- **Option C — Crisis-Ready (Ruvalcaba plan):** Collection 9, Payment 50, Inventory 20, Factoring 60%. End-M1 CCC 41 days; cash MXN 5,100K; financing drag 1.4%. Supplier score dips; M4 covenant risk low; full-year gross-margin impact 120 basis points.

Cervantes suspected Option C was right on survival arithmetic and wrong on gross-margin optics; Option B was right on composite but depended on the supplier-communication execution; Option A was the option the factoring partner had privately labeled "the option that requires MAXMART to be on time." At 8:42 a.m. he closed the treasury brief and began drafting the Monday board memo.
