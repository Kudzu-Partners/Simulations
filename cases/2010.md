# Limmat School of Management: A Flagship Under a Margin Floor

**Case Code:** STR-2026-2010 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2010 — *MAS Director's Dilemma* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On 20 January 2026, at the annual portfolio review of Limmat School of Management in Zürich, Rahel Brunner was told that the programme she directed had returned about 12 per cent contribution margin for a second consecutive intake, against a floor of 18 per cent set by the parent university's finance office. The dean's office gave her one design cycle to bring the MAS Business Administration back above the floor.

The cycle closed with the September intake. Brunner had until June to redesign the twelve-module programme — what it taught, what it cost to deliver, what it charged and the order in which it was delivered — and to defend the redesign to the dean's office. If the September cohort again missed the floor, the office would move to suspend the MAS or merge it into a cheaper stackable certificate format.

Three days later, a competing institution launched a modular, employer-co-designed "Management Certificate Stack" priced at 30 per cent below the total cost of Limmat's MAS. Within a fortnight two of the school's largest corporate sponsors asked whether the MAS still earned its place in their development budgets.

## The Protagonist

Rahel Brunner, 44, had directed the MAS Business Administration since 2021. She had joined Limmat in 2013 as a lecturer in operations after seven years in corporate development at a Zürich insurer, and had run the school's CAS certificate portfolio from 2017. She had taken her own qualification part-time while employed, as her students did. The dean's office reviewed programme directors annually on enrolment, contribution margin and completion.

## The Decision

Brunner had to decide what the MAS would be by September, in a fixed order, because each choice constrained the next.

In January she had to put on paper what she believed was wrong: that tuition had simply risen too far and a cut would return the volume; that the programme was sound and under-marketed; that three years of decline was a passing dip; or that several things had moved at once — applied relevance eroding, a cheaper modular competitor arriving, a price-sensitive market, and a cost base that did not shrink when the cohort did. She also had to record what the cohort arithmetic told her, choosing between a reading in which per-student margin was the figure that mattered and one in which total contribution against the fixed base decided viability, and to state the redesign's central tension in a single sentence for the file.

In February and March she had to fix the curriculum. The MAS ran twelve modules (see **Exhibit 2**). She could convert almost all of them to applied analytics, digital and leadership content; leave all twelve generalist modules as they stood; or convert roughly four to applied, sector-specific form and hold the rest as a generalist spine. She also had to settle whether the programme carried a certificate entry and exit point — none, an open exit letting a learner take a CAS and leave at any stage, or a defined CAS entry funnelling into the full MAS with the later modules closed to early exit. She then had to budget the variable cost of teaching one student, anywhere between CHF 9,000 and CHF 20,000.

In April and May she had to set tuition, between CHF 20,000 and CHF 36,000, and the order of the twelve modules, and to say whether the result answered the learner who paid, the employer who sponsored, or both. Sequencing was not only a timetable: the school's drop-out records showed that the term in which a demanding module fell changed how many people left, and the capstone's position changed when the credential and its revenue landed.

In June she had to present the redesign, leading with the margin ratio, with the credential's standing, or with the design as a whole. She had to answer the certificate stack, by matching its price, by differentiating against it, or by declining to engage. She had to name the signals she would watch before September — none, broad interest measures such as web traffic and brochure downloads, or funnel measures such as applications, deposits and employer sponsorship commitments. The review form also asked each director to record, as a percentage, how firmly they held the position they had defended, and the figure stayed in the file beside the numbers.

## The Company and its Setting

Limmat School of Management, founded in 2002, was the continuing-education arm of a Swiss university of applied sciences. It sold part-time qualifications to employed professionals across the German-speaking Swiss market — CAS and DAS certificates and the flagship MAS Business Administration — and had generated CHF 19.6 million of revenue in 2025 across fourteen programmes, with roughly 2,300 active part-time learners.

Two structural facts governed the MAS. First, the programme had two buyers. The learner paid the tuition and needed a schedule that fitted a full-time job. The employer sponsored the fee in many cases, released the learner for teaching days, and judged the programme on what came back into the business. Second, most of the cost of running the programme did not move with the size of the cohort: faculty contracts, rooms and the teaching platform were committed before enrolment closed. That was why the school worked to a minimum viable cohort of about 60 students — below that number the fixed base was not covered, whatever the per-student figures showed.

## The Situation

The MAS was Limmat's largest programme by revenue. Its intake had fallen from 96 students to 71 over three years. Tuition stood at CHF 28,000 for the full programme and the variable cost of teaching one student ran at approximately CHF 15,500. At 71 students the programme returned about 12 per cent contribution margin, six points below the floor (see **Exhibit 1**).

Four measures went to the dean's office each cycle. Projected enrolment was the size of the coming September intake, read against the 60-student minimum and the 96 of three years earlier. Contribution margin was what the programme left after the costs it caused, as a percentage of what it collected. Completion was the share of an intake that finished the qualification; it stood at 81 per cent against a sector benchmark of 88 per cent, and it moved with when in the year people left rather than how many enrolled. The fourth was an internal index on a nought-to-hundred scale recording how well a programme answered both constituencies at once — employer relevance and learner flexibility, balanced. The MAS scored 52. Against those figures the school posted one status per programme, measuring distance from the floor and the cohort minimum: viable, borderline, or at risk. The MAS was at risk.

Inside the school the arguments had been made. Employers wanted fewer generalist modules and more applied, immediately usable content in analytics, digital and leadership. Learners wanted flexibility and the ability to enter and leave with something. Faculty wanted the academic core kept whole, on the grounds that breadth was what made the qualification a Master's rather than a set of certificates. The finance office worked to a rule of thumb for the part-time market: each 10 per cent added to tuition cost roughly eight to ten enrolments.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was fixed. At 71 students, CHF 28,000 of tuition and CHF 15,500 of variable cost, the programme returned about 12 per cent for a third intake, completion stayed near 81 per cent, and the dean's office moved to suspend or merge the MAS.

## What Brunner Controlled

By September Brunner would have committed to a reading of the decline and of the cohort arithmetic; to a curriculum mix across twelve modules and to the terms of any certificate entry and exit; to a variable cost per student between CHF 9,000 and CHF 20,000; to tuition between CHF 20,000 and CHF 36,000; to a module order; to which of her two audiences the design answered; and, in June, to the case she made, her answer to the certificate stack, the indicators she named and the confidence she recorded. She would be measured on the four figures the dean's office already held: September enrolment, contribution margin, completion and the two-sided index.

## Closing

On the evening of 20 January Brunner printed the twelve modules onto a single sheet and ruled three columns beside them: what each taught, what it cost to teach, and the term it fell in. Applications for September opened in June. The floor was 18 per cent, the cohort minimum was 60, and she had one cycle.
