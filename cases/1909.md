# Cumberland Health Brands: Ninety Days to Renewal

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1909 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1909 — *Vested or Bust* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, May 12, 2026, Corinne Vásquez laid two documents side by side on the table in Cumberland Health Brands' Nashville distribution office. The first was a chargeback file from a national drug chain, closing a peak season in which CHB's outsourced fulfilment operation had shipped complete on 88% of order lines against a contractual target of 98%. The second was a memo from the chief financial officer, dated the previous Friday, requiring Vásquez's category to take 12% out of logistics cost — US$7.7 million of run-rate savings — within eighteen months, and to bring a credible plan in six weeks.

Ninety days from that morning, the contract that governed both documents would renew itself automatically for a further term. Vásquez had until then to decide what kind of agreement Cumberland Health Brands would put in front of Apex Logistics Solutions, and how much of the existing one to carry forward.

## The Protagonist

Corinne Vásquez, 41, had been Senior Category Manager for Logistics and Distribution at CHB since January 2026. She had joined in 2022 from a contract-manufacturing sourcing role at a larger competitor, where she ran packaging and raw-materials categories and never held a contract worth more than US$9 million. Logistics was her first strategic category: the Apex agreement had been signed and twice extended before she arrived. Her new manager, VP of Procurement Dinah Okoro, had given her the category with a single instruction — fix the relationship or replace it, and say which by the renewal date. Vásquez's own bonus was tied to delivered category savings and to a service metric she did not control.

## The Decision

Vásquez had to decide what CHB was buying from Apex and how Apex would be paid for it — and to have the answer signed, or the re-bid launched, before the auto-renewal date in August. Four questions had to be answered in sequence, each narrowing the next: what kind of spend this was, what structure the agreement should take, how any savings would be measured and divided, and how the proposal would be argued to Apex and to CHB's own CFO.

## The Company and its Setting

Cumberland Health Brands, founded in 1998 and headquartered in Nashville, Tennessee, manufactured and distributed consumer health and wellness products — vitamins, supplements and personal-care lines — into national drug, grocery and e-commerce channels. It booked US$820 million of annual revenue and ran an asset-light model: it owned formulation, brands and customer relationships, and outsourced nearly all warehousing, fulfilment and outbound transport.

Apex Logistics Solutions had held that work for nine years. The agreement was worth US$64 million a year, CHB's single largest indirect spend, and it priced activity: a rate per pallet stored, a rate per line picked, a rate per shipment, and a service-level schedule backed by penalties. Every operational change ran through that rate card, and every rate-card change ran through a negotiation.

## The Situation

The peak season had made the arithmetic visible. Chargebacks from two national accounts and the expediting CHB had bought to protect the rest cost an estimated US$3.1 million in the quarter (see **Exhibit 1**). A warehouse-automation project, scoped by Apex's own engineers, would cut billable picks by roughly 18%. It had sat unfunded for a year. Against the current rate card, the same 18% removed about US$4.8 million a year from Apex's revenue on the account.

CHB's procurement function scored its largest supplier relationships each quarter on a four-part internal scorecard, and the Apex entry read badly. Incentive alignment — how far Apex's earnings moved in the same direction as CHB's costs — stood at 25 out of 100. Relationship and governance, a composite of trust, willingness to open cost data and the strength of joint decision-making, stood at 30. Deal risk, which aggregated live disputes, service exposure, the strength of CHB's fallback and the renewal clock, stood at 60 out of 100, where a lower number was better. Run-rate savings booked against the CFO's US$7.7 million stood at zero.

Two internal voices had already formed. Sasha Lindqvist, CHB's Director of Distribution Operations, had spent the peak season on escalation calls and wanted the account re-bid. Hollis Pruitt, the CFO, was indifferent to structure and specific about the number: 12%, in the run rate, verifiable, or the function would be reviewed for transfer to a competitor. Okoro had told Vásquez privately that a re-bid was real but expensive: nine to twelve months of transition and US$6–9 million of switching cost, with service risk falling on the e-commerce accounts during cutover. Warren Teague, Apex's account executive, had run this account for four years and could price that transition as accurately as CHB could.

If the agreement renewed on its existing terms, the rate card and the activity pricing would carry for another term, the automation would stay unfunded, peak fill rate would sit near 88%, chargebacks and expediting would keep running at roughly their current quarterly rate, and the US$7.7 million would have to be found somewhere other than logistics.

## What Vásquez Controlled

Vásquez had drawn up a worksheet with four blocks on it (see **Exhibit 2**).

The first block was diagnosis. She had to classify the spend — as a strategic relationship to be redesigned, as a transactional category to be optimised on rate and service terms, or as a failed supplier to be replaced through competitive re-bid — and to name the cause of the plateau: the pay-per-activity structure, under which efficiency reduced Apex's revenue; a decline in Apex's execution that firmer enforcement would correct; rates that had simply drifted above market; or a working relationship between the two account teams that had broken down. She also had to set how firmly she would hold that diagnosis, on a scale from zero to complete commitment, once the negotiation began.

The second block was structure. She could keep the existing agreement and negotiate the rate card down; keep activity pricing and add service-level bonuses alongside the existing penalties; or buy defined results — cost per order shipped, perfect-order rate — with the economics tied to those measures. Whichever structure she chose, she had to decide what the agreement specified: the results, leaving method to Apex; the methods themselves, including pick sequence, slotting and staffing ratios; or a combination. Separately, and under any structure, she had to name the rate reduction she would ask for, anywhere from none to 20%.

The third block was economics. She had to set the share of verified savings that would go to Apex, from none to 60%. She had to decide how value would be measured before it was divided: against a baseline agreed by both parties with cost data open on both sides; against a CHB estimate, reconciled later if disputed; or against a principle now and numbers later. And she had to decide how the parties would handle what the agreement did not anticipate — a new sales channel, a tariff, a demand collapse — through a standing joint body with written guiding principles, through the existing penalty-backed schedule with disputes routed to legal, or through a signed statement of shared principles with no standing body and no decision rights.

The fourth block was the argument itself. To Teague she could present the dollars under the new structure, present the CFO's requirement as a condition of continuing, or lead with the partnership framing and the principles document. To Pruitt she could present savings on a total-cost basis net of anything shared with Apex and net of avoided chargebacks, expediting and switching cost; present the headline 12% and reconcile later; or argue the relationship's value and ask for the target to be eased. She had to decide what to do with the re-bid: state its cost and timeline plainly and use it as a floor, present it as imminent, or keep it off the table. And she had to decide how far she would hold the structure if Pruitt pushed back on the net number.

## Closing

Vásquez put the chargeback file back in its folder and wrote the renewal date at the top of the worksheet. Six weeks to a plan, ninety days to signature or re-bid. Teague had asked for a meeting the following week and wanted to know what he was coming to discuss.
