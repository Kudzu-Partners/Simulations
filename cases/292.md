## Abstract

Textiles Jalisco S.A. de C.V. was, in the case universe, a two-family apparel manufacturer in the Guadalajara metropolitan area — approximately MXN 85m in 2025 revenue, 28 employees, one 620-square-metre plant on Avenida López Mateos, a single three-station line (Cutting at 120 units per hour, Sewing at 75 units per hour, Finishing at 110 units per hour) on which both the work-uniform and the school-uniform families ran, a changeover-cost matrix in which a Work-to-School transition consumed 45 minutes of line downtime and MXN 2,400 in materials and utility costs, a Friday-to-Monday cutoff beyond which the line could not recover a missed order without overtime authorisation, and an April 27 scheduling week in which three orders competed for one finite block of sewing minutes. The case opens at 6:54 a.m. on Monday, April 27, 2026, at the plant's planning office as **Gabriela Ortega-Zavala**, a 34-year-old Production Planner, prepares the one-week sequence.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Read the three-station line layout and identify Sewing at 75 units per hour as the binding constraint, calculate the line's effective hourly output as the bottleneck's hourly output rather than the station average, and articulate why protecting the bottleneck minute — not the cutting or finishing minute — is the operator's first and non-negotiable scheduling rule under Eli Goldratt's Theory of Constraints.
2. Populate the changeover-cost matrix across the Work-to-School, School-to-Work and Same-Family transitions, calculate the total changeover cost of the ABC, ACB and BAC sequences, and articulate why the sequence that minimises the total number of family transitions is the sequence that recovers the most sewing-minute capacity and therefore the most delivered units.
3. Read the long-batch-versus-Heijunka run-length choice against the variable-demand scenario, articulate the EPQ (Economic Production Quantity) trade-off between per-unit changeover cost and in-process inventory carrying cost, and articulate why longer runs lower the per-unit cost at the price of flexibility and why the Toyota Heijunka logic buys back the flexibility at the price of more transitions.
4. Design the Tuesday rush-order absorption posture, trace why the long-batch schedule breaks on a mid-week rush because the line is committed and why the Heijunka schedule absorbs the same rush because every day already carries a fraction of every family, and articulate why flexibility is a capability purchased at the short-batch cost rather than a free attribute.
5. Commit the week-long sequence and articulate why scheduling is not a spreadsheet exercise but the discipline of deciding which minute of which station becomes the gate for the whole week — and why the Line-en-Marcha primer frame is distinct from the capacity-planning and full-ERP-sim frames elsewhere in the catalogue.

On the morning of **Monday, April 27, 2026**, **Gabriela Ortega-Zavala** arrived at the Textiles Jalisco Avenida López Mateos planning office at 6:54 a.m., unlocked the planning-screen cabinet, opened the LíneaEnMarcha scheduling workbook her supervisor had finalised Friday evening, and re-read the note her plant director had taped to the inside of the cabinet: *"El minuto de Costura manda. Todo lo demás se acomoda."*

---

## The Protagonist

Ortega-Zavala, 34, had been Production Planner at Textiles Jalisco since 2023, had completed an Ingeniería Industrial degree at the Tecnológico de Monterrey Guadalajara campus in 2013, had spent six years at a Querétaro automotive-components plant in scheduling and inventory roles, and had joined Textiles Jalisco from the Querétaro role in August 2023. Her scheduling instincts, her plant director had noted in the 2025 performance review, had been formed by two teachings: a Toyota-Production-System study tour she had completed in Nagoya in 2019, and an eighteen-month stint inside the Querétaro plant's 2020–2021 ERP-stabilisation project during which she had watched a well-intentioned MRP schedule run the sewing station out of capacity in week two. The plant director had assigned her the April 27 week on three considerations: her comfort treating Sewing as the binding resource rather than as one of three stations to balance; her willingness to shorten run lengths ahead of the Tuesday rush-order probability rather than commit to a single-family long batch; and her comfort running a Heijunka-leveled schedule against the shop-floor push for longer batches.

---

## The Decision

Ortega-Zavala had to commit, by the 8:30 a.m. supervisor hand-off, a one-week production sequence the line could start at 9:00 a.m.

1. **Order A — 500 work uniforms, due Friday.** Largest order of the week; due at the end of the cutoff; changeover out of work family costs 45 minutes.
2. **Order B — 350 school uniforms, due Wednesday.** Mid-week deadline; the school-district customer is a three-year contract whose renewal reads on-time-delivery percentage as the primary metric; a missed B pushes B to Thursday at overtime cost.
3. **Order C — 250 work uniforms, due Thursday.** Second-largest work-family block; due a day before A; sequencing A and C together saves one full Work-to-School transition against an ABC sequence.

Commitments: a week-long sequence; a run-length choice between a long-batch plan (one A block, one B block, one C block) and a Heijunka plan (forty of each family per day across the week); a Tuesday rush-order absorption protocol; and a one-paragraph cover note that the shift supervisor can read at the 9:00 a.m. start-of-shift huddle.

---

## Sectoral Background

The 2020–2025 Jalisco uniform-manufacturing category had, in the case universe, grown from approximately MXN 2.4bn to MXN 4.1bn, concentrated in two segments (industrial work-uniform and K-12 school-uniform) and four operator tiers (large maquila exporters; regional institutional suppliers; small specialty contractors; informal sewing cooperatives). The 2025 Guadalajara metropolitan area registered an average on-time-delivery rate of 84% in the institutional-uniform segment and 71% in the industrial-work-uniform segment, with significant variance across operators driven primarily by scheduling discipline rather than by raw line capacity. The rush-order probability — defined as a mid-week order landing after the Monday commit — was approximately 22% across the panel and was heavily concentrated in the school-district segment in April-May and August-September.

## Industry and Market Context

The 1984–2025 production-scheduling literature produced three findings the primer sim had to respect. First, Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, operationalised as a bottleneck-minute-reservation discipline rather than as a line-balancing exercise, produced documented on-time-delivery readings approximately 16–34% higher than line-balancing regimes on comparable 2018–2024 small-apparel-plant panels. Second, the Economic Production Quantity tradition, with segment-level rather than plant-level changeover-cost estimates, produced documented gross-margin readings approximately 9–22% higher than single-EPQ regimes on comparable multi-family portfolios. Third, the Toyota Heijunka levelled-production discipline, applied to variable-demand portfolios where a mid-week rush order is more-likely-than-not, produced documented rush-absorption rates approximately 2.4× those of long-batch regimes on equivalent capacity.

The post-2010 operations literature added two findings. The setup-time-reduction tradition — SMED in the Shigeo Shingo line — had compressed average Work-to-School changeovers in the 2022–2025 Jalisco panel from approximately 72 minutes in 2018 to approximately 45 minutes in 2025, but had not eliminated the trade-off between batch size and flexibility. And the digital-scheduling board, deployed as a real-time constraint visualisation rather than as a static MRP output, predicted week-end on-time-delivery to within approximately 4% on comparable week-ahead commits.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The April 20 read-out for the prior week's schedule — an ABC long-batch sequence that ran 500 work, then 350 school, then 250 work — had registered an 84% on-time-delivery rate against a 92% target and had consumed two full Work-to-School changeovers against the one-transition minimum. The April 23 rush-order forecast from the school-district account executive indicated a 40% probability of a 120-unit school-uniform rush for a Tuesday morning delivery the following week. The plant director's April 24 email: *"Elige tu secuencia con el rush de Martes en mente. Si lo ignoras, lo pagas."*

### What Ortega-Zavala knew

The bottleneck-minute output of 75 units per hour at Sewing, equal to 3,000 units across a 40-hour week after changeover deductions; the three changeover-cost magnitudes (45 minutes Work-to-School, 30 minutes School-to-Work, 5 minutes within family); the rush-order probability; and the on-time-delivery weight in the school-district contract renewal.

### What she did not know

Whether the Tuesday rush would materialise in the 40% expected or the 22% long-run-average or not at all; whether the shift supervisor could hold a Heijunka rhythm against operator preference for longer runs; whether the Finishing station could absorb the mid-week surge without bottlenecking on quality inspection; whether the school-uniform fabric roll scheduled to arrive Monday afternoon would arrive Monday or Tuesday; and whether the CAD-cutting file for the school-district pattern was release-ready or required one more revision cycle.

### What others were saying

**The Plant Director**: *"Decide la secuencia. Si cambias a mitad de semana, no has decidido."*

**The Shift Supervisor (Cutting)**: *"Los operadores trabajan mejor con lotes largos. La curva de aprendizaje dentro del lote se pierde con Heijunka."*

**The Shift Supervisor (Sewing)**: *"Mi estación es el cuello. Si el planner respeta mis minutos, el resto se acomoda."*

**The School-District Account Executive**: *"El rush de 120 es más probable que 40/60. Planea con eso en mente."*

**The Finance Manager**: *"Cada hora de cambio cuesta 3,200 pesos en utilidad perdida. Cuente los cambios."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Ortega-Zavala had narrowed the sequence space to three commitment postures.

- **Option A — ACB long-batch sequence (Recommended if rush probability ≤ 25%).** Start with A (500 work), transition to C (250 work, five-minute same-family changeover), transition to B (350 school, 45-minute Work-to-School changeover). One major transition across the week. Lowest total changeover cost. Tuesday rush absorbs poorly — the line is mid-Work when the school rush lands.
- **Option B — Heijunka levelled plan (Recommended if rush probability ≥ 35%).** Produce 100 work and 70 school every day, Monday through Friday, with one daily Work-to-School and one daily School-to-Work changeover. Total changeover cost higher than Option A. On-time-delivery on all three standing orders close to 100%. Tuesday rush absorbs inside the day's school block at marginal cost.
- **Option C — Rush-first pre-commit: run 120-unit school block Monday morning before A starts.** Rejected — pre-commits to a rush that is probabilistic, consumes two full changeovers before any standing order starts, and leaves the long-batch A at risk of slipping to Friday evening overtime.

Ortega-Zavala's private view was that Option B was the right commitment at the 40% rush probability the account executive had flagged and that the scheduling decision most correlated with the contract-renewal outcome was the Heijunka versus long-batch choice, because the on-time-delivery percentage on the school-district orders was the primary contract-renewal metric and the Tuesday rush was the single largest week-to-week variance driver. The *Línea en Marcha* primer sits at the undergraduate operations tier in the 2026 Express catalogue, distinct from the Heliconia Plant ERPcircuit (sim 248, full ERP-simulated multi-period plant) and the Moda Dinamica Predictive Analytics (sim 045, demand-forecasting-driven apparel ops). Primer 292 emphasises the bottleneck-minute reservation and the changeover-cost-versus-flexibility trade-off for the single-line operator; 248 emphasises the full ERP integration; 045 emphasises the forecasting layer.
