# Selat Analytics: Twenty-One Days to the Readout

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-2001 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2001 — *Smart, Sharp, Stuck* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Thursday, 12 March 2026, Nur Hafiza Rahim read two documents about the same engagement and could not make them agree. The first was a one-page model summary: the customer-churn model Selat Analytics had built for Bumi Telekom scored 0.84 AUC against a contracted target of 0.75. The second was the client steering committee's note rejecting the second readout in four weeks, in four words that had already travelled the whole firm — *technically impressive, practically useless*.

Hafiza had been Project Lead on the engagement for ninety minutes. The board readout was fixed for 9 April, twenty-one days out, and it would be the third attempt. RM 1.4 million (Malaysian ringgit) of fee sat behind it, and behind that the RM 3–4 million of follow-on work Bumi's group functions had been dangling since the contract was signed. She had those twenty-one days to work out what the pod was missing, change something, and get a readout past the committee.

## The Protagonist

Nur Hafiza Rahim, 36, had joined Selat Analytics as a data scientist and spent four years building the credit and attrition models that made the firm's name with Malaysian banks. She had moved onto the delivery side three years ago and run six engagements as a project manager without losing one. Her reputation inside the firm was for arithmetic — she was the person partners sent when a burn-down chart stopped making sense. She had never taken over a live engagement in week four, or from a colleague who had asked to be moved off it.

## The Decision

Hafiza had to say, first, what was actually stopping the pod — and then act on that answer for twenty-one days without changing it every time someone pushed back.

Selat's founder, Ravi Sundram, had built the firm's hiring around a distinction he repeated at every intake: **sharp** work — modelling, statistics, the analytical craft — and **smart** work — aligning a client, surfacing a disagreement, owning a decision, turning an analysis into something a board could act on. The job is easy, he said; the people are not. Selat recruited hard for sharp and assumed smart would follow.

The vocabulary gave Hafiza her first question, and the evidence pointed both ways. There was a real sharp gap: nobody had done the cost-benefit sizing a telco board would demand before approving anything. There was also a set of symptoms that had nothing to do with statistics. She would have to name one of them as the single most critical gap the readout would expose — the model's accuracy, the missing sizing, or the fact that no one had converted the model into decisions Bumi could take — and record on the engagement file, as a percentage, how confident she was. Selat's protocol required the number at the point of commitment, before the outcome was known.

## The Company and its Setting

Selat Analytics Sdn Bhd was a Kuala Lumpur data-and-strategy consultancy billing roughly RM 48 million a year in fees with 130 consultants, serving banks, telcos and consumer-goods firms across Southeast Asia. It sold project-based engagements combining data science with the operating-model change needed to act on it, delivered through small mixed pods rather than large staffed teams.

The firm's recurring post-mortem finding was not technical. Engagements rarely failed because the analysis was wrong; they failed because a pod with more than enough analytical horsepower could not align a client, disagree with one, or say who was accountable for the relationship.

Bumi Telekom, one of the country's largest mobile operators, was Selat's flagship account. The eight-week engagement was sold as a churn-prediction model *and* the operating changes required to act on its output. Bumi's sponsor, consumer-retention director Ridzuan Aziz, had changed what he wanted from the readout more than once, most recently the previous week.

## The Situation

Four weeks in, the engagement had consumed 62% of its budgeted hours and delivered, on the delivery office's assessment, about 40% of the client value contracted for. That gap ran straight into the economics (see **Exhibit 1**): at the current run-rate the job finished RM 180,000 over budget at a 9% margin, against the 35% the firm underwrote its work at.

Selat's delivery office kept two indices on every engagement. The first was a **skill-fit score** out of 100 — its judgement of whether the right skill family was working on whatever was actually blocking delivery, rather than on whatever the team found most comfortable. Bumi stood at 38. The second was a **team-cohesion index**, also out of 100, built from the office's own check-ins. Bumi stood at 54, and two consultants had told the firm's talent partner, separately and in confidence, that the pod was dysfunctional.

The detail behind those two numbers was in the week-four staffing record (see **Exhibit 2**). Tan Wei Jian, the Data & Analytics Lead, and Amirul Hashim, the pod's other senior data scientist, had spent the fortnight since the second rejection re-running model variants no one had asked for. Wei Jian was the engagement's strongest performer and, the talent partner said, close to leaving. Priya Muthusamy, the Client & Stakeholder Lead, and Daniel Lim, the other client-facing consultant, had absorbed each of Ridzuan's changes of direction, rebuilt the deck, and pushed back on none of them. No name stood against the Bumi relationship; the engagement plan said the pod owned it jointly.

If Hafiza changed nothing, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The model would improve slightly, the burn would keep climbing, the sizing would still be missing on 9 April, and the committee would see a third version of a readout it had rejected twice — with the flagship account and the follow-on pipeline attached.

## What Hafiza Controlled

She controlled the roles, the work, and how the last three weeks were spent.

She could put a single name against the Bumi relationship — Priya's, as the Client & Stakeholder Lead — or Wei Jian's on the argument that the firm's strongest performer should front its most important account, or leave it with the pod as a whole. She could let Wei Jian and Amirul keep tuning a model that was already past its target, send them on a course, or freeze the model and move them onto the cost-benefit sizing. She could run the next three weeks as a three-day offsite, a booked series of stakeholder-management seminars, no intervention at all, or make the readout preparation itself the thing the pod learned on — and whichever she chose, she had to say how much of twenty-one days it would take.

She also had to split the pod's effort between the analytical work and the human work, as a percentage, and defend the split. On Ridzuan's shifting requirements she could leave Priya and Daniel to work it out, coach them through a prepared pushback conversation they would own, or go over their heads herself. She could authorise weekend work and longer hours, add hours only where the human work needed them, or redeploy the effort she already had.

The readout itself was three decisions. If Ridzuan asked for one more model variant in the final week, she could produce it, quietly not produce it, or reopen the conversation with him. She could take the committee fourteen slides of model performance, lead with methodology and add recommendations at the end, or build the spine out of what Bumi should do on Monday and what each move was worth. She could pitch it on analytical depth, on an apology for two rejected readouts, or as three churn-reduction moves with the decision she needed that day. At close-out the protocol asked for one more number: how firmly she held that what unstuck the pod was the reallocation of skill rather than more analysis.

## Closing

At 9:20 that morning Hafiza wrote two headings on the whiteboard in the Bumi war room — SHARP and SMART — and put the six things that had gone wrong in four weeks underneath one or the other. Three of them she could not place. Wei Jian's laptop was open on the far side of the room, running another variant. The readout was in twenty-one days.
