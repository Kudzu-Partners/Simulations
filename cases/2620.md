# Jalisa Merriweather: Salon Owner at Velvet Fern Salon Suites

**Case code:** ENT-2026-2620 · **Associated simulation:** Express 2620 — *Salon Owner* · **v1.0 · October 2026**
**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, 4 January 2027, Jalisa Merriweather stood in the empty fourth chair of Velvet Fern Salon Suites, a four-chair salon suite in Atlanta's West End, with three text messages open on her phone. Three licensed stylists wanted the three chairs she did not work herself, and each of them wanted a different deal. The salon would open on Saturday, 16 January (see Exhibit 1).

Jalisa Merriweather had to decide that same morning, before she answered the three stylists, what kind of business the other three chairs would be and how she would price her menu. Three more decision points would follow before her first year closed (see Exhibit 2).

## The Protagonist

Jalisa Merriweather, 20, had finished cosmetology school, passed the state board exam and spent two years behind someone else's chair before she signed the lease on the suite in December 2026. She had registered the salon with the state board before opening. "Doing hair pays your rent," her former instructor, Delphine Strother, had told her. "Running a salon is a different job. Learn both."

## The Decision

Jalisa would judge her salon's typical week on five things: how many completed appointments the four chairs delivered, out of about 120 they could take; how few hours she worked beyond her own 50-hour week; how small a share of booked appointments ended as no-shows or late cancellations; the weekly profit the salon left her before taxes; and the loyalty of her clients — how many came back and sent their friends.

## The Company and its Setting

Velvet Fern Salon Suites was hers alone: a lease, four chairs, four mirrors, a shampoo bowl and a small front counter. She worked the first chair. Tavia Okonkwo-Bell, who did braids and natural hair, wanted a lease at a weekly rent and her own clients. Bastien Ruddock, who did cuts and color, would rather be paid a commission with a steady schedule. Mireille Duchamp-Hayes, who did silk presses and styling, asked whether she could be "a renter, but you handle the products and the booking." A friend from school had told Jalisa to "just call them renters, take a cut and skip the payroll — everybody does."

## The Situation

The menu on the front counter was still blank. Delphine had sent her a one-line message: "Decide what you are before you decide what you charge."

If nothing changed, the year would run the way most new owners ran their first one: handshake deals with friends for the other chairs, no price list, booking by direct message with no policy and no reminders, twelve-hour Saturdays alone, a quick spray and wipe between clients, every no-show forgiven, personal and business money in one account, and the second year just like the first.

## What Merriweather Controlled

On 4 January Merriweather would choose what the other three chairs would be: commission employees on payroll, at a 45% commission with taxes withheld, a set schedule and her own training; handshake deals with three friends, "pay when you can", what new owners usually do; booth rent, with a written lease for each chair at $275 a week, each renter keeping her own clients, prices, hours, products and license and handling her own taxes; or "renters" on paper whose hours and prices she set, whose color line she supplied and who paid her 40% of every ticket, with no payroll. And how to price the menu: no price list, quoting each client in the chair, what new owners usually do; a price for every service built from its cost — product, time and a share of the suite's rent — checked against salons nearby and posted at the door and online; the prices of a luxury salon from across town; or undercut every salon nearby by 30%.

On Monday 5 April, three months in, Saturdays were booked solid and the waiting list was long, while on weekdays about one appointment in five ended as a no-show. Merriweather would choose how to book: by direct message and text, with no policy and no reminders, what new owners usually do; walk-ins only, with no appointments at all; online booking with a card on file, a 24-hour cancellation policy stated at booking and reminder texts 48 and 24 hours before; or double-book the busiest slots to cover the no-shows. And who would work the Saturday rush, since her cousin Shontae, 22, "great with color" but unlicensed, had offered to do color for cash, Philippa Trevathan, a licensed stylist, would rent the empty chair by the day, and Marcus Ebo, 18, wanted a few hours shampooing: work twelve-hour Saturdays alone, what new owners usually do; Philippa in the empty chair, her license checked on the state's online lookup, with Marcus paid by the hour to shampoo and blow-dry only; turn Saturday clients away; or let Shontae do color on Saturdays.

On Tuesday 6 July, the week after the holiday weekend, five clients missed their appointments in two days. A client had posted a photo of a comb taken from one station and used on the next client, and an inspector of the state board walked in unannounced. Merriweather would choose how tools were disinfected: a quick spray and wipe with a household cleaner between clients, what new owners usually do; close one day a week for a deep clean; clean every multi-use tool and fully immerse it for at least 10 minutes in a registered, hospital-grade disinfectant after every client, change the solution daily, keep clean and used tools in covered, labeled containers and keep a daily checklist; or disinfect everything once, at closing. And how to handle the no-shows: waive the policy for everyone, since "they'll come back", what new owners usually do; apply the stated policy to everyone the same way, kindly, and offer the empty slot to the waiting list; ban anyone who missed once and post about them online; or squeeze late clients in anyway.

On Monday 4 October, the September estimated-tax payment had come and gone. The landlord offered an eight-chair salon two doors down, a product rep offered a $3,000 retail order, and Mrs. Ottilie Lumpkin, a regular since January, told Merriweather the prices were fair and the chair was never late. Merriweather would choose how to manage the money: personal and business money in one account, with the receipts in a shoebox, what new owners usually do; hand everything to an accountant each month without keeping her own books; a separate business account, a set share of every week's profit put aside for taxes, quarterly estimated payments and the books kept every week; or spend the money now and pay whatever was owed in April. And how to start year two: change nothing, what new owners usually do; place the $3,000 retail order; raise prices 8% for new clients only and rebook every client before she left the chair; or sign the lease on the eight-chair salon.

At each decision point the salon's typical week would be projected again with the decisions taken so far, and Merriweather would see the completed appointments, the booked ones that actually happened; her owner hours over a 50-hour week; the no-show rate, the share of booked appointments missed or cancelled too late to refill; the weekly profit, before her taxes; the client loyalty, on a scale from 0 to 100; and the open violations, any worker treated as a renter while run as an employee or any licensed service done by someone without a license. She would receive a round score out of 100 at each decision point.

## Closing

Tavia had already sent a photo of her braiding chair, ready to move in. Bastien was waiting for an answer about the schedule, and Mireille had added a second message: "So who orders the color?" Jalisa set the phone face down on the counter, picked up a pen and wrote two headings on the blank menu.
