# Helbion Systems GmbH: The Trust-Control Dial

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1777 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1777 — *Alliance Lab: Trust, Control & Co-Development* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 10 June 2026, Katja Brenner carried two numbers into Helbion Systems GmbH's Munich boardroom: a fixed EUR 12,000,000 innovation budget the board had just approved, and fourteen days before a competitor could lock up the one partner that budget was meant to buy. Helbion's precision sensors were as good as any in industrial automation; its embedded-AI software was not, and a fast-moving Toulouse firm called Cortexa AI SAS could close that gap in eighteen months — if Helbion could agree with it, quickly, on how much to trust the partner and how much to control it.

Brenner, the R&D manager the board had just named alliance lead, had two weeks to set that posture, decide how much of Helbion's core sensing technology to open to Cortexa, and structure the terms around it — before Sentek, a direct rival already known to be courting Cortexa, made the decision for her.

## The Protagonist

Brenner, 41, had run Helbion's advanced-sensing engineering group for six years after joining from a mechatronics doctorate and eight years at a Stuttgart automotive supplier. She had never negotiated an alliance; Helbion had not attempted one since a joint project a decade earlier ended with a core design leaking to a competitor, an episode that predated her but still shaped every conversation she had with the board about outside partners. She had been given the mandate partly because her engineering judgment was trusted and partly because she carried none of the old scar tissue personally — the board wanted the decision made on the alliance's merits, not refought as an old argument.

## The Decision

Brenner's task was not to make the alliance safe in the sense of minimizing every risk. It was to find the configuration of trust and control that would let Helbion and Cortexa actually build something together, given the real risks on both sides — and to do it inside a budget where every euro spent on one thing was a euro not spent on another.

## The Company and its Setting

Helbion Systems GmbH, founded in Munich in 2004, employed 1,150 people and had generated EUR 380,000,000 in revenue in 2025, with 12% of that — EUR 45,600,000 — spent on R&D (see Exhibit 1). The firm made precision sensing modules for factory automation and held a strong position in the hardware; its embedded-AI capability lagged the market. Cortexa AI SAS, 140 people and based in Toulouse, held exactly the edge-AI expertise Helbion lacked. Helbion's board had approved an eighteen-month co-development alliance with Cortexa and set a fixed EUR 12,000,000 budget for it — and had told Brenner directly that Sentek, a direct industrial rival, was rumored to be courting Cortexa as well.

## The Situation

At nine that morning, the alliance did not yet exist in anything but the board's approval. If Brenner did nothing — no clear posture, no term sheet, two weeks spent in internal debate — the outcome was not neutral: Cortexa's attention, and its two-week window, would keep moving, and Sentek's rumored approach would keep looking more attractive to a firm choosing between two suitors. Helbion's board had put a number on the cost of that outcome: EUR 30,000,000 in addressable revenue over three years if the smart-sensing window closed without a partner.

Brenner's dashboard for the alliance showed four figures, all at their opening values (see Exhibit 2). Joint creativity output — what the alliance existed to produce — read 30 out of 100. Trust with Cortexa read 40 out of 100. IP risk, the exposure of Helbion's core sensing designs, read 25 out of 100, where lower was better. Alliance ROI, the innovation value the partnership would generate against the money spent, read 0.0x. Of the EUR 12,000,000 budget, none had yet been committed to either engineering or to the audits, escrow and monitoring that would police the relationship — the whole amount was still available, and every euro that went to policing the partner was a euro that would not go to building anything with them.

## What Brenner Controlled

Brenner's most direct instrument was a single dial, running from zero — full control: exhaustive contracts, gated information, sign-off at every step — to a hundred — full trust: shared roadmaps, open data, joint engineering teams. Alongside it she had to submit her own read of mutual dependence, also on a 0–100 scale: an honest estimate of how much Helbion actually needed Cortexa to compete in embedded AI, since that diagnosis was meant to anchor wherever she set the dial.

Beyond the posture sat the architecture. The IP-sharing tier ran in four steps: share interface specifications only, walling off every sensing model; share those interfaces plus a handful of selected sub-models; share most of the sensing models while keeping the core architecture back; or open the core sensing models to Cortexa outright, holding nothing in reserve. Separately, she could commit anywhere from zero to EUR 6,000,000 of the fixed budget to control machinery — audits, escrow rights, ongoing monitoring — money that came directly out of what was left for joint engineering. And independently of both, the term sheet's IP bounding could be left open, with no field-of-use limits and no split between background and foreground IP; tightened to loose field-of-use boundaries, though foreground ownership stayed ambiguous; narrowed further with clearly defined field-of-use limits written into the agreement; built out with a formal background/foreground split and clear joint-ownership rules; or completed with a sign-off rule requiring both firms' consent before filing a joint patent.

Because Helbion's one prior partnership had ended in exactly this kind of dispute, her mandate also asked her to decide, in advance, how the alliance would answer if disagreement ever flared: demand immediate correction and threaten to walk away entirely; formally re-impose controls, issue a warning, and tighten oversight; pause and establish what had actually happened before answering; treat it as a governance gap and fix the term together; or let it pass entirely and keep the relationship calm. And over the life of the alliance she would have to decide how to carry it forward: abandon the alliance altogether and rebuild the capability inside Helbion; patch only the disputed IP term and otherwise continue as before; patch the term and add staged milestones on top of it; add joint creativity targets and co-located teams to that; add early-warning metrics and a standing review cadence on top; or blend all of it into one lasting settlement of contract and relationship.

Brenner would be judged on the same four gauges every round: how much joint creativity the alliance actually produced, how much trust it held with Cortexa, how contained the IP risk stayed, and what the alliance returned against the EUR 12,000,000 spent.

## Closing

Brenner set her laptop down at 9:04 a.m., the trust-control dial still at its default midpoint, the four gauges reading 30, 40, 25 and 0.0x. Fourteen days were already counting down, and the board was waiting for a posture, not a study.
