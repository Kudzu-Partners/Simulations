# Atelier Lumière: Reinventing the Model Before the Cash Runs Out

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1813 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1813 — *7 Steps to a Business Model* · **v2.0 · September 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Thursday, June 11, 2026, Élise Vasseur read the letter from Atelier Lumière's lead bank twice before calling anyone. The bank cited three consecutive years of declining print revenue and was cutting the company's overdraft facility from €600,000 to €200,000, effective immediately. By the time Vasseur reached the boardroom that evening, the board had turned the bank's letter into an ultimatum of its own: bring a credible new business model to the board meeting on Thursday, July 9, or prepare the company for sale.

## The Protagonist

Élise Vasseur, 54, had founded Atelier Lumière in 2009 after eleven years managing production at a larger Lille print group, betting that a boutique house serving regional retailers, restaurants and cultural venues directly could compete with bigger houses on turnaround and service. She had built much of the roughly 600-account client roster herself over the company's first decade. She held the majority of the equity and had reinvested profit rather than draw it out for most of the company's history; the €1.8 million of net cash the bank and board were now scrutinizing was cash she had chosen, year after year, to leave in the business.

## The Decision

Vasseur had four weeks to design a complete new business model and bring it to the board and the firm's lead outside investor — solid enough, and affordable within the cash she actually had, to win approval rather than a vote to sell. The work would unfold in stages: establish why the print business was failing and what she would pay to learn what she did not know; commit to a new value proposition; choose how it would be monetized and when the money would arrive; decide what had to change operationally to support it; and only then bring the finished plan back for a verdict.

## The Company and its Setting

Atelier Lumière SAS had made its name in commercial print — brochures, catalogues, point-of-sale displays — for regional businesses. At its 2017 peak it employed 70 people and turned over €9.8 million. By 2025, as clients shifted budgets from print to digital channels, revenue had fallen to €5.4 million, gross margin on the print work had compressed from 34% to 22%, and the operating margin had thinned to 4% (see Exhibit 1). The company still employed 38 people and ran the printing plant that had always sat at the center of the business, at a fixed cost of roughly €95,000 a month — a bill that would arrive every month the question of what to do with it stayed open. It also still had, largely untapped, eight years of design files, brand assets and content built for its roughly 600 regional clients.

## The Situation

Print revenue was falling at roughly 12% a year, a trend Vasseur's team had watched for three years running. If nothing changed, that trajectory alone would exhaust the company's €1.8 million of net cash in about fourteen months — almost exactly the runway she now had left. A tracker her team had begun keeping on the reinvention put two further numbers in front of her that evening: a "model coherence" score of 20 out of 100, meant to capture how well a future value proposition, revenue model and operations would fit together, and an "investor confidence" score of 15 out of 100 — the board and the lead investor needed to see that figure reach at least 70 before approving any new model at the July meeting (see Exhibit 2). Both numbers described intentions, not decisions yet made. Two more facts mattered. First, the lead investor had already said, before the bank's letter arrived, that he would not approve a plan that bolted a new digital line onto print operations left otherwise unchanged. Second, the two senior designers best placed to build any new offering had been approached by a design agency in Paris.

## What Vasseur Controlled

The first decisions were diagnostic. Vasseur could tell the board that the model itself was failing — a commoditized proposition, an eroding margin and a fixed cost base, three faults with one cause; that the mix was the problem and the company should retreat to the complex work it still won; that demand had moved to channels Atelier Lumière did not serve; or that price and cost had drifted apart. She had to name the asset the reinvention would be built on — the design files and client content, the plant and the people who knew how to run it, or the name the company had earned since 2009. And she had to decide that week whether to commission research into how many of the 600 would really convert: a full segmentation for €60,000, a fast sample of sixty for €20,000, or nothing. The number would not be needed for another month, and she would be buying it before she knew which model she was buying it for.

Then the value proposition — the commitment everything after it would be measured against. Six were on the table. She could run the digital presence — website, social, catalogue — for SMEs who could not run it themselves; sell production capacity to regional agencies rather than to their clients; build a self-serve toolkit the SME used without her; put the 600 on one storefront and take a margin on what moved; narrow into the regulated, luxury and short-run print the commodity houses could not take; or license the archive back to the clients who had commissioned it. Tied to that was how much of today's business to keep serving while she built: one segment and a public exit from everything else, the adjacent work that funded the build, or every order that came.

Third, monetization. The offering could be billed as a monthly subscription, an annual retainer invoiced quarterly in advance, a take-rate on each transaction, a free tier converting to paid plans, or project fees quoted per job the way Atelier Lumière had always billed. Separately she had to say when the money arrived — slowly and compounding over eighteen months, blended so that a paid pilot cohort funded part of the build, or largely inside the first year as quoted work was delivered and invoiced. Then two numbers: how much of the runway to commit up front, from nothing to a €700,000 ceiling, and what share of the 600 her revenue case assumed, from 5% to 90%.

Fourth, operations, with the plant billing €95,000 for every month the question stayed open. She could reconfigure it to the capacity the new model used; keep it running in full; take €150,000 off the building in a sale-and-leaseback and rent back what she needed; or pay €180,000 to close it. The thirty-eight people could be retrained into recurring delivery, joined by product and engineering hires, shrunk to fewer and more senior heads, or left as they were until the new model said what work there would be. The two designers with the Paris offer could be matched — equity, a real role and a retention package, €120,000 out of the same runway — or left unspoken to, or released in favor of juniors hired later.

Finally, the pitch. Vasseur could put it to the board as one model, showing how the proposition, the revenue logic and the operation held each other up; as a trade-off, naming what she was giving up and why it was worth it; as a bet, leading with the size of the prize; or as an addition, a new digital line beside a print business she was not proposing to touch. She had to name the single risk she was asking them to carry — conversion of the 600, delivery capacity she had not yet staffed, adoption of something clients would have to use without her, the cash itself, or none material — commit to an operating margin for the end of year two, and decide how firmly to hold her framing if the board pushed back.

## Closing

Vasseur folded the bank's letter, opened a blank page, and wrote the date at the top: four weeks to July 9. Model coherence stood at 20. Investor confidence stood at 15. Somewhere inside €1.8 million of cash and a €700,000 ceiling on new spending were several versions of Atelier Lumière, each of which a reasonable person could argue for, and only one of which she could build. She had not yet decided what was broken.
