# Closing the Strategy Gap — Execute Lumière 2030

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1845 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1845 — *Closing the Strategy Gap* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 11 May 2026, Élodie Faure listened to a non-executive director ask the board of Lumière Cosmétique S.A. a question nobody in the room wanted to answer. Six months earlier, the board had approved "Lumière 2030," a sustainability strategy built around refillable packaging, certified sourcing, a 50% cut to Scope 1 and 2 emissions, and a shift of one-third of revenue to a certified "clean" product line. The deck had won a sustainability award. Now the director wanted to know what had actually changed. The audit in front of the board answered for her: of fourteen flagship initiatives, eleven had not started, two were stuck in pilot, and one — a marketing relaunch — had gone ahead of the supply chain meant to support it.

Faure, appointed Strategy & Transformation Director three months earlier specifically to close this gap, left the meeting with a fixed change budget of EUR 30 million and eighteen months to diagnose why the strategy had stalled, decide which of the fourteen initiatives to fund, remove whatever was blocking delivery, and defend a credible plan to the same director — before the strategy's 2027 milestones came due.

## The Protagonist

Faure had spent the previous decade in operations and transformation roles at two other consumer-goods manufacturers before Lumière recruited her for exactly this mandate. She reported to the CEO, who had personally sponsored Lumière 2030 and whose credibility was now tied to it, and she worked alongside an Operations Director, a Commercial & Marketing Director, a Head of People & Change, and a CFO delegate who controlled the investment purse strings. None of the fourteen initiatives had a single accountable owner; some in the company already said the strategy was "everyone's job," which in practice meant it belonged to no one.

## The Decision

Faure's task was not to rewrite Lumière 2030. It was to diagnose why a strategy that had been formulated carefully and communicated across the company had produced almost no delivery, decide which of the fourteen initiatives to fund and in what order inside a budget that could not be increased, remove whatever was blocking the people who had to do the work, and then defend a credible plan to the same director who had asked the question that started the review — all before the strategy's 2027 milestones came due.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumière Cosmétique S.A., headquartered in Lyon, made skincare and haircare products sold through retail, pharmacy, and e-commerce channels across Europe. It generated EUR 720 million in annual revenue at a 12% operating margin and employed 2,100 people. It sat in an uncomfortable middle: too large to be an artisanal natural-products brand, far smaller than the global beauty conglomerates it competed against on packaging and sourcing claims.

The board's patience was not open-ended. A sustainability-linked covenant on the company's financing would raise its interest cost by 75 basis points — roughly EUR 2.1 million a year — if Lumière missed its 2027 milestones. Two retail customers together holding 22% of the company's revenue had set their own 2027 supplier-sustainability requirements and could stop carrying Lumière's products if those requirements went unmet. An internal staff survey, taken before Faure arrived, found that 38% of employees already called the strategy "greenwash slides" (see **Exhibit 1**).

## The Situation

The gap between the deck and reality was not a mystery to anyone who looked closely. Plant and commercial managers were bonused entirely on cost and volume; sustainability carried no weight in what they were measured or paid on. No one owned any of the fourteen initiatives individually. The company had no eco-design engineers and no function dedicated to supplier sustainability, and of the 38 packaging suppliers Lumière worked with, only two could currently deliver the mono-material packaging the strategy called for. And the fourteen initiatives were all competing at once for the same scarce capital, factory time, and management attention.

Faure inherited four numbers to move (see **Exhibit 2**). Execution — the share of initiatives actually delivering, not merely announced — stood at 5%, against a target of at least 70%. Stakeholder buy-in, a 0–100 measure of how much the people affected by the change believed in it, stood at 25, weighed down by that 38% "greenwash" figure. Sustainability KPIs Met, also scored 0–100, tracked progress toward the 2027 loan and customer milestones and stood at zero. Change-cost efficiency — how much execution the company got for every euro of the EUR 30 million it committed — could not even be calculated yet, because none of the budget had been spent. If nothing changed, the eighteen-month review would look like the six-month one: a stalled strategy, a loan penalty triggering, and two large customers reconsidering the relationship.

## What Faure Controlled

Before committing a euro, Faure had to name what had actually gone wrong: an implementation gap — a strategy sound on paper never translated into delivery; a formulation gap needing a sharper, better-sequenced strategy; over-ambition, with targets to quietly scale back; or resistant managers who simply did not want to change. She then had to locate the barrier worth unblocking first — misaligned incentives bonused entirely on cost and volume, the fragmentation of fourteen simultaneous initiatives, weak communication calling for a louder relaunch of the strategy, or the narrow readiness of the supplier base — and state, on a 0-to-100 scale, how sharply she separated structural barriers (incentives, capability) from sequencing problems (too many initiatives at once).

Diagnosis settled, she had to decide how much of Lumière 2030 survived a fixed budget: four or five initiatives, properly funded and owned; a compromise portfolio of around eight; or the full fourteen, at roughly EUR 2 million apiece. Whatever she kept, the EUR 30 million had to be split three ways — foundations (eco-design capability, supplier development, incentive redesign), the board-pleasing flagship refill range, and a sequenced follow-on and reserve for the clean line and later-quarter contingencies — and sequenced: foundations before any visible launch, foundations and the flagship in parallel, or the flagship first, which meant putting EUR 9 million into the refill range in the opening quarter for the sake of board optics.

Removing the barriers meant three more calls. She had to set, as a percentage, how much weight sustainability carried in plant and commercial managers' bonuses, against an Operations Director already resisting any change to the incentive structure his factories ran on. She had to choose between a named, accountable owner per initiative, a cross-functional steering committee holding the whole portfolio, or CEO sponsorship alone. And with zero eco-design engineers and no supplier-sustainability function, she had to decide whether to partner with a packaging specialist while hiring in-house over time, hire engineers from scratch, retrain existing staff alongside their day jobs, or defer the capability build and assign the targets anyway — then whether to engage the 38 suppliers and two demanding retail customers as co-creators of the new model, develop the two already-ready suppliers while supporting the rest toward readiness, or send a compliance letter promising to "try to meet" the 2027 requirements.

Finally, she had to decide how the eighteen-month plan would be tracked and defended: quarterly checkpoints against the four KPIs, an annual review timed to 2027, or no formal checkpoints beyond a promise to "drive real execution this time"; whether to commit the board to landing both the loan and customer milestones, prioritise the loan milestone and ask the retailers for more time, or signal that both 2027 dates might slip; and, when the director probed the plan's most fragile dependency, whether to name it honestly with its owner and fallback, reassure the board without naming it, or point to the flagship launch as proof things had changed. A final figure, 0 to 100, recorded how firmly she stood behind the claim that removed barriers — not a sharper deck — had closed the gap.

## Closing

Faure closed the audit folder at her desk in Lyon. Eleven initiatives sat untouched, two were stuck, and one had already gotten ahead of a supply chain that could not support it. She had EUR 30 million, eighteen months, and a board that had just watched a deck win an award and change nothing. The director's question was still open: what, this time, would actually change?
