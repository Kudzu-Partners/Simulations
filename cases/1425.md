# Twenty-One Days to Answer

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-1425 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1425 — *Pre-Trial Strategy — Plumeline.io vs Mantorra Capital* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 06:48 on Tuesday, June 16, 2026, Tobias Wexler-Marín, general counsel of Plumeline.io, opened an email his chief executive had sent at 23:14 the night before. It ran to four sentences: settle for USD 3.5 million; the company could not afford the distraction; the Series D process opened in eleven weeks; make this go away by Friday. The complaint behind it had been filed the previous Friday at 16:42 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. It sought USD 38 million plus interest and fees.

Wexler-Marín had booked a partner meeting for 09:00. By its end he had to set the company's pre-trial posture and be ready to defend it to the chief executive and the Audit Committee.

## The Protagonist

Wexler-Marín was forty-nine. He had spent eleven years at the law firm Halvorsen Drake, making partner in 2013, before joining Plumeline.io in late 2019 as its first general counsel. He described himself as an operator with a law degree rather than a lawyer with a chief executive. He ran a department of three and used his former firm sparingly, aware of how easily an in-house lawyer leaned on the partners he had grown up with. He answered executives' emails with numbered questions before committing to anything, and his team could quote his rule back to him: he did not price a case before he had read it.

## The Decision

Wexler-Marín had to commit to eight calls in three rounds: how to open the meeting and defend each of the three counts; which overall posture to take and how much discovery to buy; and how to carry the result to a chief executive who had already named his number and to an Audit Committee that would ask about reserves.

## The Company and its Setting

Plumeline.io, founded in 2018 with offices in San Francisco and Bogotá, sold collaborative document-editing software to enterprises. Every open legal matter would become a Series D diligence question once the data room opened.

Mantorra Capital Partners, L.P., an investment firm, had been a customer since a February 2024 master services agreement governed by California law. The agreement capped direct damages at twelve months of fees, roughly USD 4.8 million, and excluded consequential damages. A November 2024 side letter extended "audit deliverables generally" by up to forty-five days for "good-faith auditor delays." Whether that reached the SOC 2 Type II report, an auditor's attestation of a vendor's security controls over a period, or only the earlier Type I and component reports, the documents did not say.

## The Situation

The complaint ran to forty-one paragraphs and three counts (see Exhibit 1). Count One alleged breach of contract: the agreement required a Type II report by March 31, 2026, and the company delivered it in mid-April. Mantorra said the delay cost it a USD 21 million commitment from a public pension fund whose rules required a Type II from vendors of its size. The fund's rejection letter, listing its reasons, was in Mantorra's hands.

Count Two alleged negligent misrepresentation under Section 552 of the Restatement (Second) of Torts. On a recorded procurement call on December 14, 2025, Mateo Salazar, who ran strategic accounts, had told Mantorra's chief technology officer that the SOC 2 work was "complete and audited." The auditor's evidence-gathering ended on December 19, and the Type I report issued on January 11, 2026. Mantorra's chief technology officer had the Type I documentation and the auditor's email thread on the Type II evidence window.

Count Three alleged tortious interference with prospective economic advantage. At a vendor dinner in February 2026, a customer-success employee had allegedly described Mantorra to two of its other prospective investors as "a difficult customer with unrealistic compliance demands." California law (the *Korea Supply* decision of 2003) required an independently wrongful act for such a claim, and federal pleading (the *Twombly* and *Iqbal* decisions) required facts that made a claim plausible, not merely conceivable.

Several clocks ran at once. The company had twenty-one days from service, until July 3, to answer or move to dismiss. One trade publication had already asked for comment.

Hannah Reisinger-Voss, the Halvorsen Drake litigation partner who had taken six cases to verdict in a decade, had sent preliminary figures for five paths (see Exhibit 2). She put Mantorra's likely floor for a sealed settlement at USD 4 million to USD 7 million. The chief executive's arithmetic was simpler: USD 3.5 million was less than her estimate of trial fees alone, and it would close before the data room opened. Two documents, she said, would move every estimate: the rejection letter and any 2024 emails showing what the parties had meant by the side letter. A targeted request for both would cost about USD 80,000.

If nothing changed, the matter would drift toward trial. The running estimate of expected total cost that her team kept, settlement plus fees plus a discount for reputational damage, stood at USD 12.0 million, the headline verdict figure in her trial estimate. Wexler-Marín tracked three further readings on a scale of 0 to 100 alongside it: the doctrinal soundness of the company's position, how well the matter would read in Series D diligence, and the confidence of his principals, the chief executive and the Audit Committee. On Tuesday morning all three stood at 50.

## What Wexler-Marín Controlled

The first round was diagnosis. He could open the meeting by choosing to accept the chief executive's directive and settle at USD 3.5 million that week; build a count-by-count case-theory matrix before any number was discussed; instruct Reisinger-Voss to draft a motion to dismiss all three counts at once; or authorise a full discovery plan before any motion or settlement. On Count One, he could concede liability and negotiate the amount; lead with the damages cap and the line between direct and consequential loss; argue that the side letter covered the Type II; or combine the side letter, the cap and an attack on causation built on the rejection letter. On Count Two, he could deny that Salazar's statement was false when made; attack whether Mantorra could justifiably have relied on it; dispute both; or concede the claim and contest only the measure of loss. On Count Three, he could file a narrow motion to dismiss that count alone; take discovery on the dinner first; concede it and offer a corrective statement to Mantorra's investor base; or fold it into a motion against all three counts.

The second round was design. For the overall posture he could settle now at USD 3.5 million; take limited fact discovery and settle once the strength of Count One was known; move against Count Three only and hold the rest; push for summary judgment on all three counts; prepare for trial; or combine a motion on Count Three with targeted discovery and a sealed settlement of Counts One and Two. For discovery he could subpoena the rejection letter and the side-letter correspondence only; depose every witness and request every custodian's files; take no discovery and settle to avoid the spend; or depose Salazar and Mantorra's chief technology officer without seeking documents.

The third round was defence. With the chief executive, he could call it a legal matter and ask for trust; walk him through the pleading standard and the decision arithmetic line by line; reframe around Mantorra's likely settlement floor and the story the matter would tell in diligence; or agree to settle at USD 3.5 million as asked. For the Audit Committee, chaired by a former chief financial officer, and against a reasonably possible exposure counsel put at USD 2 million to USD 6 million, he could disclose the full USD 38 million demand as a material contingency; reserve USD 2.5 million, treating disclosure as permitted but not required; reserve nothing, on the view that the case was defensible; or reserve USD 6 million with full footnote disclosure.

After each call the four readings moved.

## Closing

At 08:57 Priya Krishnamurthy, the chief revenue officer who owned the Mantorra relationship, joined on video from home. Reisinger-Voss was already on the line. The chief executive's email was still open on the second screen, its number in the first sentence. Whatever Wexler-Marín said first would set the frame for the rest of the morning.
