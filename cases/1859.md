# Atelier Cadence: Designing With Veridian

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1859 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1859 — *Co-Creating Change* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 11 May 2026, Isabelle Chastain read the covering note that had arrived with Veridian Mobilité's request the previous evening and understood that it was not, in fact, a request for training. Veridian wanted three hundred of its managers trained on its new services model. What eighteen months of stalled internal change told her was that training alone would not move them.

She had four weeks to design and sign a proposal, sixteen weeks after that to launch it, and nine months to complete the first cohort — before Veridian's FY27 budget meeting decided whether the pivot the programme was meant to support got funded or shelved. A cheaper off-the-shelf consultancy academy was one signature away from taking the mandate instead, if Atelier Cadence did not sign first. As Executive Education Director of Atelier Cadence, Chastain would decide, round by round, what the programme was really for, who would build it with her, and what would count as proof it had worked.

## The Protagonist

Chastain, 44, had led Atelier Cadence's executive-education unit for five years, after eleven running its custom-programme delivery. She had joined the school from industry, believing a programme designed with a client outlasted one designed for it. Two years earlier, on her watch, the unit had lost a EUR 1.6 million leadership academy with a logistics group for exactly that reason: a design built entirely in-house, delivered to acclaim but never renewed, because nothing about the client's managers had changed. She had rebuilt the unit's method around one conviction — that a client's own people belonged in the design room — and had not yet proved it at this scale.

## The Decision

Each round, Chastain committed a set of choices that together defined what Atelier Cadence was selling Veridian and what would count as evidence it had worked.

## The Company and its Setting

Atelier Cadence was the custom executive-education unit of a mid-sized business school in La Rochelle, generating roughly EUR 11.4 million a year across 18 full-time programme staff and 140 affiliated faculty and coaches. Its custom programmes cleared a 28% average contribution margin, against a 22% internal hurdle for budget approval.

Veridian Mobilité was a EUR 2.3 billion French mobility-and-charging-infrastructure group pivoting from hardware to subscription services. Eighteen months of internal change effort had stalled: middle managers still protected the legacy hardware P&L that had built their careers, and resisted the shift. Veridian's invitation was to co-design a transformation programme for its top three hundred managers, spread across six countries, four languages and three seniority bands.

## The Situation

The terms were unforgiving. The programme budget was capped at EUR 840,000, ring-fenced by Veridian's CFO, who would claw back any overrun rather than approve it after the fact. The programme had to launch within sixteen weeks and complete its first cohort within nine months. A parallel bid from a global consultancy, priced roughly EUR 200,000 lower, would win the mandate by default if Atelier Cadence did not get a signature within four weeks. And the three executives sponsoring the decision wanted different things: the CHRO wanted behaviour change, the COO wanted a measurable services-revenue lift, and the CFO wanted completion and cost under control (Exhibit 2).

Four figures described where the programme stood at the outset. Transformation Impact — the behaviour and business-outcome change the CHRO and COO were buying — stood at 12 out of 100. Sponsor Alignment — how far the three executives backed one shared scorecard — stood at 20 out of 100. The Co-Creation Index — how much of the design and delivery belonged to Veridian's own people rather than Atelier Cadence's — stood at 10 out of 100.

Contribution margin stood at 28%, above the 22% hurdle, with the full EUR 840,000 still uncommitted (Exhibit 1). If Chastain changed nothing — accepted the brief exactly as written, built the programme in-house, and let the steering committee's disagreement run unresolved — the mandate would default to the cheaper bid, or launch on schedule with nothing behind it to show Veridian's FY27 budget meeting.

## What Chastain Controlled

In the first round, Chastain had to name, in one page, what Veridian was actually paying Atelier Cadence to change. One reading took the brief at face value: train three hundred managers on Veridian's new services model, exactly as specified. A second read it as a skills gap — managers never taught subscription-economy commercial skills, needing to be brought up to speed. A third held that the barrier was structural: managers protecting the P&L that had funded their careers, immovable by training alone. A fourth read it as a communications failure, with leadership having rolled out the pivot without explaining why it mattered to the people living it. Alongside the diagnosis, she had to pick the single headline metric she would defend to all three sponsors — behaviour and services-revenue lift, completion rate, participant satisfaction, or attendance hours logged — and set how candidly her one-page diagnosis would name the legacy-P&L resistance as the real barrier, on a dial from cautious to blunt.

In the second round, she converted the diagnosis into an architecture. First, how Veridian's own people entered the design and delivery: an advisory board that reviewed and signed off a design Atelier Cadence built alone; a co-design lab where Veridian's experts sat in the design room and its leaders co-taught the live modules; or an entirely in-house build with no Veridian voice in it at all. Then she had to split the full EUR 840,000 across four channels — on-the-job action-learning against real Veridian P&L problems, coaching and peer cohorts, in-person intensives and faculty instruction, and a digital self-paced platform — weighting toward on-the-job application and coaching over classroom instruction and digital work, starting from a placeholder split of EUR 200,000, 160,000, 320,000 and 160,000 that she was free to redraw entirely, so long as the four numbers still summed to the cap.

In the third round, she faced the steering committee live. She had to reconcile the CHRO, COO and CFO: lead with the CFO's language of cost-per-participant and a promised 95% completion rate; lead with the CHRO's language of maximised behaviour change, cost and completion left vague; build one scorecard blending satisfaction, impact, budget adherence and completion so all three sponsors signed the same page; or leave the decision to however the steering committee's own internal debate happened to resolve itself. Against the cheaper bid, she could narrow the design to match the consultancy's price by removing the action-learning and coaching layers; defend the co-creation premium directly, making the case for why designing with Veridian beat a rented academy; or set the competing bid aside entirely and take the time needed to get the design right. She also set how hard to push for a signature inside the four-week window, from patient to urgent, and decided whether to differentiate format by seniority band and language or run one uniform design for all three hundred managers.

In the fourth round, she committed the measurement and risk plan and pitched the CEO. Impact could be evidenced at three, six and nine months, in time for the FY27 budget meeting; measured a single time, at course end, through satisfaction scores and completion rate; or left to attendance logs while every remaining hour went into hitting the sixteen-week launch. Renewal could be left to hope for a reference logo and a later referral; built around a second cohort and an alumni-faculty plan tied to the FY27 decision; or set aside in favour of moving straight to the next mandate. She had to choose which delivery risk to address first: a register naming all three risks, each with its own mitigation and owner; senior managers too busy to complete, protected by reserved time and CEO sponsorship; legacy-minded managers gaming attendance, countered by tying progress to real P&L deliverables; or the sixteen-week launch slipping, addressed with a buffered, staged rollout. Finally, she set how firmly her CEO pitch would defend co-design as a measured business outcome, from measured to emphatic.

## Closing

Chastain closed the laptop at 8:55 a.m. and looked at the four weeks between her and Veridian's decision. The steering committee would not agree with itself before she spoke to them. The consultancy was still cheaper. She had one page to write before lunch.
