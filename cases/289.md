## Abstract

The Operations Planner at the case-universe Bogotá-headquartered Colombian whey-protein manufacturer ProteínaCo S.A.S. — approximately COP 48bn in 2025 revenue, 120 employees, one production plant in the Bogotá industrial-belt, 12,000-unit monthly baseline demand across three customer tiers (eight gym chains in Bogotá, twelve sports retailers in Quito, eighteen small retailers in Lima), three supplier options (a local dairy at COP 7,800 per kilo of whey with reliable lead time, a mid-tier Colombian consolidator at COP 7,200 per kilo with medium lead, and a U.S. import at COP 6,000 per kilo with a 10-day lead time and 8% quality rejection rate), and a Monday-morning directive from the Bogotá gym-chain buyer that a promotional campaign would spike demand 40% the following week — had been asked, in the April 2026 COO directive, to run the 60-minute three-monthly-cycle supply-chain primer sim and deliver a supply-chain commitment. The point of the sim was not that the decisions were complex. The point was that the decisions interacted. Choosing the cheapest supplier per kilo would amplify the promotion into a bullwhip that ran up the chain to the dairy. Holding the forecast steady would dampen it. The case opens at 2:24 p.m. on Thursday, April 23, 2026, at ProteínaCo's Bogotá plant office as **Natalia Suárez-Betancur**, a 31-year-old Operations Planner, prepares the three-cycle commitment.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Select the Cycle-1 supplier from the three cards — local at COP 7,800 with reliable lead, mid-tier at COP 7,200 with medium lead, import at COP 6,000 with a 10-day lead and 8% quality rejection — using Ellram's total-cost-of-ownership framework rather than the per-kilo-price headline, and articulate why the import option's cheaper per-kilo price loses on TCO once quality rejections, lead-time variability and stockout costs are loaded in.
2. Position the Cycle-1 order inside the Harris-EOQ U-curve rather than calculating the formula explicitly, and articulate why too few orders blow up holding costs while too many blow up ordering costs, why the optimal zone is a shape rather than a point, and why the 0/1/2-week safety-stock slider converts the EOQ curve into a risk-calibrated ordering policy.
3. Diagnose the Cycle-2 Bogotá gym-chain Monday-promotion spike as a classic Forrester-1958 bullwhip event running from the end consumer up to the dairy, articulate why the 40% end-demand spike becomes a 60–80% mid-tier order spike and a 100%+ dairy-whey spike through the amplification mechanic, and articulate why the forecast-hold discipline (treating the spike as transient) dampens the bullwhip while the forecast-revise discipline (treating the spike as a new baseline) amplifies it.
4. Read the Cycle-3 normalisation cycle and articulate why the bullwhip reverses — ProteínaCo stuck with four weeks of inventory, holding costs surge, the mid-tier supplier also stuck with dairy whey that arrived after the spike collapsed — and why the teams that held the forecast in Cycle 2 and used the safety-stock buffer for the spike avoid the Cycle-3 hangover.
5. Commit to the cost-to-serve customer-tier review — Bogotá gym chains profitable, Quito sports retailers break-even after cross-border logistics, Lima small retailers loss-making after quality-rejection costs — and articulate why a supply chain is not nodes and arrows but the cost of reacting to noise one tier down, and why retention, de-scoping or price renegotiation at the Lima tier is the cost-to-serve-driven commitment.

## The Protagonist

Suárez-Betancur, 31, had been Operations Planner since 2023, had completed a B.Sc. in Industrial Engineering at the Universidad de los Andes in 2018, had spent three years as a production scheduler at a Colombian beverage manufacturer where she had absorbed the Forrester bullwhip discipline during a 2020–2021 SKU-rationalisation programme, and had joined ProteínaCo in Q1 2023 as the firm's first operations-planning hire. The COO had assigned her the April sim on three considerations: her command of the EOQ-and-safety-stock discipline; her willingness to reject the founder's instinct that the cheapest import supplier was the right default; and her comfort telling the Lima small-retailer sales representative that the tier was loss-making after TCO.

---

## The Decision

Suárez-Betancur had to commit, by the 4:00 p.m. COO debrief, the three-cycle supply-chain posture.

1. **Cycle 1 — Supplier and safety stock.** Supplier: mid-tier Colombian consolidator at COP 7,200 per kilo (TCO lowest after quality and stockout loading). Safety stock: one week (not zero, not two). Ordering cadence: EOQ-zone position at approximately 2,800-unit order quantity, monthly frequency.
2. **Cycle 2 — Gym-chain promotion response.** Forecast hold. Use the one-week safety stock to absorb the 40% spike. Do not over-order the mid-tier supplier. Do not switch to the import for the spike.
3. **Cycle 3 — Normalisation response.** Ride out the modest overhang from the safety-stock draw-down. Communicate with the mid-tier supplier on the absence of a follow-on spike order.
4. **Cost-to-serve customer-tier review.** Bogotá gym chains (profitable, retain and grow). Quito sports retailers (break-even after cross-border logistics, retain with logistics re-negotiation). Lima small retailers (loss-making, price-renegotiate and de-scope).

## Industry and Market Context

The 1913–2025 operations-management literature produced three findings the sim had to respect. First, Harris's 1913 EOQ formula — operationalised as a U-curve zone rather than a point estimate — remains the documented benchmark for ordering-cadence optimisation in low-to-moderate-variance demand environments, with EOQ-zone ordering producing documented total-cost readings approximately 15–25% lower than ad-hoc ordering on comparable small-manufacturer cycles. Second, Forrester's 1958 bullwhip demonstration — subsequently formalised by Lee, Padmanabhan and Whang (1997) — correctly predicts that end-consumer demand variability amplifies up the supply chain by factors of 2–4× per tier under forecast-revise discipline, and that forecast-hold discipline with safety-stock buffering dampens the amplification by factors of 0.4–0.6× per tier. Third, Ellram's 1995 total-cost-of-ownership framework correctly predicts that per-unit-price-based supplier selection systematically under-performs TCO-based selection on comparable 2015–2024 cycles, with the under-performance concentrated in the quality-rejection and stockout-cost lines.

The post-2010 cost-to-serve literature added two findings. Customer-tier profitability analysis with cross-border logistics loaded at full cost produced documented retention recommendations approximately 30% different from gross-margin-based analysis. And cost-to-serve-driven tier review is the single strongest correlate of mid-sized manufacturer EBITDA uplift over a 12-month horizon.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The Cycle-1 forecast had been set at 12,000 units on the January 2026 annual-planning exercise. The three supplier cards had been on the table since Q3 2024 when the import option was first validated by the Bogotá quality-control team. The Bogotá gym-chain Monday-promotion directive landed April 11. The Cycle-2 window opens April 28.

### What Suárez-Betancur knew

The import's 8% quality-rejection rate loads approximately COP 480 per kilo of additional cost even before stockout risk; the mid-tier's COP 7,200 price compares favourably to the local COP 7,800 only when the mid-tier's medium lead time is not stressed; the gym-chain promotion will spike end-consumer demand approximately 40%; forecast-hold dampens bullwhip, forecast-revise amplifies it; the Lima tier is loss-making after quality-rejection loading.

### What she did not know

Whether the gym-chain promotion's 40% spike would be matched by a subsequent 10–15% baseline uplift (a permanent gain) or would fully collapse in Cycle 3; whether the mid-tier supplier's medium lead would absorb a Cycle-2 stress test without slipping; whether a Cycle-2 import-supplier hedge order at the lower COP 6,000 price would offset the quality-rejection cost at 8% or would exceed it at higher rejection rates; and whether the Lima tier price renegotiation would be accepted by the distributor or would trigger tier exit.

### What others were saying

**The COO**: *"Cheapest per kilo is not cheapest. I want the TCO commitment, not the headline price."*

**The Bogotá Gym-Chain Buyer**: *"Monday promotion. 40% spike. Two weeks. Do not stock us out."*

**The Mid-Tier Supplier Account Manager**: *"Hold the forecast. I can absorb the Cycle-2 spike with your safety stock if you do not revise upward."*

**The Quito Sales Representative**: *"Cross-border logistics loads are the issue. The tier is break-even. Do not cut it."*

**The Lima Sales Representative**: *"The small retailers will push back on any price increase. But the tier is loss-making."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Suárez-Betancur had narrowed the space to three commitment postures.

- **Option A — Mid-tier supplier, one-week safety stock, forecast-hold through Cycle 2, cost-to-serve-driven tier review (Recommended):** TCO-optimal supplier, EOQ-zone ordering, bullwhip dampening, tier profitability restored. Recommended.
- **Option B — Import supplier for the per-kilo saving, zero safety stock, forecast-revise through Cycle 2:** rejected — TCO-inferior, bullwhip amplification, Cycle-3 hangover cost, Lima tier loss-making deepens.
- **Option C — Local supplier for the lead-time reliability, two-week safety stock, forecast-hold through Cycle 2:** rejected — pays approximately COP 600 per kilo in supplier-price premium for redundancy that the one-week safety stock already purchases, and the additional week of holding cost in Cycle 3 compounds.

Her private view was that Option A was the right commitment and that the cycle most correlated with three-month total-cost performance was Cycle 2 — the gym-chain promotion — because Cycle 2 was the cycle where the forecast-hold discipline either dampened the bullwhip or amplified it, and the Cycle-3 outcome flowed directly from the Cycle-2 decision.
