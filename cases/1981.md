# Studio Lumibec: The Idle Twenty Per Cent

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1981 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1981 — *Communities of Creativity* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, May 11, 2026, Véronique Dubé opened two documents side by side. The first was the chief financial officer's draft development plan for the coming year. It recovered the twenty per cent of engineering, art and writing hours that Studio Lumibec protected from production schedules and moved them onto the next instalment of the flagship franchise, due to ship in nine months. The second was the quarterly craft report. It recorded that voluntary attendance at the rendering guild's Thursday sessions had fallen forty per cent since the crunch began. Both documents described the same 620 people.

Dubé had one planning cycle — four working sessions, ending with a presentation to the board — to set the studio's innovation model for the year. After that the sequel's schedule would be locked and the hours in dispute would already be spent.

## The Protagonist

Véronique Dubé, 43, had been vice-president of innovation at Studio Lumibec since 2022. She had joined in 2011 as a gameplay programmer, spent four years as a technical director, and moved into the innovation role after running the group that prototyped the studio's last original property. She had no project teams reporting to her. Her budget was the studio's new-development envelope; her authority over the three craft communities was, as the studio's own handbook put it, an invitation rather than a line.

## The Decision

Across the four sessions Dubé had to commit to one set of choices: where creative knowledge at Lumibec actually circulated; how much protected time to preserve, for whom and under what rules; how to divide the fixed CA$36 million; and how to fund the concept funnel, whether to counter a competitor's offer for two senior staff, and which measures she would defend to the board.

## The Company and its Setting

Studio Lumibec, founded in Montréal in 2008, made interactive entertainment. It employed 620 designers, programmers, artists, writers, audio staff and testers. It had recorded CA$180 million of revenue in 2025, of which roughly seventy per cent — about CA$126 million — came from a single franchise now in its sixth year and declining about twelve per cent a year (see **Exhibit 1**). The studio ran on a fixed CA$36 million annual research and new-development budget, of which 85 per cent went to the franchise and 15 per cent to original concepts.

Lumibec's delivery structure was project teams. Its knowledge structure was not. Three voluntary craft groups ran across the project lines: a rendering guild of about seventy members, a narrative circle of about forty-five, and a gameplay-systems community of about sixty (see **Exhibit 2**). Nobody was assigned to them and nobody was paid to attend. The studio's own account of its original titles traced each back to an experiment made inside one of them, in the twenty per cent of hours the studio held back from production schedules and called slack.

## The Situation

The slack cost money that was easy to count. Twenty per cent of the relevant teams' hours was roughly CA$9 million a year of production capacity the studio did not sell. Gilles Perreault, the chief financial officer, had put that number to the board in April beside the sequel's schedule, and argued that the studio could not fund unsold hours and ship on time at once. The board had asked for a thicker pipeline of new titles and a lower cost base in the same resolution.

What the slack produced was harder to count, and Dubé's own instruments were thin. She tracked three things. Voluntary participation across the three communities stood at 62 per cent of eligible staff, with the rendering guild's attendance down forty per cent. Idea throughput — the number of new concepts entering the funnel, kept as an index against a healthy year set at 100 — was still sitting at 100. Time from first idea to a playable prototype had stretched to 22 weeks; the studio's own history put a healthy figure near 14 weeks and treated anything past 30 as a stall. Eight concepts sat somewhere in the funnel, none of them yet greenlit.

Two people complicated the arithmetic. One senior engineer held the rendering guild together; one senior systems designer did the same for the gameplay community and carried ideas between projects that had no other reason to talk. A competitor had approached both. Human resources had costed a counter-offer covering salary, title and a growth path at roughly CA$400,000 for the pair.

If the plan carried forward unchanged — the slack redeployed, the budget left at 85/15, the two offers unmatched — the consequences were arithmetic. The franchise would keep shedding about twelve per cent of a CA$126 million base each year, the sequel would ship, and nothing in the funnel would reach a greenlight behind it; finance's model put the franchise's drop-off about eighteen months out.

## What Dubé Controlled

In the first session Dubé recorded her reading of the studio. She had to state where creative knowledge at Lumibec actually moved — down the project teams and their managers, through the three voluntary craft groups, or into the documentation and the internal wiki. She had to name the signal she would treat as decisive: the current quarter's idea throughput, the participation and prototype measures, or the CA$36 million envelope. She had to judge how far the concept pipeline depended on slack-born experiments — barely, almost entirely, or not at all, on the argument that concepts were commissioned by the greenlight committee. And she had to set how much of the cycle to spend diagnosing before committing, from none of it to all of it.

In the second session she set the protected time itself: any figure from zero to twenty per cent. She then had to say how it would be governed — returned to people as unrestricted hours with no rules attached, routed into the guild, circle and community with light stewardship and scheduled demonstration days, or allocated by each project manager against that project's roadmap. And she had to say who received it: all 620 staff equally, the craft roles that fed the concept funnel with the rendering guild first, or whoever was not staffed on the sequel.

In the third she divided the CA$36 million, setting the share going to original concepts anywhere from zero to sixty per cent, against today's fifteen. She had to decide how concepts reached the funnel — commissioned top-down by a formal innovation committee that the communities then executed, surfaced bottom-up from community experiments into a light set of stage reviews, or funded by whatever won the most support in the corridor. And she had to fix the risk logic for that money: one large bet on a single ambitious original title, several cheap prototypes with most expected to fail and the survivors backed harder, or only low-risk concepts close to what the studio already sold.

In the fourth she funded the pipeline — all eight concepts carried to prototype with nothing stopped, or several carried to prototype with the two strongest greenlit and the weakest stopped early, or nothing new greenlit and the whole envelope put behind the sequel. She answered the two offers: counter both, counter one, or match neither. She chose the measures she would defend to the board — this quarter's throughput and the sequel's ship date, or community participation, protected hours, retention and time-to-prototype, or revenue and margin alone. And she recorded, on a scale from zero to one hundred, how firmly she would hold her position when the board pushed back.

She would be judged on idea throughput, community participation, time-to-prototype, whether the two senior staff were still in place at the end, and a return on the original-concept portfolio that would not resolve until the last session.

## Closing

At 7:50 that Monday, Dubé wrote three headings on her office whiteboard — HOURS, MONEY, PEOPLE — and under the last one two names and the figure CA$400,000. The first session started at nine. The sequel was due in nine months.
