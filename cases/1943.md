# Lumen Atlas Foundation: The Planning Cycle in Public

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1943 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1943 — *Open the Strategy Room* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Wednesday, 4 March 2026, Rehana Malik opened the Atlas community forum before she opened her own inbox. The Board memo she had helped draft — a 2026–2031 strategy in ninety days, covering the Foundation's answer to artificial-intelligence (AI) assistants, a new revenue line, and a governance update — had been posted in full by a volunteer within forty-eight hours of the Board approving it. By breakfast the thread ran to several hundred replies.

Malik, Chief Strategy Officer of the Lumen Atlas Foundation, had until the Board's meeting on 15 June to deliver the plan. She had ten weeks of process time and GBP 600,000 to run the planning cycle with, and she would now run it in front of the 240,000 volunteers whose work the plan would govern. If the plan missed the June meeting, the Board had said it would commission an external management consultancy to write the strategy instead.

## The Protagonist

Rehana Malik, 46, had been Chief Strategy Officer since 2023, recruited from a national research council where she had run a member consultation across sixty institutions. She had joined Lumen Atlas as head of planning in 2020 and had sat in the room in 2021, on the staff side, when the Foundation's last five-year plan — written behind closed doors with a consultancy — was put to the community and rejected by 71% of those voting, then abandoned. She had drafted the March memo herself. Her name was on the leaked document the forum was arguing about.

## The Decision

Malik had to decide who entered the planning process and how deeply, how much of the process the community would see and at what point, which of the proposals that came back would become the plan and which would not, and how to carry the Board and the community through a single vote on 15 June. The Board had asked for a strategy. The community expected to be asked.

## The Company and its Setting

The Lumen Atlas Foundation, based in Cambridge, United Kingdom, and founded in 2006, operated Atlas, one of the ten most-visited reference sites in the world: 14 million volunteer-edited articles in 38 languages, read by 480 million people a month. Annual revenue was GBP 92 million, of which 87% came from 7.1 million small donors. The Foundation employed 340 staff. The encyclopaedia itself was written and governed by 240,000 active volunteer editors, of whom roughly 1,200 produced 41% of all content.

The Foundation had no shareholders and almost no formal authority over its own contributors. Editors could slow down, organise, fork the project, or stop. They had done so before: an editor slowdown during an earlier dispute had reduced donations.

Reading traffic had gone flat for the first time in the Foundation's history. AI chat assistants were answering questions that had previously brought people to Atlas, and the donation base followed reading traffic on a lag. A consortium of AI firms had offered GBP 20 million a year to license Atlas content. The offer was named in the leaked memo, so the forum already knew the number existed.

## The Situation

The Foundation tracked its planning cycle on four measures, each scored from 0 to 100 and reviewed by the Board (see **Exhibit 1**). On 4 March, buy-in — stakeholder support for the process and the plan — stood at 35. Idea quality, the diversity and strength of the input gathered, stood at 30. Legitimacy, the community's sense that the process had been fair, stood at 40. Coherence, the strategic focus the Board would test at the vote, stood at 45. None of the GBP 600,000 participation budget had been committed, and none of the ten weeks had been used.

Opinion in the forum had already hardened into four positions. One held that the Foundation should refuse all AI and commercial revenue and stay as it was. One wanted the GBP 20 million licence signed. One wanted a paid premium tier built instead. One wanted costs trimmed and nothing structural changed. Consultations of this kind had never returned Malik fewer than six hundred distinct proposals, and rarely more than nine hundred.

The Board that would vote on 15 June had seven members: three trustees elected by the community and four appointed. A plan carried 4–3 by the appointed trustees would be approved and would also be a plan the elected trustees had voted against.

If Malik changed nothing about how the Foundation planned — ran the cycle inside the building, brought a finished document to June — she would have a coherent plan and the 2021 precedent. If she missed the date, the consultancy wrote it. Neither route addressed the flat traffic, the eroding donation base, or the licence sitting on the table.

## What Malik Controlled

The participation budget bought inclusion at four depths, priced by the finance office (see **Exhibit 2**). Malik had to set one depth for each of nine groups: core editors, casual editors, chapter affiliates, major donors and grant-makers, small donors, staff, the Board, partner institutions such as libraries and museums, and readers. She could co-create with a group, consult it, inform it, or leave it out of the process.

She then had to set, for each of four phases — problem framing, option generation, financial modelling, and the final recommendation — whether that phase was fully public, published in summary only, or closed. She had to pick the channels that collected input: the open forum alone; structured surveys alongside the forum; forum, surveys, deliberative panels and town halls together; or invited deliberative panels alone. And she had to fix the moment the GBP 20 million figure became public: in week two, as a figure with no conditions attached; at the recommendation, once conditions, ring-fencing and a community veto had been attached to it; or not at all, disclosing only the final decision.

When the proposals came back she had to build one plan from them. She could take all four positions together, the licence and the premium tier, the refusal and the premium tier, or the status quo with a cost trim. The plan would commit to two priorities, to three, or to six or more. On the licence she could accept it as offered, accept it with the revenue ring-fenced for editor tools and a community veto on the terms, or decline it and forgo the money. Those whose proposals were not adopted could receive nothing beyond the published plan, a general acknowledgement that all input had been valued, or an itemised log naming each position and showing where its idea had gone or why it had been dropped.

On 15 June she would pitch the plan on its content and the numbers behind it, on the openness of the process that had produced it, or on both together. She would commit to no new mechanism, leaving implementation to the existing structures; to an annual open review with no binding community role; or to a standing community-and-board strategy council with a defined role through 2031. She would close with an announcement that the Board had approved the strategy and nothing further, a statement describing the plan as a historic and fully co-created effort, or an account of what the community had shaped, what had been chosen, what had not, and how people stayed involved. And if the community moved against the plan in the last days — the instrument it had used before was a coordinated editing freeze — she would withdraw the contested clause, decline to reopen it, or keep it with the revenue ring-fenced and a community veto on the terms.

## Closing

At 8:15 Malik closed the forum tab and wrote the nine groups down the left-hand side of a sheet of paper, with four columns beside them. The Board would sit on 15 June. She had ten weeks, GBP 600,000, and a memo that had been public for two days.
