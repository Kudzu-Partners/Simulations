# Helvetia Präzision AG: The Energy Audit

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1807 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1807 — *Energize the Org* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of 14 January 2026, Matthias Kobler opened two documents that had landed overnight and realised they described the same unit from opposite directions. The first was the quarterly pulse survey for Robotics & Automation, the unit he had led for five weeks, plotted onto the two-axis grid Helvetia's HR team used company-wide for energy and its direction. Only 38% of answers fell into the box for effort aimed at getting the work done; 16% fell into the box for effort aimed at something else — politics, blame, protecting turf — a share that had doubled in a year. The second was a resignation letter, the fourth filed that week, from engineers on the same team.

Kobler had three quarters until the Executive Board's review to move the productive-energy reading above 55% and cut annual departures below 12%, without new budget. Miss either number and the board would fold Robotics & Automation into another division and end, company-wide, the compressed four-day week the unit had pioneered.

## The Protagonist

Kobler, 45, had spent twelve years at Helvetia Präzision AG, the last six inside Robotics & Automation, most recently as Head of Applications Engineering. He had been promoted into the unit's top job when his predecessor left for a competitor, inheriting a team in which several new direct reports had, five weeks earlier, been his peers. His reputation rested on discipline rather than charisma: the applications group he had run posted the unit's best on-time delivery record for three years, by refusing to commit engineers to more than they could deliver. He had never run a unit this size, and had no human-resources background.

## The Decision

Across four checkpoints, Kobler had to read the unit's condition before acting, commit a quarter's budget and his own attention across a menu of fixes, decide how much freedom people kept over where and when they worked, defend a wellbeing budget Finance wanted to shrink, and tell the board whether the recovery he reported was durable or borrowed.

## The Company and its Setting

Helvetia Präzision AG, headquartered in Winterthur, machined high-tolerance parts for medical-device, robotics and aerospace clients across Europe. Its Robotics & Automation unit — 180 people across engineering, applications, production and a commercial team, CHF 64 million of revenue against Helvetia's CHF 218 million total — had been the company's growth engine for a decade, and had persuaded the rest of the company to trial a four-day working week, a decision other units still mentioned with a mix of admiration and envy. Then an enterprise-software migration went badly, two engineering teams were merged into one, and the unit head who had championed the four-day week left for a rival. Kobler took over five weeks before the survey landed.

## The Situation

Read against exit interviews and delivery data, the survey showed effort was not missing — it was misdirected and running on no reserve. Beyond the 38% productive and 16% hostile readings, 24% described people who had gone quiet and stopped raising problems, and 22% described people who were comfortable but coasting. A separate 100-point engagement index had slid from 74 to 58 in four quarters, and 31% of staff scored in the range flagged as high risk for exhaustion, with overtime averaging nine hours a week. On-time delivery of engineering milestones had fallen from 88% to 71%, putting a CHF 4.2 million contract renewal with a medtech client at risk. Voluntary departures ran at 18% a year against a 7% company average, and Helvetia's finance function put the cost of replacing one engineer at roughly CHF 95,000 (Exhibit 1). Left alone, none of these numbers would move on their own, and the unit would still be short of both board thresholds when the three quarters ran out.

## What Kobler Controlled

At his first checkpoint, before spending anything, Kobler had to commit to a reading of the unit. He had to name which of four descriptions best fit Robotics & Automation as a whole: high-intensity effort pointed against the organisation itself — politics, blame, an undermining faction; low-intensity withdrawal, people who had quietly stopped raising problems; no real energy problem, just a unit not working hard enough; or energy that was relaxed and positive but had stopped stretching. He had to separately read the production group, whose answers looked quiet and disengaged rather than combative: was it withdrawn, needing re-engagement and small wins rather than confrontation; quietly hostile, needing direct confrontation to break the resistance; or simply a quiet, steady team that was not a problem at all? He had to name what he believed was driving the hostile share of the unit — a view that engineers wanted better pay; months of chronic overload wearing the unit down on its own; the compressed four-day week itself eating into on-site time; or a leadership vacuum left by the departed unit head, filled by an informal coalition. And he had to set, on a single dial from zero to a hundred, how much of his first move to concentrate on the group he judged most hostile versus spread evenly.

At his second checkpoint, Kobler held CHF 240,000 of discretionary budget and twenty units of scarce attention to commit, and had to decide what moved first: easing the workload on the two late projects, confronting the group he suspected of driving hostility directly, holding a unit-wide off-site to reset morale in one event, or hiring replacements for the four engineers already gone — priced in Exhibit 2. Whichever came first, he still had to divide the twenty attention units across workload relief, the hostility problem, recognition gestures, and manager coaching in whatever proportions he judged right, and decide separately how much cash to spend on recognition activity alongside it.

At the third checkpoint, two decisions arrived together. Kobler had to set the unit's ongoing work-location policy — require fixed on-site core days for the whole unit, keep the four-day week and add light in-person collaboration time, move to a flexible hybrid pattern with agreed overlap days, or return to five days on-site to speed the late projects — and respond to a finance proposal to cut the unit's CHF 1.1 million annual wellbeing-and-development budget by 40%, about CHF 440,000: protect the budget in full and make the case in board terms, accept the proposed cut to help this year's operating margin, or reshuffle what sits inside it while holding the total unchanged. Whatever he chose, he also had to set the financial case's rigour, on a scale from zero to a hundred, and decide how much further discretionary cash, if any, to put behind deepening workload relief.

At the final checkpoint, Kobler presented the cycle to the board. He had to decide what to do about the nine-hour weekly overtime average that had never come down: declare a stop-doing list and cap the hours before adding anything new, add protected recovery time — real breaks and focus blocks — alongside whatever came next, layer two more energising initiatives on top of the existing workload, or leave the overtime pattern as it was, a sign of commitment rather than risk. He had to choose one monthly measure to watch for early signs of relapse — a combined read on energy mix, overtime and sick days; on-time delivery alone, the number the board already watched; or departures alone, reviewed only once a quarter — and how to frame the pitch: a win built on the enthusiasm of the off-site event, a durable recovery with named guardrails and owners, or good numbers that justified pushing harder next quarter. Alongside that framing, he had to state, on a scale from zero to a hundred, how firmly he would argue that sustainable energy mattered more than energy borrowed against the next quarter.

## Closing

At 8:15 a.m., Kobler set the two documents side by side: a survey telling him where his unit's energy was going, and a resignation letter telling him four engineers were already gone. The board's clock started when he read them. He had four checkpoints ahead, a budget that would not be topped up, and no map yet of which parts of Robotics & Automation would respond to which kind of attention.
