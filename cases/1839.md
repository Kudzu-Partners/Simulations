# Botané SAS: What the Premium Was Worth

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1839 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1839 — *Marketing for Impact* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 14 March 2026, Botané SAS's board approved a national campaign it needed to work: an 18% increase in revenue inside twelve months, worth roughly €14 million, built around a new "Climate Positive" hair-care line and the company's first entry into mainstream supermarket chains. Camille Auvray, the company's Chief Marketing Officer, had eight weeks to get that campaign to market. In the same week the board signed off, the European Union's Green Claims Directive and France's climate law tightened what a brand could say about its own environmental record, and a competitor was fined €2 million for a "carbon neutral" claim regulators judged unsubstantiated. A claims-testing advocacy group had already said, informally, that it would put Botané's launch claims through the same kind of test within 72 hours of the first broadcast.

## The Protagonist

Auvray, 43, had spent eleven years at Botané, the last four as CMO, after joining from a larger personal-care group where she had run regional brand marketing. She had helped write the sustainability claims that built the company's reputation and had sat on the committee that pursued its B Corp certification. She reported to the CEO and, through him, to a board that included the founder and a growth-equity investor whose own position was tied in part to the year's revenue number. The +18% target was already wired into the leadership bonus pool. Nobody on the board had told her to cut corners on the claims; they had given her a number and left her the method.

## The Decision

Auvray had to decide, across four stages running from mid-March through the weeks after launch (see **Exhibit 2**), which of the campaign's proposed environmental claims to run and how, how to split a fixed €3.2 million budget across the channels that would carry them, which of three partnership offers to accept, and — if a challenge landed once the campaign was live — how to answer it. Every one of those decisions moved the same three things: how much revenue growth the campaign produced against the board's target, how much of the "buy because of what it stands for" customer segment survived the campaign, and how exposed the company was, on a zero-to-100 scale, to a regulator or a public claims audit.

## The Company and its Setting

Founded in Lyon in 2009 as a farmers'-market soap maker, Botané had grown into a €78 million natural personal-care business (see **Exhibit 1**) selling shampoo, soap and skincare through French pharmacies, organic retailers, and its own website, with 240 employees and a 9% EBIT margin — thinner than a conventional competitor's, because the company reinvested in its B Corp status, a reforestation programme, and a supply chain it could trace to 92% of its raw material sources. Its own customer tracking said 71% of buyers chose Botané "because of what it stands for," not price or performance, and that conviction was what let the company charge roughly 25% more than conventional rivals for comparable products — a premium with nothing else holding it up if the story behind the ingredients ever failed publicly.

## The Situation

Seven claims were drafted for the launch. Three had already been flagged by legal and the new regulatory guidance as fragile: "Climate Positive," attached to the new hair-care line, rested on a reforestation programme that was real but not yet fully verified by an outside auditor; "100% natural" and "plastic-free" described a product whose refill caps were, in fact, 6% fossil-based plastic. Running all seven exactly as drafted would chase the full +18% fastest, and would also hand the DGCCRF — the French consumer-protection regulator — grounds to fine the campaign up to 80% of its spend, as much as €2.56 million, the same order of penalty just applied to a competitor. Dropping every claim that carried any risk would remove that exposure but, on the marketing team's own modelling, left the campaign closer to a few points of growth than eighteen. A publicised claims dispute would not only risk a fine; the tracker Auvray relied on was the same one that measured the 71% values segment behind the 25% premium. If nothing changed from the draft in front of her, the campaign would go to market with all three fragile claims live, at the height of a regulatory season built specifically to catch exactly that kind of claim.

## What Auvray Controlled

On the flagship "Climate Positive" claim, Auvray could run it as it stood, hold it back until the pending third-party verification was complete, or drop the claim from the campaign entirely. On "100% natural" and "plastic-free," she could narrow both to a specific formulation — for instance, describing the product as roughly 94% plant-based rather than absolutely natural — leave the wording as it was, or remove any reference to ingredients and packaging from the campaign altogether. Tied to that same audit, she could set aside anywhere from nothing up to €0.8 million of the €3.2 million budget to pay for the verification work a claim would need.

Once the claim set was fixed, Auvray had to divide whatever remained of the budget across three channels: national television and retail media, which moved sales fastest but carried whichever claim risk was already in the mix furthest and fastest; paid social and influencer placement, similarly quick-moving but harder to keep contained once a claim was circulating; and the company's own content, PR and in-store materials, which grew sales more slowly and could not by itself close an eighteen-point gap, but protected credibility more directly than either paid channel. Bundled into the same stage was the supermarket question: enter Carrefour and Leclerc on Botané's own pricing and its recyclable packaging at a smaller volume, accept the chains' terms of a 12% lower shelf price and plastic multipack wrapping, or decline the listing for now.

Three partnership offers arrived while the campaign was being built. A celebrity with 4.1 million followers, whose own feed carried frequent fast-fashion promotion, offered reach no other channel could match; Auvray could accept the partnership as proposed, decline it, or substitute a smaller tier of independent creators instead. An established eco-label offered to co-brand the launch, lending its own verified credibility in exchange for 30% of the campaign's control and margin; she could accept the arrangement or decline it. A sustainability NGO offered an endorsement on condition that Botané publish its full supply-chain data, including the 8% of sourcing it could not yet trace; she could agree to disclose it or decline the endorsement quietly. Separately, she could set how much of a pre-drafted response to a possible claims challenge would be built on documented evidence rather than reassurance, on a scale from none of it to all of it.

If the challenge she had prepared for did land — a claims-testing group publicly disputing "Climate Positive," a journalist asking directly whether third-party verification existed, the DGCCRF opening an inquiry, a retail buyer asking whether to pull stock — Auvray would have to choose a response: substantiate the claim with whatever verification she had actually funded, reframe it into a narrower claim she could fully prove, withdraw the contested claim alone while the rest of the campaign continued, withdraw the entire campaign, or defend the claim as it stood. She would also decide her holding line to a direct question — answer with the evidence itself, answer with a statement of Botané's commitment to its mission, or decline to comment while the company reviewed internally — how strictly, expressed as a percentage, the whole recovery would be run on evidence rather than reassurance, and what standing process, if any, would govern how the next claim got approved: no fixed process, an informal marketing self-check, or a named claims owner in legal and sustainability who signed off before any claim ran.

## Closing

By the second week of April, the launch date was fixed and the claim set had to be locked. Auvray had eight weeks from the board's approval to get the campaign right, €3.2 million to spend once, and a claims-testing group that had already said, before a single advertisement had aired, that it would be watching the first 72 hours.
