# SuryaHealth Logistics: The Vaccine Run

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1847 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1847 — *The Vaccine Run* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 4 March 2026, Meera Kulkarni, Operations Lead at SuryaHealth Logistics Pvt. Ltd., opened the state's allocation notice for the coming measles-rubella catch-up campaign and found the number the district health officer had warned her about: the central supply had arrived 18% short of what the plan required. The campaign was due to launch in nineteen days, on 23 March, and had to reach 210,000 eligible children across eleven districts of western Maharashtra inside a six-week window. The hill blocks in SuryaHealth's territory were already trailing the urban wards by nineteen percentage points in coverage, and the funder paying for the campaign wanted that gap closed without any additional money.

Kulkarni would answer to that funder in six weeks with a scorecard, and every commitment she made between now and launch day would be locked in before the next round of decisions began.

## The Protagonist

Meera Kulkarni, 41, had been Operations Lead at SuryaHealth Logistics since 2021, having joined the organisation in 2016 from a state public-health logistics cell. Trained as an industrial engineer, she had built the barcode-scan tracking system that gave SuryaHealth's analytics cell near real-time visibility into coverage, stockouts, and wastage at each of its 38 cold-chain points. That same data was the reason the district health officer had handed her this campaign, and the reason she could no longer plead ignorance about the gap between the hill blocks and the urban wards. Her standing at SuryaHealth rested on a simple record: her last two routine immunisation cycles had closed on budget and above the historical coverage average.

## The Decision

Across four rounds before and through launch, Kulkarni had to decide where the network would actually fail and what supply figure to plan against; what service level to promise each type of site and how to split the safety-stock buffer between the central store and the edge; how to route the cold-chain fleet within the transport capacity and the temperature limits; and what rule would decide who received the last dose when a site ran low, including whether to hold that rule when a shock hit mid-campaign.

## The Company and its Setting

SuryaHealth Logistics Pvt. Ltd., founded in 2014 and headquartered in Pune, was a not-for-profit health-logistics operator running the cold-chain and last-mile distribution arm of a state immunisation programme across eleven districts of western Maharashtra. It operated one regional vaccine store, 38 cold-chain points, and a fleet of 22 vehicles — nine refrigerated vans and thirteen motorcycle cold-boxes — serving 240 immunisation sessions a week across urban wards, peri-urban clusters, and remote hill blocks, on an annual operating budget of ₹46 crore, roughly 60% of it logistics and cold-chain.

The organisation's distinguishing asset was data: barcode-level tracking of nearly every dose, giving its analytics cell the ability to compute coverage, wastage, and delivery performance in near real time. That same data had surfaced the fact driving this campaign: coverage in the three hill blocks lagged the urban wards by nineteen percentage points.

## The Situation

The manufacturer's lot slip meant the regional store would receive only enough vaccine to nominally cover about 172,000 of the 210,000 eligible children in the campaign window, an 18% shortfall. Projected coverage — the share of eligible children the campaign was on track to reach — stood at 80% for the full six weeks against the funder's target of at least 90%; each percentage point represented roughly 2,100 children. The hill-urban equity gap, the difference in coverage between the hill blocks and the urban wards, stood at nineteen percentage points, and the funder wanted it closed to eight points or less. Effective supply — children the dose stock could actually cover once open-vial wastage was subtracted, distinct from doses simply shipped — stood level with the nominal 172,000 until a wastage assumption was applied; wastage was budgeted at 8%, a rate that climbed in thinly attended hill sessions. Cost per dose delivered — the true unit cost of an immunisation actually given, including wastage and failed delivery — stood at ₹180. The cold-chain fleet's total transport capacity was 3,400 vial-equivalents per delivery cycle, and any route had to keep doses inside a four-hour cold-chain window or risk the vaccine arriving non-viable. All logistics spending had to fit inside a fixed envelope of ₹3.8 crore; an extra dedicated remote delivery cycle cost ₹0.62 lakh. Left as scheduled, the plan would land at 80% coverage, the nineteen-point gap would persist, and a state review had flagged that a confirmed outbreak cluster or week-three coverage below 60% would trigger reallocation of the campaign to a different operator.

## What Kulkarni Controlled

In round one, Kulkarni named where she believed the network would actually fail: open-vial wastage in thin hill sessions leaving effective supply well below the nominal figure; high-demand urban wards drawing doses away from the hill blocks; a shortfall in raw cold-chain transport capacity; or a staffing problem at the sessions rather than in the supply chain. She then chose the effective supply figure to plan against once the budgeted 8% wastage was netted out: roughly 158,000, netting the wastage out now; the full nominal 172,000, deferring the wastage question; or an optimistic 180,000, assuming the manufacturer made good the shortfall in week two. A third dial set how sharply her team's diagnostic attention concentrated on the point she had named versus spreading evenly across the network.

In round two she assigned a service level across site types — equity-weighted at 95% for hill sites and 92% for urban wards; a flat 93% applied evenly everywhere; or a split weighted toward the urban wards, 97% in town and 85% in the hills — and split the safety-stock buffer between the central store and the edge sites that could not be resupplied mid-week, the two figures required to sum to 100%. She also chose how to handle multi-dose vials in the thinly attended hill sessions: switching to 5-dose vials with batched caregivers, at a cost of ₹0.35 crore; keeping the existing 10-dose vials everywhere; or shipping extra 10-dose vials to the hills as a margin against shortfall.

In round three she designed the route within the 3,400 vial-equivalent capacity and the four-hour cold-chain limit — a mixed approach combining hub-and-spoke runs across the plains with a motorcycle cold-box leg for the final hill kilometres; dedicated remote runs, sending vehicles directly to the remote sites rather than through a hub, at ₹0.62 lakh per extra cycle; or hub-and-spoke milk runs only, the cheapest option, which served the hill blocks last — chose how many extra dedicated cycles to add at that price, and set cold-chain protection effort (refrigeration, dry-ice top-ups, route timing) on a 0-to-100 percent scale.

In round four she published the triage rule for the last dose: by coverage deficit plus outbreak risk, serving the hill child behind on coverage first; by age cohort, youngest eligible children first regardless of location; first-come-first-served; or leaving the call to be made site by site, without a written rule. She then answered a mid-campaign shock on day 22 — a hill-block cluster signal alongside a further 5% supply slip — by holding her rule and reallocating supply toward the cluster; freezing the plan and escalating to the state for emergency doses; or setting equity aside and directing supply to the easier urban wards. She decided whether to stay within the ₹3.8 crore envelope, treating any overrun as borrowed from next quarter's routine immunisation budget; allow a small, controlled overrun of ₹0.2 crore, only if that was the difference between finishing under 90% and reaching it; or spend whatever it took and deal with the budget consequences afterward. Finally, she rated her own conviction, from 0 to 100 percent, in how firmly she could defend the resulting scorecard of coverage, equity gap, effective supply, and cost per dose delivered (Exhibit 1) to the funder as coverage that would last.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 4 March, Kulkarni pulled up the round-by-round decision sequence she would work through before the 23 March launch (Exhibit 2). Nineteen days remained. Whatever she decided in the next four weeks would be measured against a funder that wanted 90% coverage, a closed equity gap, and a bill that did not exceed ₹3.8 crore.
