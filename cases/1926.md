# Tuatha Renewables: The Sliabh Bán Decision

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1926 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1926 — *Value Conflict* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, March 9, 2026, Niamh Corrigan read the ecological review twice: once in the copy her own department had commissioned, and once in the version a national newspaper had posted that morning. Both said the same thing. The turbine layout consented for Sliabh Bán — the 90-megawatt (MW) wind project Tuatha Renewables plc had spent three years bringing to financial close — would damage roughly 14 hectares of protected peatland that the townlands below the ridge treated as part of their own history. An assessment commissioned two years earlier, under Corrigan's signature, had reached a different conclusion.

By that evening the local committee had asked for the project to be stopped or moved, and the infrastructure fund that owned most of Tuatha had asked for a plan within the week. The environmental organisation that had supported the consent backed the community, then noted that cancelling Sliabh Bán would forfeit about 180,000 tonnes a year of avoided carbon dioxide (CO₂). Corrigan had forty-five days to decide what Tuatha would do about Sliabh Bán and to be able to say why. That was when the construction drawdown terms lapsed on a project already 40% financed.

## The Protagonist

Niamh Corrigan, 44, had been Tuatha's Chief Sustainability Officer since 2022. She joined in 2019 from a rural development agency, where she ran statutory consultations and watched several fail. At Tuatha she wrote the community-benefit standard applied to every site. In 2023 she had sat in the Sliabh Bán parish hall and told 90 people they would be heard before anything was built. The chief executive handed her the file on the morning of the leak with one instruction: whatever the company did, it had to be able to stand over how it had decided.

## The Decision

Inside the financing window she had to settle three things, in order: what kind of dispute this was and who had a claim to be at the table; how the decision would be reached; and what it would be, together with the account of it she gave the parties it disappointed. A credible challenge to the ecological assessment could put the project into judicial review and freeze it for 12 to 18 months.

## The Company and its Setting

Tuatha Renewables plc, founded in 2011 and headquartered in Galway, developed and operated onshore wind and solar assets across the west of Ireland: 480 MW operating, a pipeline of a further 600 MW, 310 employees, EUR 168 million of revenue. An infrastructure fund held the majority of the equity and marketed Tuatha to its own investors as its flagship environmental holding.

One fact shaped every conversation in the building. Tuatha had built community-benefit funds and local co-ownership into its projects years before either was standard. The sentence in its tender documents and on the first slide of every pitch was that it was the developer communities trusted. That sentence was the basis on which it won sites, and the review put it in question.

## The Situation

Sliabh Bán was consented, 40% financed, and expected to return a project margin of EUR 48 million after costs and risk (see **Exhibit 1**). The fund had EUR 140 million of committed capital in it. Roughly 220 construction jobs and EUR 18 million of local spend depended on it going ahead. About 1,200 residents lived in the affected townlands, alongside a heritage group that had catalogued the bog's cultural sites. Her engineers had costed moving the turbines clear of the bog at between nothing and EUR 22 million.

The sustainability committee's dashboard carried three numbers beside the project accounts. A reputation index, built by an external agency from media and community sentiment, stood at 72 out of 100. A quarterly stakeholder panel scored two more: whether Tuatha's decisions were justifiable by reasons the panel could accept, at 40 out of 100, and whether the parties affected felt genuinely heard, at 30 (see **Exhibit 1**).

The positions had hardened by that evening (see **Exhibit 2**). The committee chair asked Corrigan a question she wrote down verbatim: "If we object, will anything actually change?" The fund's investment director said it could live with a decision it could defend to its own investors, and not with one that lost the returns and the trust together. The environmental organisation's campaigns director wanted the peatland protected; its energy lead pointed at the avoided CO₂ and would not sign the same letter. The site foreman had 220 people on a start date.

If Tuatha did nothing, the position resolved itself without her. The review was public and the objection lodged; a challenge to the ecological assessment would hold the project in review for a year or more; and at day forty-five the drawdown terms lapsed and the financing had to be rebuilt from the beginning.

## What Corrigan Controlled

Corrigan controlled how the company characterised the dispute. Four readings circulated in the building: that community trust, climate, the fund's duty, livelihoods and the bog were each defensible and genuinely incompatible; that the substance was settled and what remained was the telling of it, a communications problem; that one committee was driving the objection and the file would close once it went quiet; or that the consent was valid and the only question was the entitlement to build under it. She set who had a claim to a seat: everyone the project touched, the parties that could stop the company, the parties generating the coverage, or the board and its counsel alone. She recorded as a percentage how sharply she separated the tradable parts of the dispute from the rest.

She controlled the shape of the process. She could convene every affected party in one room, with ground rules agreed. She could convene a forum once the layout was settled internally and take reactions to it. She could keep the fund and the committee in separate rooms, talking to each in turn. She could hold no external process and announce the decision when it was made. Within that she set how much of the file — the ecological review, the financials, the legal exposure — participants received. She set the rule the process ran under: that reasons raised could change the decision, that the process was advisory, or that nothing proceeded without unanimity.

The decision itself was hers: build the consented layout as it stood; move the turbines clear of the peatland at a cost the project carried; stop the project; or pay the local committee EUR 3 million under a confidentiality agreement and build as consented. She set the sum committed to repairing the harm to the bog and the community, between EUR 0 and EUR 22 million. She chose the account she gave the parties it went against: reasons each could accept, with what had been traded away named openly; the planning consent; the board's authority to decide; or the decision alone, with no further comment.

She controlled what Tuatha would say if the decision became public and contested. She could set out how the process had worked and what it had changed; restate the company's mission and leave the process undiscussed; describe the objection as a minority view against a valid consent; or say nothing. She had three minutes with the fund's investment committee and a community representative in the same room, and could give one account addressed to both, a separate account for each, or one that opened with the returns. She had to name what she had been aiming at: a compromise, an agreement to disagree, unanimity, or a noted disagreement and a decision taken anyway. And she recorded, as a percentage, how firmly she held that how a decision was reached mattered more than how fast the objection ended.

The dashboard carried her round by round: the reputation index, the panel's two scores, the project margin in euro, and the sum committed against the EUR 22 million ceiling.

## Closing

Corrigan wrote the four parties across the top of a single sheet and the four measures down the side. The drawdown terms lapsed on April 23. She had a review she could not un-publish, a consent she could still build on, and a sentence in the company's tender documents she had to be able to say out loud in the parish hall.
