# Glocal Balancing Act: Aurelia Türkiye's H2 Plan

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1836 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1836 — *Glocal Balancing Act* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, June 1, 2026, Elif Kaya, the Subsidiary Marketing Lead at Aurelia Türkiye, opened two messages that had landed within the same hour. The first was a directive from global headquarters: every Aurelia subsidiary would run the newly standardized "Bella Global" campaign at no less than 70 percent of second-half media weight, to fund a rebrand headquarters was financing worldwide. The second was from her own trade team: a local rival had relaunched its value-tier hair-care line at a price 22 percent below Bella's, built around a campaign timed to the Ramadan and wedding season. Between the two sat the number that made both messages urgent — Aurelia Türkiye's entire second-half marketing budget was frozen at ₺240 million, with no top-up coming from headquarters no matter which instruction she answered first.

She had four weeks to lock the plan.

## The Protagonist

Kaya, 43, had led marketing for Aurelia Türkiye for two years, having spent the eleven before that inside the subsidiary's brand and trade teams. She had built her standing on a claim repeated at enough planning meetings that people now expected it from her: that the Turkish team understood the Ramadan and wedding season, and the traditional-trade shelf, better than a campaign built for forty markets at once ever could. Her bonus, like every subsidiary lead's, moved with two things that did not always move together — Bella's share performance in market, and headquarters' assessment of how faithfully the subsidiary ran the global playbook.

## The Decision

Kaya had to decide how to split the frozen ₺240 million between the standardized global campaign and local adaptation, how to answer the rival if it moved again before the season's window closed, and what to tell headquarters about any of it — knowing the same choices that defended Bella's position in market would be read, line by line, by the people who controlled the subsidiary's autonomy.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurelia Türkiye was the Istanbul subsidiary, based in the Maslak business district, of Aurelia Group, a €14 billion European home- and personal-care multinational. Its flagship in the local market was Bella, a hair-care brand holding 19.0 percent value share — the number two position, three points behind the local leader (Exhibit 1). Headquarters supplied a standardized "Bella Global" campaign built for use across forty countries; the subsidiary held a separate budget meant to adapt that campaign, or fund something of its own, for Turkish taste, language and the religious calendar. The two were meant to reinforce each other. This season they were pulling apart.

## The Situation

The two shocks had landed together for a reason: headquarters wanted the standardized campaign at scale precisely as the local market moved against it. The rival's relaunch undercut Bella by 22 percent and was timed to the Ramadan and wedding occasions — the year's two biggest hair-care moments — while the lira's recent slide had pushed the cost of Aurelia's imported inputs up by roughly 15 percent, so every lira of the frozen budget now bought less than it had a season earlier. Kaya's dashboard showed four numbers moving under her: Bella's value share at 19.0 percent, against a defense floor of 18; the Brand Consistency Index — headquarters' own score of how closely the subsidiary tracked the global standard — at 88 out of 100, against a review floor of 80 below which headquarters could put the subsidiary's autonomy under review; a Marketing ROI index, adjusted for the weaker lira, sitting at 40 out of 100; and a Marketing Agility Index, meant to capture how well she balanced speed against fit, at 22 out of 100 (Exhibit 2). If she simply held the compliant course — ran the mandate to the letter and left the local budget untouched — the rival kept the season it was already winning, and none of the four numbers had anything pushing back on their own.

## What Kaya Controlled

Before she committed a lira, Kaya had to name what she believed was happening in the market: whether the more urgent local signal was a shift in format or fragrance preference, a straight price match against the rival everywhere Bella sold, the Ramadan and wedding occasion, where the rival's value tier was said to be gaining ground, or no strong local signal at all — in which case the global platform could be trusted to carry Türkiye as built. Alongside that, she had to name the single biggest mismatch between the global creative and Turkish reality, choosing among a packaging issue in traditional trade, a language-only localization gap, the global creative depicting the wrong season entirely, or no real mismatch. She also set, as a percentage, how much rigor to put into reading the local market before committing any spend.

Then came the split itself: what share of the ₺240 million went to the standardized campaign against headquarters' 70 percent mandate, and how the remainder was spent — held back as contingency to stay maximally compliant, put into a separate value-tier line built to fight the rival directly, poured into trade promotions and discounts, or used to adapt the global asset itself for the Ramadan and wedding season so a single campaign served both purposes. Depending on where she landed against the mandate, she also had to decide what to tell headquarters: nothing, since she wasn't deviating; nothing, while quietly running more local weight anyway; or a documented case asking headquarters to flex the number to roughly 65 percent for the season.

Mid-season, when the rival cut price again and its campaign went viral, she had to respond inside the same frozen budget — matching the cut everywhere, holding the existing plan exactly as mandated, commissioning a fully modeled plan to be delivered later in the season, or moving fast with an adapted version of the global asset alongside a targeted counter to the value tier. She also set how much speed to put behind that response, on a dial running from a slower, more deliberate process to a good-enough plan locked within the following week, and how hard to re-screen the plan's economics against the weaker lira — adjusting only the largest line items, leaving everything at the prior year's costs, or re-running every activity's payback at today's costs. When headquarters asked why the Consistency Index was slipping, her four-line answer could be defensive, silent until the season ended, or a transparent account of what she was trading for what.

Finally, in a pitch to headquarters' Global Brand Director and the subsidiary's General Manager, she would frame the season's result — as a trade in which consistency had been given up for share, as compliance with the 70 percent rule, or as an outcome in which global and local had reinforced each other — and commit to a 90-day scorecard covering share alone, no scorecard at all, or share, consistency and ROI together with named owners. She would also decide how to argue for the subsidiary's standing with headquarters going into the following year: deferring to headquarters on every future call, pushing headquarters to drop standardization for the subsidiary outright, or building the case from the season's own results for more local latitude — and set, as a percentage, how firmly she defended her own account of how the season had actually gone (Exhibit 2).

## Closing

At her desk in Maslak, Kaya opened a blank planning grid with four columns, one for each week she had left before the media plan locked. The rival's Ramadan campaign was already running. The lira had not moved back. Headquarters was waiting for a number, and so was the market.
