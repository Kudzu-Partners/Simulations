# Quillca-Lima: Realigning the Executive-Education Portfolio

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1977 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1977 — *Program Pivot* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, March 16, 2026, Lucía Talavera read the spring reconciliation twice before she accepted it. Open enrollment at Escuela de Alta Dirección Quillca — Quillca-Lima, the executive-education unit of a Peruvian business school in the San Borja district of Lima — had landed 24% below plan. The flagship face-to-face courses in finance, marketing and leadership, the catalog that had carried the unit for a decade, had filled 62% of the seats offered, against the 80% an open course needed to cover its own costs. On the second screen sat two requests for proposal (RFPs) from the unit's two largest corporate clients, for programs Quillca-Lima did not run.

Talavera had to say what the next catalog would contain before the school's board met to approve it and release the money. She had PEN 4,000,000 — Peruvian soles, written S/ in the unit's accounts — of portfolio investment for the cycle, and a board instruction to hold blended contribution margin at 32% while she rebuilt the catalog. The two corporate proposals were due in June.

## The Protagonist

Talavera, 51, had directed executive education at Quillca-Lima since 2023. She had come from the buying side: six years running learning and development at a Lima bank, where she had commissioned programs from three business schools and cancelled two of them. Her mandate on arrival had been growth, and she had delivered it by widening the open catalog — two of the functional flagships now filling at 62% were courses she had launched herself, taught by affiliated faculty who had backed her appointment. Her budget, her headcount and the dean's patience all moved with the numbers the unit reported each cycle: contribution margin, fill rate, learner advocacy, the investment budget committed and the custom revenue signed.

## The Decision

Talavera had to decide what Quillca-Lima would sell, how each program would be delivered, and who would build the content it did not have — and to keep deciding through the cycle as the board, the two clients and a competitor answered back.

## The Company and its Setting

Quillca-Lima ran two businesses from one office. Open enrollment sold short courses seat by seat to individual executives and to companies buying places; custom, in-company work was designed for a single client, carried higher margin and produced stickier relationships. Annual revenue was PEN 28,000,000, and 68% of it came from the open catalog — functional topics, delivered face-to-face in Lima.

That concentration had become the unit's exposure. Global online providers were teaching comparable functional content at roughly a third of Quillca-Lima's price, on demand and without a fixed schedule. Corporate buyers had shifted from generic seminars toward custom programs whose effect on the job could be measured. And demand had tilted toward data and artificial intelligence (AI), sustainability and digital leadership. Three of the unit's largest clients had asked for one of those in the past year and been told Quillca-Lima had nothing. The school had faculty depth in finance, marketing and leadership, and thin coverage in everything the clients were now asking for (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Situation

Four measures framed the argument inside the unit (see **Exhibit 1**). Blended contribution margin — the share of revenue left after the direct costs of running a program, taken across open and custom work — stood at 26% against the board's 32% target. Open-program margin had been 38%; course leaders chasing enrollment had been cutting prices 15% to 20% and had brought it to 26%. Portfolio fill rate, the seats sold as a share of the seats put on sale across the whole catalog, was 64%, with the flagships at 62% and the open break-even at 80%. Learner net promoter score (NPS) — the share of participants who would recommend a program minus the share who would warn a colleague away, on a scale from −100 to +100 — had fallen from +42 to +28; word of mouth was the unit's cheapest source of enrollment. The fourth was the PEN 4,000,000 of portfolio investment, of which nothing had yet been committed.

The two RFPs were worth PEN 6,000,000 of combined annual spend and had been written to be answered inside the current budget cycle. Grupo Antara, a mining and energy group, wanted a digital-leadership program for its plant and commercial leadership; Banco Surandino wanted a sustainability program for its credit and risk teams, and had put measurable learning outcomes into the terms as a condition of award (see **Exhibit 2**). Neither client had run a program with Quillca-Lima in the disputed topics, because there had been none to run.

Inside the unit the argument had settled into two positions, neither of which turned on the numbers. Several course leaders wanted the discount deepened and held until fill recovered; the academic director wanted prices held and the catalog defended, on the grounds that the room, the cohort and the school's name were what a premium fee bought. Both had support among the affiliated faculty, whose teaching days depended on which programs survived.

If the catalog went to the board unchanged, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The flagships would run at 62% against an 80% break-even; the discount would hold margin under the 32% the board had set; learner advocacy would carry on down from +28; and the PEN 6,000,000 would be awarded in June to whoever had a digital-leadership and a sustainability program to bid.

## What Talavera Controlled

Talavera held four sets of decisions across the cycle, and the options on each had already reached her desk.

First, the diagnosis. She had to state how the catalog would be sorted, what she believed demand was doing, whether the open courses would be discounted — an 18% cut across the catalog, an 8% promotional price on the weakest courses only, or no cut at all — and how much of the term to spend reading the portfolio before committing to a redesign, from a quick pass to an exhaustive one.

Second, the catalog itself. She had to fix the topic mix, assign a delivery mode to what she kept, choose whether the missing content in artificial intelligence and sustainability would be developed in-house, licensed from a global partner, or co-delivered with an outside firm, and commit a tranche of the budget: PEN 1,200,000, PEN 2,400,000 or the full PEN 4,000,000.

Third, the bids and the launch. She had to price the two custom proposals — at a 24% margin, at the board's 32% hurdle, or at a 42% premium — answer the global provider that had just undercut the online functional programs, decide what the contracts would promise about measuring outcomes, and sequence the launches against the faculty and partner capacity available.

Fourth, the board. She had to state a portfolio philosophy in one line, answer the question the dean had already told her was coming — why partner away content and margin rather than build everything in-house — commit to a standing way of tracking demand or decline to, and choose how firmly to put the argument, from hedged to unequivocal.

Her progress would be read off the same four measures plus one: how much of the PEN 6,000,000 of anchor spend she had actually signed.

## Closing

At 8:20 that morning Talavera printed the spring reconciliation and the two RFPs and laid them side by side on the meeting table. The board would approve a catalog and release a budget at the end of the cycle; the proposals were due in June; the discount her course leaders wanted was already drafted. She had PEN 4,000,000 and one cycle in which to spend it.
