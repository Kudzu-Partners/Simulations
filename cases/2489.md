# Merrow Valley Bancorp: The Stress Test

**Case code:** FIN-2026-2489 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2489 — *The Stress Test* · **v1.0 · September 2026**
**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday 14 February 2028, a little after seven, Maya Lindqvist sat in the CFO's office in Columbus and read the scenarios the Federal Reserve had published the previous week. In the severely adverse one, unemployment rose to about 10% and commercial real estate prices fell by about 40%. Merrow Valley's capital plan was due at the Fed on Wednesday 5 April. Before then she had to decide how the bank would project its own losses, because everything else in the plan would rest on that projection.

She would make eight decisions in the year, two at each of four meetings: on 14 February, the stress scenario and the loss projection; on 27 March, the dividend and the buybacks in the capital plan; on 26 June, the answer to the Fed's results; and on 13 November, the distributions and the balance sheet for the months to June 2029 (see Exhibit 2).

## The Protagonist

Lindqvist, 44, had joined Merrow Valley in January as Chief Financial Officer from a larger bank holding company, where she had run capital planning for six years. She did not yet know which of Merrow Valley's numbers she could trust.

## The Decision

The file on the 2026 cycle had been on her desk since her first week. That year the bank's own projection had implied the lowest buffer the rules allowed, 2.5%; the Fed, running its own models on the same balance sheet, had set 3.4%. The capital plan had also drawn a Matter Requiring Attention from the supervisors: the bank's own stress scenario had not stressed its office book. Thomas Harrigan, the CEO, had since told investors, at the November 2027 investor day, that Merrow Valley would raise its dividend by 20% and buy back $1.6 billion of stock over the next year. Each decision of 2028 would be taken on its date and could not be revisited.

## The Company and its Setting

Merrow Valley Bancorp was the holding company of Merrow Valley Bank, N.A., a regional bank with branches in Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky, Pennsylvania and Michigan. At 31 December 2027 it had $118 billion of assets, $78 billion of loans and $94 billion of deposits (see Exhibit 1). With more than $100 billion of assets it submitted a capital plan to the Fed every year and was put through the Fed's supervisory stress test every other year; 2028 was a test year.

The rule that mattered was simple to state. The bank had to keep its Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio, common equity divided by risk-weighted assets, above a requirement: a 4.5% minimum plus a stress capital buffer that the Fed computed with its own models. The buffer was the fall in the CET1 ratio the Fed projected under the severely adverse scenario, plus a year of the bank's planned common dividends as a share of its risk-weighted assets, and never less than 2.5%. The Fed would publish each firm's buffer in late June; it would apply from 1 October. Below the requirement, the rules limited how much a bank could pay out.

## The Situation

Merrow Valley's CET1 ratio stood at 10.27%: $9.45 billion of CET1 capital against $92 billion of risk-weighted assets. The bank planned $1.38 billion of net income for 2028. It paid $0.30 a share each quarter on 500 million shares, $600 million a year, and its last buyback program had run at $800 million; the first half of 2028 was already committed, with $300 million of dividends and $400 million of buybacks.

The loan book held $7.5 billion of office loans, much of it refinancing in 2028 and 2029. Run as they were, the bank's loss models, estimated on 2010–2019 data, projected 7% office losses over the nine quarters of the Fed's scenario. Priya Raman, who ran model risk management, had written in the margin of the draft that the office model had never seen an office downturn. If Merrow Valley ran the 2028 cycle as it had run 2026 — the Fed's scenario through the models as they were, a plan sized on the bank's own projection, the plan kept as submitted, the gap with the Fed put down to the Fed's conservatism, and loan growth and buybacks continuing through the year — the plan would be built on that 7%.

## What Lindqvist Controlled

On 14 February she would choose the scenario the plan was built on: a milder in-house downturn as the planning scenario; a consultant's generic severe scenario, from Harlow & Pierce Advisory, for $1.2 million; the Fed's severely adverse scenario only, as in the last cycle; or the Fed's scenario plus the bank's own scenario built on its office book and its uninsured deposits, at $2 million of internal work. And how the office losses were projected: a $300 million management overlay agreed in her office; a 25% cut in the modelled losses, on Frank DeLuca's argument that "our borrowers are better than the market"; a documented overlay from a loan-by-loan review of the office book, independently validated, for $1.5 million; or the loss models as they were.

On 27 March, with the board meeting, she would set the quarterly dividend from the third quarter: $0.33 a share, 10% more; $0.36, the 20% Harrigan had promised; $0.27, a 10% cut; or $0.30, unchanged. And the buybacks from July 2028 to June 2029: none; $1.6 billion, as promised; $400 million; or $800 million, the last program's pace.

When the Fed published its results on 26 June, the bank would have two business days to adjust planned distributions and fifteen days to ask for its buffer to be reconsidered. Lindqvist would choose between keeping the distributions as submitted; keeping the buybacks and issuing $500 million of noncumulative perpetual preferred stock at 7.25%; requesting reconsideration and pausing buybacks until the answer; or resizing the buybacks to keep a 1.5-point management buffer over the requirement at every quarter-end of the plan, a cut that would be announced. And what to do about any gap between the bank's models and the Fed's: redevelop the office model with independent validation, for $12 million over nine months; a challenger model and the Fed's published loss rates, with overlays reviewed quarterly by model risk management, for $1.5 million; set an overlay each year so that the bank's losses matched the Fed's; or nothing, since the Fed's models were conservative by design.

On 13 November, with the year's results in view, she would set the distributions for the fourth quarter and the first half of 2029: pause buybacks and keep the dividend; accelerate, with $500 million of extra buybacks in the fourth quarter; halve the dividend and stop buybacks; or continue the buybacks and the dividend. And the balance sheet to June 2029: sell $2 billion of office loans at 85 cents on the dollar, a $300 million loss; keep growing loans to budget, $1.0 billion more of risk-weighted assets by year-end and $2.0 billion more by June 2029; a synthetic risk transfer on $6 billion of prime auto and mortgage loans, taking $3.6 billion off risk-weighted assets for $15 million of cost in the fourth quarter and $18 million in the first half of 2029; or freeze loan growth.

After each meeting, Eleanor Voss, who chaired the board's risk committee, would see the bank's position in seven figures: the stress capital buffer, the bank's own estimate until June and the Fed's after; the CET1 low point, the lowest ratio projected at the end of 2028 or in June 2029; the headroom over the requirement, in percentage points; the capital returned to shareholders from July 2028 to June 2029, net of the market's reaction to the bank's announcements and to thin buffers; from June, the projection gap, the difference between the fall in capital the Fed projected and the one the bank had projected; the supervisory findings the process drew; and the year's net income. With all of it, Lindqvist would receive a score out of 100 after each meeting.

## Closing

At half past seven Raman knocked on the open door with a printout. "The office model, run on the Fed's scenario," she said. "Seven percent. I can tell you what the model thinks. I can't tell you it's right." Harrigan's investor-day slides were already on the agenda for 27 March. Lindqvist read the 7% again, then turned to the 2026 numbers.
