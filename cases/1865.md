# The Key Account Gambit: Liant Composants' Portfolio Defence

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1865 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1865 — *The Key Account Gambit* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of 11 June 2026, Élise Renard, six weeks into the job of Commercial Director at Liant Composants, opened an email from Aérospatiale Garonne. Aérospatiale, an aerospace-components OEM accounting for 18% of Liant's EUR 72 million in annual revenue, was demanding a 9% price cut and a dedicated on-site engineer. If Liant refused, Aérospatiale would move 40% of its volume — roughly EUR 5.2 million a year — to a German competitor before the quarter closed. Renard had until then to answer, and redesign the commercial model behind it, inside a EUR 1.5 million budget the board had told her could not grow this year.

## The Protagonist

Élise Renard, 44, had spent twelve years running national accounts for a Lyon-based adhesives distributor before Liant's board recruited her in May 2026, after her predecessor left amid frustration over flat margins on steady revenue. She had been hired to professionalise a commercial function built one relationship at a time, with no system underneath it. Her mandate: hold the portfolio, lift margin, without new headcount or budget. She reported to the founder-CEO, with a bonus tied to gross margin, not revenue.

## The Decision

Over the coming quarter Renard had to make four linked calls: how to read the 180-account book, which lens showed where money was made and lost; how to reallocate her account managers' time and redesign service levels inside the fixed budget; which AI module to switch on and how to answer the ultimatum without losing the account or the margin funding the team; and how to lock the new model in so it outlived her. At quarter's end she would report four measures against her starting point: profit after cost-to-serve, strategic and mid-tier accounts still held, top-account spend captured, and how lean the cost-to-serve had become.

## The Company and its Setting

Liant Composants, founded in 1994 near Lyon, made and distributed specialty bolts, rivets, adhesives and engineered clips — customers ranging from aerospace OEMs to small fabricators. It employed 310 people, 24 in commercial roles, six strategic account managers covering 180 active accounts (see **Exhibit 1**). 2025 revenue had been EUR 72 million at 28% gross margin but concentrated: the ten largest accounts produced 62% of it. Liant had recently licensed a business AI suite it lacked the data and headcount to run in full.

## The Situation

Aérospatiale Garonne was the book's anchor: an aerospace-supplier OEM worth EUR 13 million a year, 18% of revenue, now the source of the ultimatum. Before answering, Renard had to read the portfolio. Three lenses were on offer: rank the 180 accounts by revenue size, ordering them from largest to smallest, so the biggest customers led the list; segment the whole book by value and potential together, weighing current profitability net of cost-to-serve against each account's room to grow; or rank them by the gross margin each account already booked, reflecting how the book's profit was currently distributed.

She also had to decide how far to take the cost-to-serve question itself: build cost-to-serve out across every segment, small accounts included, covering the whole book at once; skip the question entirely, on the view that revenue per account already told her enough to act on; or estimate cost-to-serve for the ten largest accounts only, and treat that as sufficient for now.

The early numbers pointed the same way, however she reached them. Her six account managers spent 55% of their time on the anchor and the two accounts next to it (see **Exhibit 2**). Twenty-two mid-tier accounts, worth EUR 9.5 million and high-margin, got little attention despite room to grow. Forty small accounts, each worth around EUR 25,000 a year, together cost Liant an estimated EUR 1.1 million more than they returned each year, because every account — the EUR 13 million OEM and the small fabricator alike — received the same service package. Nobody had measured share-of-wallet at the top accounts; a rough benchmark put it near 35%.

Renard also had to diagnose in one line where effort was mis-spent: conclude the current split was justified, since the anchor accounts still generated the most revenue in the book; conclude the effort was concentrated on three accounts, leaving the mid-tier with less attention and the small accounts still costing more than they returned; or conclude the answer was to drop the forty small accounts from the book, freeing capacity for the accounts that could actually grow.

If nothing changed — same effort split, same uniform tier, whatever she told Aérospatiale — the EUR 1.1 million leak would recur yearly, the mid-tier's headroom would stay unclaimed, and the EUR 5.2 million at risk would remain at risk.

## What Renard Controlled

Renard would set the split of her six account managers' time across four groups: the anchor and its two neighbours, the mid-tier accounts, the profitable long tail, and the small accounts, summing to full capacity.

Service tiers came next. For the forty small accounts: keep them on the same premium, high-touch tier as every other customer, on the principle that all accounts deserved equal treatment; move them to a middle growth tier, a partial trim that split the difference on cost and attention; or move them to a low-cost, self-serve tier built for transactional relationships rather than dedicated account management. For the twenty-two mid-tier accounts: fund a growth tier for them, with proactive account management aimed at their headroom; put them on the same transactional tier already used for the smallest customers; or give them the same full strategic-partner package the anchor account received, at the same cost.

On the AI suite: commit to churn prediction alone, to flag accounts at risk of leaving before they gave formal notice; or switch on all three modules — churn prediction, account scoring, next-best-action — splitting the same data and budget three ways; or commit to account scoring alone, to re-segment the full 180-account book by value and potential together; or commit to next-best-action alone, to guide cross-selling prompts inside accounts Liant already held. Each module needed an estimated EUR 90,000 of data cleanup to run well; running all three split that cost three ways.

Answering the ultimatum offered five paths: match the 9% cut and extend it across the rest of the book, to keep pricing uniform across the portfolio; give Aérospatiale the full 9% cut plus the free engineer, no conditions attached, to keep the whole account; offer a targeted discount tied to a multi-year volume commitment, selling the engineer separately as a paid service; hold price and make a value case instead, built on total cost of ownership and reliability, engineer available only as a paid add-on; or accept a partial loss, letting Aérospatiale dual-source the 40% it wanted, keep the profitable 60%, and redeploy the engineer elsewhere.

To replace whatever revenue was at risk, she could concentrate on raising share-of-wallet inside the top accounts held, where an estimated 65% of spend went elsewhere; chase new logos, prospecting for entirely new customers to replace the volume; or split effort between some new-logo pursuit and deeper cross-selling into accounts already held.

Finally, Renard had to set a share-of-wallet target for the top accounts, from the current 35% up to 80%, and choose an operating rhythm: keep no formal rhythm at all, revisiting the portfolio only whenever something next broke; keep the annual revenue review Liant had always run, unchanged, without adding anything new; or set a quarterly review built around value-and-potential segmentation, with explicit tier rules and AI usage written in. She also had to name the account or segment she would deliberately under-serve: none, holding every one of the 180 at full service; the forty small accounts, consciously moved to self-serve since their cost exceeded their value; or a high-potential mid-tier account, pulling effort back from its growth to save cost — and state a firmness score, 0 to 100, for how hard she would hold the new model against the pushback it would attract.

## Closing

By mid-morning, Renard had the account list, the budget line and Aérospatiale's ultimatum on her screen, and a board expecting numbers before the quarter closed. Six account managers, a budget that would not grow, and one large customer were waiting to hear what she would do.
