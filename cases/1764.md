# Banca Aurelia: The Yield Curve Call

On the morning of 11 June 2026, Marta Oderzo opened the overnight risk report on her second monitor before she had finished her coffee. A hot Eurozone inflation print had just reset rate expectations: two-year yields were up 90 basis points since the previous close, ten-year yields up 60. The curve had not shifted — it had bent. Somewhere in the EUR 9.0 billion securities portfolio she managed, that difference mattered more than the headline number.

Within the ten trading days the shock had opened, Banca Aurelia's investment committee had to approve a diagnosis of the damage, a written risk mandate, and a reallocation of the portfolio — all without letting the bank's capital ratio drift toward the level that would force a filing with the regulator. Oderzo would bring the first draft of all three to the committee table.

## The Protagonist

Marta Oderzo, 41, was Head of Portfolio Management at Banca Aurelia S.p.A., a post she had held for three years after eight running a fixed-income desk at a larger Milan-based group. She sat on the investment committee alongside the Chief Risk Officer, the ALM & Treasury Lead, an equity strategist, and the bank's Fixed-Income & Capital Officer, and it was her job to bring the committee a number they could vote on rather than a debate they could have forever. She had built the current 20% equity / 80% bond book over eighteen months of a calm rate environment. Nobody on the committee had run this book through a shock this sharp.

## The Decision

Oderzo's task ran in stages. First, she had to characterize the damage: how severe the duration exposure really was, what shape the curve move had actually taken, and how to treat the losses sitting on bonds held at amortized cost, which do not appear on the balance sheet until the bonds are sold. Second, she had to propose a risk mandate — explicit limits the reallocated portfolio would have to live inside for the rest of the shock. Third, working inside that mandate, she had to reweight the EUR 9.0 billion across equities, short-duration bonds, long-duration bonds, and cash, and decide how to fund a possible outflow of rate-sensitive deposits. A further shock and a deposit outflow were both risks Oderzo had to weigh, and whatever mandate she wrote would have to hold up regardless of what came next.

## The Company and its Setting

Banca Aurelia S.p.A., founded in 1923 and headquartered in Turin, served retail and SME clients across northern Italy through 210 branches. The bank carried EUR 38.0 billion in total assets and EUR 24.0 billion in customer deposits, and had reported 2025 net profit of EUR 312 million on a CET1 ratio of 14.2% — comfortably above the 11.0% regulatory floor (see Exhibit 1). The EUR 9.0 billion securities portfolio Oderzo ran was not the bank's core lending book; it was Aurelia's liquidity and earnings buffer, invested across equities and bonds, and a meaningful share of the bond side was booked at amortized cost rather than fair value — a classification that had never been tested by a move this size.

## The Situation

The numbers on Oderzo's screen were specific. The bond book carried an effective duration of 5.8 years; against a shift of this magnitude, that pointed to roughly a 4% mark-to-market hit, an unrealized loss near EUR 290 million. The 20% equity sleeve, worth about EUR 1.8 billion, had fallen 8%, a further mark near EUR 144 million. Management's early estimate was that the shock alone would trim CET1 by 70 to 90 basis points — enough to narrow, though not by itself close, the gap to the 11.0% floor. A separate EUR 180 million of losses sat unrecognized on the amortized-cost book; those would become real only if the bonds were sold. And EUR 1.5 billion of deposits were rate-sensitive enough that some could migrate toward higher-yielding accounts elsewhere, which meant the committee also had a liquidity problem sitting on top of a capital one. If Oderzo changed nothing — held the 20/80 split, wrote no mandate, raised no cash — the shock would still pass through the existing book and the committee would face the ten-day deadline with a CET1 ratio it had not chosen and a deposit outflow it had not funded.

## What Oderzo Controlled

On the diagnosis, Oderzo had to characterize three things separately. On duration risk, she could call the move manageable — a shift of this size within a range the book could absorb without urgency — call it severe, putting the duration-times-move arithmetic at roughly a 4% hit and near EUR 290 million unrealized, or call it catastrophic and argue for an immediate wholesale sale to stop further loss. On the shape of the curve, she could treat it as a parallel move that a ten-year hedge would cover, as a bear-flattening in which the short end had moved further and carried the open exposure, or as a bear-steepening in which the long end had taken the damage. On the amortized-cost book, she could leave the unrecognized losses out of the capital walk since they were invisible until a sale triggered them, or flag the roughly EUR 180 million now, on the reasoning that it would become real capital the moment the book was tapped to fund an outflow.

For the mandate, four limits needed a number each, and each offered a narrower or a wider setting. The duration ceiling could sit at 3.5 years, a defensive posture that kept the book short and liquid; at 4.5 years, a moderate ceiling that still left room to capture yield; or at 6.0 years, keeping the long book intact and letting it pull back toward par. The equity cap could hold at 15%, trimming the sleeve well below its current weight; at 20%, holding the sleeve at its current weight; or at 30%, allowing a further rotation into equities. The drawdown ceiling could run at 4%, a tight budget with little room for a bad quarter; at 6%, a balanced ceiling between caution and flexibility; or at 9%, a wide ceiling that gave the reallocation more room to run. And the CET1 floor the mandate would defend could be set at 11.0%, the bare regulatory minimum; at 12.0%, the board's own internal trigger; or at 13.0%, an extra-conservative cushion above both.

The reallocation itself split the EUR 9.0 billion across four buckets (see Exhibit 2). Equities carried the existing EUR 1.8 billion position and an 8% mark the shock had already put on it. Short-duration bonds, roughly two years out, carried little of the rate move's remaining sting and offered a lower yield. Long-duration bonds, roughly eight years out, had taken most of the shock's mark already and now carried the richest yield. Cash and overnight paper earned little, but it was the one bucket that could fund the deposits that might walk. Tied to that weighting sat a funding choice: raise the liquidity from the fair-valued short book instead, leaving the amortized-cost losses deferred rather than realized, or sell into the amortized-cost book directly and crystallize the roughly EUR 180 million of hidden loss into capital immediately. And a thesis had to accompany the move — set the weights on feel, without stating an expected-return view at all; back a re-rating in equities and overweight that sleeve on the strength of it; argue that bond yields were the highest they had been in a decade and allocate to capture them; or hold the increase in cash and wait for clearer signals before taking any of it back on.

Later committee cycles would return Oderzo to the same four buckets as conditions evolved, with a governance choice layered on top: hold the mandate exactly as written, de-risk further by cutting duration and equity and raising cash beyond the mandate's own floor, or rebuild duration if the market allowed it.

## Closing

Oderzo pulled up the portfolio screen and the blank mandate template side by side. Three diagnosis fields, four mandate limits, four allocation weights, a funding choice, and a thesis to state in writing — all before the committee reconvened at nine. The coffee had gone cold.
