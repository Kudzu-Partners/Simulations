## Abstract

Moda Dinámica, a twenty-store Mexican mid-market women's fashion retailer with 2025 revenue of MXN 720M (approximately USD 36M), approved an MXN 18M, four-season transformation budget in February 2026 to introduce a machine-learning-based demand-forecasting and inventory-optimization system. The retailer's pre-program metrics were unambiguously below segment benchmark: forecast accuracy (Weighted Absolute Forecast Accuracy, or WAFA) was nominally tracked but not measured at the SKU-store-week level; gross margin was 42%; stockout rate was 18%; markdown rate was 28%. The CEO had committed in the December 2025 board meeting to a markdown rate below 16% and a gross margin above 46% by the end of the fourth season under the new system. Andrea Cortés, the newly appointed Director of Analytics, owned the program's three Round-1 dials — ML forecast investment, trend-item allocation share, and markdown trigger week — and the eight further decisions that would unfold across Spring, Summer, Fall, and Winter. The case opens at 6:42 a.m. on Friday, March 27, 2026, six business days before the Spring buy-and-allocate window closes at the Mexico City distribution center.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the trade-off between ML forecast investment level and the marginal accuracy improvement at three investment tiers.
2. Diagnose the consequences of a higher trend-item allocation share on stockout, markdown, and gross-margin outcomes under different store-cluster signals.
3. Analyze the markdown trigger week as a working-capital and full-price-sell-through lever, and recommend a default trigger and an exception protocol.
4. Design a four-season retraining cadence that uses Season N data to update the Season N+1 model under a constrained data-engineering budget.
5. Recommend a Season-1 dial package and identify the random event (weather, currency, traffic) most likely to require an inter-season override.

On the morning of **Friday, March 27, 2026**, **Andrea Cortés** stood at the long table in the Moda Dinámica buying office on Avenida Insurgentes Sur in Mexico City and looked, for the fifth time, at the Spring 2026 store-cluster forecast that the new gradient-boosting model had returned twenty-two minutes earlier. The model — trained on three years of point-of-sale data, two years of weather observations, and a small set of trend signals — projected a +40% spring demand for pastel-category items in the Guadalajara store, against a flat or slightly negative projection in three of the four Monterrey stores. The Spring buy-and-allocate window closed at 5:00 p.m. on Friday, April 3. She had until then to commit the buy quantity, the cluster allocation, and the markdown protocol that would govern the next ninety-one days.

---

## The Protagonist

Andrea Cortés, 39, had been Director of Analytics at Moda Dinámica since November 2025. A graduate of ITESM in Monterrey with a master's degree in operations research from Universidad de los Andes in Colombia, she had spent the prior twelve years at three companies: four years as a demand planner at a Mexican consumer-electronics retailer, four years as senior analyst at a Latin American grocery chain (where she had owned the migration to a probabilistic forecasting system), and four years as Director of Forecasting at an Argentine apparel retailer where she had — privately — been responsible for the post-mortem on the 2024 Patagonia-line buy that had produced a 38% markdown rate. Moda Dinámica had recruited her on the strength of that post-mortem, which had been published internally as a 14-page document on what to never do again.

---

## The Decision

Cortés had to commit, by 5:00 p.m. on Friday, April 3, the Season-1 dial package the system would treat as her Round-1 input:

1. **ML Forecast Investment** — three tiers: MXN 1.6M (logistic regression baseline), MXN 3.2M (gradient boosting on POS plus weather), MXN 5.0M (ensemble with deep learning on trend signals).
2. **Trend Item Allocation** — the percentage of the Spring buy designated as trend-driven (the risky half) versus carryover and basic (the safe half), in the range 25%–55%.
3. **Markdown Trigger Week** — the week of the season at which automatic markdown logic would fire on items below sell-through threshold, between week 4 and week 12 (default week 10).

Eight further decisions would follow across the four seasons. Summer (Round 2) would face an automatic-reallocation policy decision. Fall (Round 3) would face an economic-shock override decision. Winter (Round 4) would face the auto-replenishment-engine investment decision and the size-pack optimization decision. Each Season would feed retrained data into the next Season's model.

## Company Background

Moda Dinámica had been founded in 2008 as a single-store women's fashion concept in Polanco and had expanded across Mexico to twenty stores by 2024 — five in Mexico City, four in Monterrey, three in Guadalajara, two in Puebla, two in Tijuana, two in Querétaro, and two in Cancún. The 2025 revenue was MXN 720M with a gross margin of 42% (a segment benchmark would be 48%), an inventory turn of 3.1x (benchmark 4.5x), and a markdown rate of 28% (benchmark 18%). The company carried four collections per year — Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter — with a roughly USD 8M Spring buy and an average of 2,400 SKUs per season across women's apparel and accessories.

The pre-program operating model was buyer-led: the head buyer for each category — Tops, Bottoms, Dresses, Outerwear, Accessories — set the buy quantity and the store allocation based on prior-season actuals plus a 5%–15% growth assumption, with no formal forecasting model. Inventory transfers between stores were ad hoc. Markdowns were triggered by store-level manager discretion within a corporate-set price-band policy. The CEO had, in his December 2025 board memo, described the analytics transformation as "the difference between three retailers in our segment that survive 2027 and the seven that do not."

## Industry and Market Context

The Mexican mid-market apparel retail segment in 2026 was characterized by three structural pressures the program was designed to address. First, fast-fashion competition from international entrants (Zara, H&M, Shein) had compressed full-price sell-through for non-trend items by 8–14 percentage points across the segment over 2022–2025, mechanically increasing markdown dependence. Second, store-cluster heterogeneity had increased: the Guadalajara and Cancún customer bases had begun, around 2023, to diverge from the Mexico City base in trend adoption (Guadalajara faster on color, Cancún faster on warm-weather silhouettes), creating an opportunity for store-level allocation that the inherited buyer-led model could not capture. Third, climate variability — measured as deviation from the prior-decade seasonal averages — had increased materially, with Cancún summers averaging 1.8°C above the prior decade and producing measurable shifts in lightweight-fabric demand timing.

Empirically, the post-2018 retail-analytics literature had converged on three findings. First, gradient-boosting models trained on three years of POS plus weather typically lifted SKU-store-week WAFA by 18–25 percentage points over a logistic-regression baseline; the marginal lift from adding deep-learning ensembles on trend signals was an additional 4–6 percentage points at roughly 1.5x the cost. Second, trend-item allocation above 50% raised gross-margin variance materially without raising expected gross margin; the optimal range for mid-market retailers was 35%–48%. Third, markdown trigger weeks earlier than week 6 protected gross margin but reduced full-price sell-through; markdown trigger weeks later than week 10 grew end-of-season inventory liability and produced the steeper Q4 markdowns the segment was famous for.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The CEO had commissioned the analytics-program scoping in September 2025 with a small external consultancy, which had produced the MXN 18M, four-season investment plan in November and the gradient-boosting model architecture in January 2026. Cortés had been recruited in November to lead execution. Her first two months had been spent on data integration: cleaning the POS history, sourcing the weather data, and building the SKU-store-week training set. The Spring forecast that morning was the first model output the system had produced on a live buying decision.

### What Cortés knew

The internal team had modeled three Season-1 dial packages. A Conservative Package (MXN 1.6M investment, 35% trend allocation, week-10 markdown trigger) projected end-of-Spring WAFA of 64%, gross margin 43%, markdown 26%. A Mid Package (MXN 3.2M investment, 45% trend allocation, week-6 markdown trigger) projected WAFA 73%, gross margin 44%, markdown 24%. An Aggressive Package (MXN 5.0M investment, 50% trend allocation, week-4 markdown trigger) projected WAFA 78%, gross margin 45%, markdown 22%. The MXN 18M four-season budget supported the Mid Package across all four seasons or the Aggressive Package across two.

### What she did not know

Whether the head buyer for Tops, Eduardo Pérez, would accept the model's +40% Guadalajara pastel signal or override it on intuition; whether the Cancún store managers, who had asked for a wider trend allocation, would accept a corporate-set 45% boundary; whether the Spring weather pattern would track the model's training data or produce the weather event the model could not yet anticipate; and whether the Round 2 (Summer) auto-reallocation policy decision could be deferred to Round 3 if Spring underperformed.

### What others were saying

**Eduardo Pérez**, Head Buyer, Tops: *"The model says forty percent on Guadalajara pastel. I have bought this category for nine years. It is not forty percent. It is twenty-five. I want a manual override on the Guadalajara cluster."*

**Sandra Hernández**, Chief Operating Officer: *"Pick the Mid Package. Spend MXN three-point-two now. Hold the rest for the auto-replenishment engine in Winter. The replenishment engine is the program's actual unlock."*

**Roberto Quiñonez**, Chief Executive Officer: *"I do not need an Aggressive Spring. I need a Markdown rate that lets me sleep. Show me a Spring that ends with markdown below twenty-five and I will give you the Summer budget."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Cortés had narrowed the space to three Season-1 packages.

- **Option A — Conservative (Logistic Baseline):** MXN 1.6M, 35% trend allocation, week-10 markdown trigger. WAFA 64%, gross margin 43%, markdown 26%, end-Spring profit MXN 1.4M. Lowest investment; minimal head-buyer disruption; weakest Cancún signal capture.

- **Option B — Mid (Gradient Boosting):** MXN 3.2M, 45% trend allocation, week-6 markdown trigger. WAFA 73%, gross margin 44%, markdown 24%, end-Spring profit MXN 2.1M. Sustainable across all four seasons; requires the Pérez Tops override or escalation.

- **Option C — Aggressive (Ensemble):** MXN 5.0M, 50% trend allocation, week-4 markdown trigger. WAFA 78%, gross margin 45%, markdown 22%, end-Spring profit MXN 2.4M. Highest first-season impact; depletes the four-season budget by Season 2.

Cortés's private view, not yet shared with the COO, was that Option B was the right Season-1 package and that the real four-season unlock was the Round-4 auto-replenishment-engine investment, which only Option B preserved budget for. The disagreement with the head buyer was the harder of the two conversations she had on her calendar for Friday morning.

At 7:48 a.m., Cortés printed the model's Spring forecast, the three-option summary, and a one-page memo titled *"Why Eduardo's twenty-five and the model's forty are both wrong, and the answer is thirty-six."* The Tops buyer arrived at 8:00 a.m. The Spring buy-and-allocate window closed at 5:00 p.m. the following Friday.
