# Formación Digital Inclusiva: The Missing Third

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-540 · **Companion simulation:** Express 540 — *Social Enterprise — FDI Turnaround* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, April 6, 2026, at 8:10 in the morning, Alejandra Gómez Tovar read the letter a second time. The United States Agency for International Development (USAID) was reducing the youth-employment grant that funded her organisation, effective April 1. For Formación Digital Inclusiva (FDI), a Bogotá non-profit that trained young people from low-income neighbourhoods as software developers, data analysts and designers, the cut came to COP 1,400 million for 2026, about a third of its budget. The 2026 cohort of 580 students had started classes on March 1.

Gómez, FDI's executive director, had until the board meeting on Friday, April 10, to decide how many of those 580 students FDI would carry through the year, and which placement figure it would put in front of its funders. The same week, an investigative journalist had asked in writing how FDI arrived at the 72% placement rate on its website.

## The Protagonist

Gómez was forty-one. She had co-founded FDI in 2019 after twelve years running technical training programmes in the private sector, and had led it ever since. Her board, three founders and two external members, trusted her and rarely pushed back. She had raised money in good years and in lean ones. She had never cut a cohort.

## The Decision

Gómez faced four decision points over the rest of 2026. In April: the size of the cohort and the placement number FDI would stand behind. Next: a new funding model, meaning how FDI's income would be spread across its five sources, how far to go with a paid-bootcamp offer from a commercial bank, and what minimum share of places FDI would guarantee to young people from estratos 1 to 3, the lowest strata in Colombia's socio-economic classification of households. Then three conversations: with her programmes director, who had an outside offer; with the journalist; and with a foundation that had made its renewal conditional on governance reform. Finally, the funding mix FDI would lock in for 2027, whether to set aside an emergency reserve, and whether to invest in rigorous impact measurement.

## The Company and its Setting

FDI trained young people aged 17 to 24 in ten-month intensive programmes at sites in Bogotá (Ciudad Bolívar and Usme), Medellín (Comuna 13) and Cali (Aguablanca). It had 28 full-time staff and 45 part-time instructors, and had graduated 1,820 students since 2019. Graduates in work earned on average COP 2.4 million a month, against roughly COP 900,000 for comparable young people without the programme. The 2025 budget had been COP 4,200 million.

Most of the money came from others. In 2025, 38% of FDI's income had come from competitive grants, from USAID and the Inter-American Development Bank; 22% from corporate social-responsibility donations; 18% from paid training alliances with companies; 12% from placement fees that employers paid when they hired a graduate; and 10% from individual donors and local government (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The cut had landed on the largest of the five streams. FDI held COP 400 million in cash reserves. Keeping all 580 students to December would cost more each quarter than FDI was bringing in, and the reserves would shrink every quarter until the income side changed. Cutting the cohort would save money; it would also mean sending home young people who were already enrolled.

The placement figure was the second problem. FDI's headline rate counted any job, including temporary freelance work. An audit that counted only formal employment held for six months or more put the rate at 52%: still well above the neighbourhood baseline, but twenty points below what FDI had been reporting. The journalist, who wrote for an independent investigative outlet, had arrived at the same question about method and was preparing a story.

Other pressures were already on Gómez's desk. A large commercial bank had offered to run a paid bootcamp with FDI for students from higher-income strata, worth up to COP 1,800 million a year, with cross-subsidised places for FDI's own students. The board was split on it: some members saw the money, others saw an organisation that would stop being what it said it was. María Fernanda Ruiz, the programmes director who had designed the curriculum, had an offer from a technology company at 2.25 times her salary. And a private foundation that gave FDI COP 600 million a year had written that it would renew for 2027 only if the board gained a majority of independent members, an annual external audit and a stronger impact-reporting method. The reform would cost COP 85 million.

FDI's development team tracked two further numbers on its dashboard. Credibility with funders, an index from 0 to 100, stood at 55. Mission integrity, the share of places held by students from estratos 1 to 3, stood at 90%.

## What Gómez Controlled

In April she would settle two things. How many students to keep in the 2026 cohort: any number from 200 to all 580. And which placement rate FDI would report to funders: the audited 52%, or the 72% it had always published.

At the second decision point she would rebuild the funding model. She would set the share of income FDI would pursue from each of the five streams, totalling 100%: competitive grants, corporate donations, training alliances, placement fees, and individual and government support. Her development team's working draft put them at 24%, 22%, 20%, 18% and 16%. She would set how far FDI would lean into the bank's paid bootcamp, on a scale from 0, none, to 100, all in; the draft said 40. More bootcamp meant more cash and a smaller share of places for the students FDI existed to serve. And she would fix a mission guardrail, the minimum share of students from estratos 1 to 3 that FDI publicly committed to keep, anywhere between 40% and 90%; the draft said 65%.

At the third, three conversations. Whether to retain Ruiz with a package built on purpose plus a 15% raise, at a cost of COP 40 million. Whether to answer the journalist transparently, owning the metric and inviting an external audit. And whether to fund the governance reform and external audit the foundation required, at COP 85 million.

At the fourth, the funding mix FDI would lock in for 2027 across the same five streams, where the team's draft read 22%, 22%, 20%, 20% and 16%; whether to set aside an emergency fund equal to three months of operations; and whether to spend COP 60 million on a rigorous impact-measurement system that funders could audit.

After each decision point Gómez would see the quarter's income, cost and net result in millions of pesos, the cash reserves, credibility with funders, mission integrity, the students enrolled, and job placements for the quarter and in total (see Exhibit 2). The board chair had told her that the year would be judged on money and mission together, not on either one alone.

## Closing

At 8:40 the journalist's email was still open on her screen: five questions, and a request for answers by Thursday. Below it, Ruiz had sent a calendar invitation for Friday afternoon with no subject line. Gómez opened a blank spreadsheet, typed 580 in the first cell, and looked at it.
