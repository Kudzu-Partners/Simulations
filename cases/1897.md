# Esmaltec Sul: The Quality Fix and the Delivery Constraint

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1897 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1897 — *Forum for Innovation* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Wednesday, 18 March 2026, Lorena Bittencourt read the CasaForte letter twice and carried it down to the plant floor in Joinville. It ran to one page. It recorded the third batch of enamelled cooktop panels returned since November — pinholes and edge chipping, rejected at CasaForte's inbound inspection — and set a condition. Esmaltec Sul had two quarters to bring the defect rate on CasaForte's lines below 2.0%. Above that line, CasaForte would dual-source half of what it bought.

CasaForte was 24% of Esmaltec's revenue: R$173 million out of R$720 million. Half of that was R$86.5 million. Bittencourt had until the end of September to answer the letter. The construction season, the four months that carried most of the year's volume, began in three weeks.

## The Protagonist

Lorena Bittencourt, 46, had been VP Operations at Esmaltec Sul since 2022. A materials engineer, she had joined in 2011 as a process engineer and run firing and finishing before taking the plant. Since 2023 she had sat on the steering group of the Innovation Forum, a consortium of manufacturers and university researchers Esmaltec had joined for help with this problem; the Forum had circulated a study that winter on how member firms tied development projects to quality outcomes, and the board had asked her to use it. Her bonus moved with plant EBITDA and with on-time delivery to the ten largest accounts.

## The Decision

Bittencourt had to choose what Esmaltec would fund inside the capital the board had authorised, decide what the plant would be measured on, and sequence the work so that the fix did not consume the volume it existed to protect.

## The Company and its Setting

Esmaltec Sul S.A. had been making enamelled steel and ceramic-coated products in Joinville, Santa Catarina, since 1982: cooktop panels, sink units, water-heater tanks and architectural cladding, sold to retail chains, construction distributors and appliance manufacturers. In 2025 it turned over R$720 million at a 12.5% EBITDA margin — R$90 million — and employed 1,150 people at a single plant with four production lines. The plant finished roughly 9,000 units a day against a ceiling close to 2.1 million units a year (see **Exhibit 1**).

That ceiling was not a planning figure: from April to July the lines ran above ninety per cent of it, and anything that stopped a line stopped orders already sold.

## The Situation

Four numbers described where the plant stood.

The **defect rate** — the share of finished units rejected at final inspection — was 4.2%. The best plants in the category ran near 1.5%.

The **cost of quality** — everything the plant spent because the work was not right the first time, counting scrap, rework, returns, warranty claims and the inspection hours that caught them — ran at 9.0% of revenue, or R$64.8 million a year. That was more than two-thirds of EBITDA. Bringing it to the 5% treated as good practice would release about R$28.8 million a year.

**CasaForte delivery** stood at 70 on a 0–100 index the commercial team kept, measuring how much of that customer's peak-season order book Esmaltec shipped complete and on time.

The Forum's rating of Esmaltec's **development pipeline** — the revenue funded work would eventually carry, and the capability the plant would own at the end of it — stood at 25 out of 100, the lowest of the eleven member firms.

Cristiane Maes, the quality director, had the twelve-month reject record ranked by category (see **Exhibit 2**), and the room did not agree about what it said. Maes read the enamel-application station as equipment at the end of its life, throwing a family of related defects that tightening would not remove. Hélio Brandão, who ran the plant and the delivery schedule, read the same table as variation: settings that drifted between shifts on lines that were fundamentally sound. Odete Fontana, who had supervised finishing for nineteen years, put the rejects on the operators and wanted retraining: her best shift ran the same equipment at half the reject rate. Wagner Toledo, the process engineer who kept the plant's data, held that no category in the table was large enough to carry the others, and that hunting one cause would burn the quarter.

Three projects had been costed, and the board had authorised R$22 million for the year.

The **application-system replacement** would put electrostatic application with in-line vision inspection on the CasaForte lines: R$19 million, six months to install, with the line down while it happened — about 180,000 units of output, most of it inside the season. The supplier projected 1.6% on treated lines, from installations on comparable but not identical substrate, and the contract provided for a validation run on the first converted line before the rest followed.

The **premium coating extension** would add a scratch-resistant nano-coating line at R$14 million, opening a price segment Esmaltec did not sell into. It added a coating step and a second set of process parameters to lines already running close to capacity.

The **improvement programme** would put R$4.5 million into structured variation reduction on the existing lines: station-by-station measurement, standard settings written and enforced, and continuous monitoring to hold whatever was gained. It took no line down and could start in April. Quality engineering's own projection put the floor it could reach near 2.8%.

Five packages had been priced: the application system with the improvement programme; the application system alone; the coating extension with the improvement programme; the improvement programme alone; and a partial slice of all three. The first came to R$23.5 million against an authorisation of R$22 million, which meant either the board moved or the package was trimmed. She was not obliged to spend the whole envelope.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was not in dispute: the defect rate stayed at 4.2%, CasaForte moved half its volume — R$86.5 million — the cost of quality kept running at R$64.8 million a year, and the two other large accounts already asking about the returns carried a further R$120 million.

## What Bittencourt Controlled

Bittencourt committed the plant to one reading of the reject data, and set how much of her engineers' time went into building that diagnosis first. She chose how to put the money to the board — as an investment against the R$64.8 million the plant was already losing, as a capital expense to be argued down to what the year could bear, or as a separate ledger, the project resting on the CasaForte deadline alone — and how hard to press whichever argument she chose. She chose the package, and how much of the R$22 million she actually committed to it.

She set what the plant would be measured on: the defect rate by itself, which was the number in CasaForte's letter; the defect rate together with the share of units that came through without rework, the time a unit took to cross the plant, and the cost of quality; or units shipped. She split engineering hours and floor time across the CasaForte lines, the other lines, and the control work that held a gain in place once a line had improved. And she set how much of the improvement to take from 100% manual re-inspection at the end of the line, which caught defects before they left the plant and consumed inspection hours and line time.

She sequenced the install: line by line, with three lines still feeding CasaForte; all the affected lines at once, to finish fastest; or after the season, with the two-quarter clock already run down. She covered the lost units with overtime and low-complexity volume put out to a contract finisher, absorbed the shortfall, or went back to CasaForte for a phased target. If the validation run on the first converted line came back below projection, she could diagnose it and re-set the parameters, carry the rollout forward on the original schedule, or stop the conversion and fall back to the improvement programme. And she decided how firmly to defend the plan at the board's quality committee.

## Closing

Bittencourt took the reject record down to line 3 and stood at the enamel-application station until the shift changed. Two quarters, R$22 million, one page from CasaForte, and a table that four people in her own plant read four different ways.
