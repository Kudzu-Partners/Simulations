# Wasatch Executive Learning: The Summit Grid Bid

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1919 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1919 — *Custom Build* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, March 2, 2026, Corinne Ballard read Summit Grid Utilities' request for proposal at her desk in Salt Lake City and then read the two pages her client engagement manager had clipped behind it. The request asked for leadership training for 220 mid-level operations managers who were being handed a grid-modernisation and decarbonisation programme. The clipped pages were extracts from Summit Grid's own engagement survey, run four months earlier: managers reported not knowing who was entitled to decide what, and that work crossing two functions stalled.

Ballard had eighteen days to put a signed programme blueprint in front of Summit Grid's board and ninety days to have the first cohort in a room. Two rival providers were writing against the same brief. The budget was US$1.8 million and Summit Grid's chief operating officer, Ray Ostler, had said on the kick-off call that the figure was set by his board and would not move. Whoever won became preferred provider under a three-year master agreement worth roughly US$6 million.

## The Protagonist

Corinne Ballard, 45, had run Wasatch Executive Learning for four years, after nine years designing and selling custom programmes for a corporate university on the East Coast. She had been hired to grow the custom side of the unit, and had done it by refusing to quote anything the unit could not later evidence. Wasatch's custom contracts committed the unit, in writing, to demonstrating behaviour change on the job and movement in a business measure the client named, six to twelve months after the last cohort finished. That commitment carried the unit's premium pricing. It also meant that a programme aimed at the wrong problem became visible to the client, in a document with Ballard's name on it, roughly a year after the invoice cleared.

## The Decision

Ballard had to decide what Wasatch would put in front of Summit Grid's board on day eighteen, and whether to put anything there at all.

She was not choosing between finished proposals but making a sequence of commitments, each narrowing the next: what the capability gap was, what the programme would consist of, who would teach it and at what cost, and what she would say when the numbers were challenged.

## The Company and its Setting

Wasatch Executive Learning was the custom and open-enrolment executive-education unit of a top-thirty U.S. business school, operating as its own profit-and-loss unit with annual revenue of US$14.2 million. It could draw on 35 internal teaching academics and a vetted external bench of 25 practitioner-facilitators, who billed at 1.5 times internal day-rates.

Summit Grid Utilities was a US$6.4 billion regional energy company. Its 220 operations managers sat between a corporate transition strategy and the crews who would execute it.

## The Situation

The arithmetic of the request was tight before any design existed (see **Exhibit 1**). US$1.8 million across 220 participants was about US$8,180 a head, roughly 26% below what Wasatch normally charged for a blended custom programme. Two of the unit's 35 academics had genuine depth in the energy transition, and both were committed to other programmes through the second quarter. The external bench could cover the shortfall at 1.5 times the internal rate.

Wasatch scored every bid on the same four-line sheet before it left the building, and Ballard's sheet on March 2 was mostly empty. Needs alignment — whether the design fitted the gap the evidence showed rather than the gap the client had named — stood at 18 out of 100, because nothing had been designed. Impact credibility — whether the measures Wasatch signed could be defended when someone audited them — stood at 15 out of 100. Feasibility — whether the thing could be built, staffed and signed inside the eighteen and ninety days — stood at 40 out of 100. The fourth line, contribution margin, stayed blank until an engagement profit-and-loss existed; the unit's floor was 22%. Beneath the four sat a running total: engagement cost committed, US$0.00 million against the US$1.80 million Summit Grid would pay.

If Ballard let the eighteen days run out, none of those numbers moved. Summit Grid's board would take an off-the-shelf programme from one of the two rivals, and the three-year agreement would go with it.

## What Ballard Controlled

Ballard's worksheet held four blocks of decisions, and she had to close each block before the next made sense (see **Exhibit 2**).

The first block was the diagnosis. The same evidence supported four readings of the gap: unclear decision rights and weak coordination across functions; a general shortfall in leadership skills; a technical shortfall in the modernisation work itself; or a shortfall in motivation. She then had to say how much of whatever she named a programme could move — all of it, none of it, or part of it with the remainder handed back to Summit Grid as its own work. She had to fix the success measures Wasatch would sign: observed on-the-job behaviour change plus a named operational number such as cycle time or outage response, at six to twelve months; end-of-course satisfaction and a knowledge test; or a general undertaking to improve leadership capability with no measure attached. And she had to set how much of the eighteen days went into interrogating the survey evidence before anyone drew a module, from none to all of it.

The second block was the architecture. For 220 managers the format could be five campus days for everyone, live-virtual throughout, or a blend of a live-virtual core with targeted in-person intensives and digital pre-work. What carried the learning back to the job could be application projects on the live grid programme with manager coaching and spaced reinforcement; a reflective assignment marked by faculty; or the workshops alone. The 220 could go through in one wave or as a pilot cohort followed by the rest. The journey could run anywhere from three to twelve modules.

The third block was staffing and money. The energy-transition content could be anchored by the two internal academics on the modules that carried the school's name with the external bench filling around them; taught by the external bench throughout; or covered by pulling both academics off their second-quarter commitments. One cost lever was available: reuse validated content assets and move volume onto the live-virtual core; drop the application projects and the coaching; or cover 120 of the 220 managers and bill the same. Design, platform and materials could absorb anywhere from nothing to US$500,000. And the bid itself was full-custom at the ceiling, a scaled version of the same diagnosis with leaner delivery, a decline, or a price-to-win bid staffed entirely from the external bench.

The fourth block was the room. Ballard expected Ostler to ask how she knew this moved operating performance rather than confidence. She could walk the chain from gap to transfer design to leading indicators to the dossier's operational number; point to Wasatch's record and the satisfaction scores its past programmes earned; or promise gains in confidence and engagement that Summit Grid's survey would pick up. She had to settle what she would say about delivery risk — faculty scarcity, mitigated by pre-contracting the bench and cross-training a third academic; adoption, mitigated by securing line-manager sponsorship and protected project time up front; or that the plan carried no material risk. She had to say something about the three-year agreement, or nothing: publish the dossier at six to twelve months and let it argue for preferred-provider status; discount hardest now and let volume earn the renewal; or leave renewal unraised. And she had to decide how firmly to hold whatever line she took when Ostler and Delia Prasad, Summit Grid's chief human resources officer, pushed back.

## Closing

Ballard put the survey extracts on top of the request for proposal and wrote the date on the corner of the folder. Summit Grid's board met on day eighteen; the first cohort was due ninety days after that. She had US$1.8 million to work inside, two academics who were not free, a margin floor of 22%, and a promise about measured behaviour change that Wasatch would publish under its own name. What she wrote in the next eighteen days would be the thing she had to defend, twice.
