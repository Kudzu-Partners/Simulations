## Abstract

Daniel Quesada, a newly appointed Country Manager for the Latin American subsidiary of a multinational consumer-health company, arrived in-country on March 1, 2026, with a dual brief from his Chief Executive Officer: grow the business and build a reputation the Board would trust with a larger region in three years. Six weeks later, on April 12, he sat at his desk with six files on a single corner of his whiteboard — labeled *Permit, River, Whistleblower, Database, Plant Closure, Board* — and a note from his Chair reading *"Your portfolio of decisions is your real CV."* The first file was live that week. A mid-level regulatory official had indicated, through a consultant, that a USD 25,000 "facilitation payment" would unlock a pending plant-operating permit on which USD 4M per month of revenue depended. The Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the OECD anti-bribery convention applied. Three scores — Ethical Coherence, Stakeholder Awareness, and Leadership Reputation, each starting at 50 — would be tracked across six decisions. The case ends at the end of Day 42.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Apply a rule-consequence-virtue triangulation to a facilitation-payment decision under a cross-jurisdictional anti-bribery regime.
2. Evaluate the portfolio effect of six sequential ethical decisions on a country manager's external reputation and internal coherence.
3. Analyze the stakeholder-awareness dimension of a decision — whom the decision is actually visible to, on what timeline, and with what asymmetry of information.
4. Diagnose the difference between a decision that maximizes the current period's KPIs and a decision that compounds into period 6 and beyond.
5. Recommend a defensible handling for Scenario 1 and a single sentence of framing that the country manager would use to govern the remaining five scenarios.

On the afternoon of **Sunday, April 12, 2026**, **Daniel Quesada** sat in the empty country-manager office and read, for the third time, the two-page memo his Director of Regulatory Affairs had emailed him at 11:14 a.m. Saturday. The memo described the conversation with the consultant who had met with a Director General at the environmental agency on Thursday, and summarized the facilitation-payment request: USD 25,000, routed through an intermediary, against release of the permit his company had been expecting since February 21. The memo ended with one sentence: *"If we refuse, the permit goes back into the queue and the queue is four months."*

---

## The Protagonist

Daniel Quesada, 41, had been appointed Country Manager on February 15, 2026. He had spent eighteen years at the company: five years as a brand manager in Central America, four years as a commercial director in the Andean region, three years running the Mexican affiliate's consumer-health portfolio, and six years as Vice President of Commercial Operations for the Latin American region before this appointment. He held an undergraduate degree in industrial engineering and an MBA from INCAE. The Board had, in his appointment letter, named three "larger regions" that could be offered in 2029 conditional on a successful three-year track record in-country. One of those regions was Southeast Asia.

---

## The Decision

Quesada had to decide, by Wednesday April 15, how to handle Scenario 1 — the facilitation-payment request — and, in the same week's Executive Committee, to signal the framing that would govern the five further decisions he already knew were coming.

1. **Pay it** through the consultant, accept the permit, and treat the USD 25,000 as a one-time cost. The permit arrives within ten days; USD 4M per month of revenue resumes; the company is in violation of the FCPA and the OECD convention; the consultant is a witness.
2. **Refuse and escalate** to the Director General's superior and to Legal at headquarters, accept the four-month queue, lose approximately USD 16M of revenue, and manage the resulting product-availability communication with trade customers.
3. **Meet the senior official directly**, without intermediary, to understand whether the request reflects a policy gap that can be resolved through a formal, on-the-record compliance process rather than a payment.
4. **Let the local general manager handle it** without Quesada's formal knowledge — preserving deniability for Quesada and the Board while the problem is resolved locally.

Five further decisions — each distinct, each with its own stakeholder set — would follow: a broken effluent sensor on a plant discharging into a 3,000-person riverside community (Scenario 2); an anonymous whistleblower report of procurement kickbacks at 15-20% by the company's best operational manager (Scenario 3); a USD 2.3M revenue opportunity tied to the purchase of 800,000 consumer records potentially non-compliant with Brazil's LGPD (Scenario 4); a headquarters decision to relocate a 22-year-old factory to Vietnam in 14 months, costing 340 direct and 1,200 indirect jobs (Scenario 5); and an audit-committee meeting at which the Chair would request Quesada's "honest assessment" across all four (Scenario 6).

---

## Company Background

The multinational — referred to internally as *the Group* — was a consumer-health company with 2025 revenue of USD 22B across five regions and 38,000 employees. The Latin American region had 2025 revenue of USD 2.8B across twelve country affiliates. Quesada's country affiliate was the fourth-largest by revenue in the region, with USD 280M in 2025 and a 22-year manufacturing footprint. The Group had, since 2018, adopted a public *Code of Conduct* that prohibited facilitation payments in all jurisdictions, regardless of local legal treatment, and had an internal *Speak Up* platform run by a third-party vendor that reported directly to the Group's Chief Ethics Officer.

The affiliate Quesada inherited employed 1,240 people directly, 3,800 indirectly, and operated two plants — the 22-year plant in Scenario 5 and a newer plant that was the subject of Scenario 2. The product portfolio skewed 62% to over-the-counter consumer health and 38% to prescription. The affiliate's commercial organization had been a strong performer for the region over the last three years, with a compounded annual revenue growth rate of 11% and an operating margin 180 basis points above the regional average. The last external ethics audit, in 2024, had flagged two items: a facilitation-payment policy that was "clear in writing and inconsistently enforced," and a procurement function whose concentration in three suppliers carried a "monitoring risk."

## Industry and Market Context

The Latin American consumer-health sector entered 2026 under four simultaneous pressures. First, cross-jurisdictional anti-bribery enforcement had intensified, with the U.S. Department of Justice and the UK Serious Fraud Office jointly pursuing two consumer-health cases in 2024–2025 and reaching disclosure-linked settlements that materially reduced penalty exposure for companies that self-reported. Second, environmental-compliance expectations at the industrial-plant level had tightened after a 2024 regional incident involving an undisclosed effluent breach that had produced a EUR 42M settlement for a peer. Third, data-protection regimes — notably Brazil's LGPD — had matured to the point where fines for unauthorized data acquisition had exceeded, in two 2025 cases, the first-year revenue from the data itself. Fourth, activist labor organizations had begun to coordinate across borders on plant-closure announcements, compressing the window between headquarters decision and community response from weeks to days.

Empirically, multinational affiliates that had, in the 2019–2025 window, handled a facilitation-payment request by refusal-and-escalation had seen a median 90-day revenue disruption of 4.8% and a 12-month revenue disruption of 1.1%. The disruption was recoverable. The reputational and compliance costs of the payment path, when discovered, were not.

---

## The Situation

### How we got here

Quesada had inherited an affiliate with two live risk items (the facilitation request and the broken effluent sensor), one dormant item (the procurement whistleblower complaint, filed anonymously on April 2 and still unassigned), and two incoming items (the database opportunity, proposed by his Head of Digital in the April 10 commercial committee; and the plant-closure decision, which headquarters had pre-announced to him on March 20 as "under regional strategic review"). The Chair of the audit committee had requested a full executive-summary write-up by the April 30 committee meeting.

### What Quesada knew

The internal compliance team had assessed the facilitation-payment request as "unambiguous" under both the Group's Code of Conduct and under the FCPA. The Group Legal team was aware and had, informally, recommended refusal-and-escalation. The Director of Regulatory Affairs had modeled the four-month queue scenario and estimated USD 16M of revenue at risk, partially recoverable through trade-customer communication. The consultant had indicated — privately, to the Director of Regulatory Affairs — that a second Director General "had a different style" and that a formal, on-the-record meeting with that official could surface a legitimate regulatory pathway.

### What he did not know

Whether the facilitation request reflected a single actor or a wider pattern within the regulatory agency; whether the consultant would, if refused, report the refusal back to the requesting official in a way that compromised future regulatory interactions; and whether the Board, if the decision were escalated, would support refusal-and-escalation in principle but push back in private on the 90-day revenue impact.

### What others were saying

**Ana Lucía Mendoza**, Director of Regulatory Affairs, argued for a **formal meeting with the second Director General**: "Do not pay. Do not refuse yet. Meet the official with the counsel present. There is a legitimate pathway. Find it before you accept the queue."

**Roberto Salas**, Commercial Director, argued for **paying through the consultant**: "USD 25,000 against USD 16M. It is not a real dilemma. Pay, get the permit, and move on. The Board will forgive revenue protection. They will not forgive revenue loss."

**Inês Carvalho**, General Counsel, argued for **refusal-and-escalation to headquarters**: "Pay this and the next one is USD 100,000. Escalate. Take the revenue hit. This is the easiest of the six you have coming."

---

## Options Under Consideration

Quesada had narrowed the space to three concrete handlings.

- **Option A — Refuse and Escalate (the Carvalho plan):** Refuse the facilitation payment, escalate to Group Legal and Ethics, accept the four-month queue, model the USD 16M revenue impact transparently, manage trade-customer communication proactively. Coherence +6. Stakeholder Awareness +4. Reputation +5 inside the Group, uncertain externally.

- **Option B — Formal Meeting with the Senior Official (the Mendoza plan):** Request an on-the-record meeting with the second Director General, attend with counsel, do not pay the intermediary, do not refuse the permit, seek a formal regulatory pathway. Coherence +3. Stakeholder Awareness +6. Reputation +4. Risk: the meeting does not materialize, the queue fires, and the decision collapses into Option A with 30 days lost.

- **Option C — Pay Through the Consultant (the Salas plan):** Pay the USD 25,000, accept the permit, treat the cost as legitimate "consulting expense." Coherence −15. Stakeholder Awareness −8. Reputation +2 in the short term, materially negative if discovered (probability of discovery over three years estimated by the internal compliance team at 28%). Five further decisions lose their moral authority as a portfolio.

Quesada's own instinct, not yet shared with any of the three advocates, was that the real decision was not Option A versus Option B but the single sentence he would say at Wednesday's executive committee to govern the five scenarios that had not yet opened.

(See **Exhibit 3** for the Group Code of Conduct facilitation-payment section and **Exhibit 4** for the six-scenario score grid, pre-portfolio.)

---

He closed the memo, wrote one sentence at the top of his whiteboard — *"Coherence is what the record shows the third time someone asks"* — and went home. The executive committee was at 8:00 a.m. Wednesday. The audit committee was eighteen days away.
