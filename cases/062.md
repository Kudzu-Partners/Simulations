## Abstract

Meridian Financial Group was, in the case universe, a mid-market LATAM financial-services group headquartered in Bogotá with retail and commercial-banking operations in Colombia, Peru, and Chile, 4,000 employees, 2025 group revenue of USD 640M, and a pipeline audit the new Chief People Officer received on day one that showed women at twelve-point-three-percent annual promotion rates against men at seventeen-point-eight, despite women outperforming men on performance ratings across all three geographies. On January 6, 2026, Valentina Rodríguez, appointed Chief People Officer on December 2, 2025, received the Ernst-and-Young pipeline audit that the outgoing CPO had commissioned in August. Six rounds would unfold across three years, beginning with a Round-1 intervention-selection decision inside a USD 1.5M Round-1 envelope and ending with a three-year Board business case in June 2028 that would determine whether the program continued, scaled, or was dismantled. The case opens at 7:20 a.m. on Monday, January 12, 2026, as Rodríguez prepares the Round-1 package for the February 4 Human Capital Committee.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the six Round-1 talent-equity interventions — promotion-calibration panels, hiring-rubric panels, women's network, sponsorship architecture, unconscious-bias training, exit interviews — and diagnose where structural interventions dominate supply-side.
2. Analyze the adjusted pay-gap pattern across Colombia (9.2%), Peru (11.7%), and Chile (7.4%) and design a remediation that anticipates the union-leak risk.
3. Diagnose the Director-of-Commercial-Banking-Colombia succession and recommend a candidate and a structural change to the succession-slate rule.
4. Design a flexible-work architecture — non-transferable paternity, calibration committees, leader modelling — that addresses the twenty-eight-percent promotion penalty on flex users.
5. Recommend a complete Round-1 package and identify the single Round-5 resistance response that consolidates the Round-6 Board business case.

On the morning of **Monday, January 12, 2026**, **Valentina Rodríguez** sat at the CPO's desk on the eighteenth floor of the Meridian Financial Group headquarters on Avenida Chile in Bogotá and opened the pipeline-audit deck her predecessor had delivered six days earlier. The deck was eighty-seven pages. The single most cited page — page forty-four — showed the promotion-rate differential. The single most quoted sentence — in the Executive Committee's December review — was: "The gap is structural. The interventions the company ran between 2022 and 2025 were supply-side."

---

## The Protagonist

Valentina Rodríguez, 47, had joined Meridian on December 2, 2025 after eight years as Chief People Officer of a Chilean insurance group, where she had led, between 2020 and 2023, a pay-equity remediation program that had closed a 7.8-percent adjusted gap to 2.1 percent in three years. A graduate of Universidad de los Andes in Bogotá with an MBA from IESE Barcelona, she had, in her prior role, co-authored a two-year longitudinal study on sponsorship-versus-mentorship in LATAM financial services that had been cited in the August 2025 Meridian pipeline audit. The CEO's onboarding instruction had been: "Build a pipeline the Board can defend to its ESG-aware investors — and the business can defend to its customers."

---

## The Decision

Rodríguez had to commit, by 9:00 a.m. on Wednesday, February 4, the Round-1 intervention-selection package the Human Capital Committee would treat as the three-year operating envelope:

1. **Intervention Selection** — six interventions on the menu, Round-1 envelope USD 1.5M, typical-implementation pricing of USD 180K–540K per intervention: promotion-calibration panels, hiring-rubric-and-panel overhaul, women's employee-resource network, sponsorship architecture, unconscious-bias training, structured exit interviews.
2. **Pay-Equity-Remediation Posture** — phased versus immediate remediation of the adjusted 9.2 / 11.7 / 7.4 gap, with union-leak risk in the Colombian bank.
3. **Human-Capital-Committee Escalation Package** — accompanying memo, three-year roadmap, and quarterly scorecard.

The three sub-decisions would interact and would compound across six rounds. A Round-1 package that funded promotion-calibration panels, hiring-rubric panels, sponsorship architecture, and structured exit interviews — with unconscious-bias training deprioritized and the women's network funded at the lower price point — produced, on the internal model, a Round-2 pay-equity path that preserved union trust through immediate remediation plus published methodology, and a Round-6 Board business-case outcome with women-at-Manager at thirty-four percent (up from twenty-nine) and the three most-equity-advanced units outperforming by 8.3 percent.

---

## Company Background

Meridian Financial Group had been founded in 1998 through the consolidation of two Colombian regional banks and had, across twenty-eight years, expanded across Peru (2006 acquisition) and Chile (2014 greenfield). 2025 group revenue was USD 640M, net income USD 128M, and return on equity 14.2%. The 4,000-employee workforce was distributed as 2,280 in Colombia, 1,120 in Peru, and 600 in Chile; of the 480 Managers and above, 141 were women (twenty-nine percent); of the thirty-four senior officers in the sponsor-eligible population, nine had at any point been identified as a sponsor of a woman in a director-level succession slate.

The pipeline audit, delivered in August 2025 by a tier-1 consultancy, had found: (a) women promoted at twelve-point-three percent against men at seventeen-point-eight; (b) women outperformed men on performance ratings in each of the three geographies; (c) the raw gender pay gap was thirty-one percent; the adjusted gap (same role, same level, same tenure) was Colombia 9.2%, Peru 11.7%, Chile 7.4%; (d) women were thirty-one percent of telework users against men twenty-three percent; (e) men took 4.2 days of ten entitled paternity days; (f) flex-users were twenty-eight percent less likely to be promoted in the following cycle.

## Industry and Market Context

The LATAM women-in-leadership landscape in 2026 was characterized by three structural features the Round-1 decision sat inside. First, two of Meridian's three direct competitors had, between 2023 and 2025, published externally audited gender-equity reports and had, in the 2024 and 2025 campus-recruiting cycles, outperformed Meridian on women-candidate acceptance rates by 8 and 14 percentage points respectively. Second, the Colombian banking-sector union had, in the November 2025 collective-bargaining round, flagged pay-equity audits as a 2026 priority; a leaked Meridian adjusted-gap report would almost certainly appear in the union's March 2026 press cycle. Third, the proxy-advisor ISS had, in its September 2025 LATAM voting-guidelines update, introduced a new category on board-oversight of gender-pay-equity disclosure.

Empirically, the DEI research had two robust findings the Round-1 package had to respect. First, structural interventions — promotion-calibration panels and hiring-rubric panels — had, across a 2021–2025 sample of forty-eight Global-2000 financial-services companies, produced measured promotion-rate-gap closure of 3.8 percentage points on average within twenty-four months, against 0.6 points for supply-side interventions such as women's networks and 0.2 points for unconscious-bias training. Second, immediate-remediation-plus-published-methodology had, across a 2020–2024 sample of twenty-two European and LATAM pay-equity programs, produced union-trust-score movement averaging plus-eighteen points in the six-month window after remediation, against minus-nine points for phased-remediation-without-disclosure programs.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The Board had, in the September 2025 off-site, reviewed the pipeline audit and had, on October 14, instructed the Nominating and Human Capital Committee to commission a CPO replacement search with "build the pipeline" as the single written mandate. Rodríguez had been identified in the second-round interview cycle in November and had accepted on December 2. Her first week had been a round of one-on-ones with the CEO, the CFO, the heads of Colombia, Peru, and Chile, and the outgoing CPO. The February 4 Human Capital Committee meeting was the first scheduled decision window.

### What Rodríguez knew

The internal team had modeled three Round-1 packages. A Minimum-Viable Package (unconscious-bias training, women's network, exit interviews; pay-equity phased; USD 780K) projected Round-6 women-at-Manager at thirty-one percent, adjusted-pay-gap at 5.4%, Board business-case outcome: "Continue under review." A Structural Package (promotion-calibration panels, hiring-rubric panels, sponsorship architecture, exit interviews; pay-equity immediate; USD 1.48M) projected Round-6 women-at-Manager at thirty-four percent, adjusted-pay-gap at 3.1%, Board business case: "Scale and embed." A Maximum-Intervention Package (all six interventions; pay-equity immediate; USD 1.92M against the USD 1.5M envelope) projected Round-6 women-at-Manager at thirty-four percent, adjusted-pay-gap at 3.0% — a two-tenths improvement over the Structural Package at forty percent higher cost.

### What she did not know

Whether the Colombian banking-sector union would leak the adjusted-pay-gap report in March 2026 (base-case timing on the internal-regulatory calendar) or hold it to the August collective-bargaining window; whether the Director of Commercial Banking Colombia would retire on the announced July 2026 date or accept a six-month extension as the CEO had signalled in the December board pre-read; whether the January 2027 anonymous letter from the eight senior commercial bankers the internal-culture team had placed at thirty-four-percent probability would materialize in the Round-5 window; and whether the ESG-focused institutional investors, who held eleven percent of Meridian's equity, would treat the Round-6 Board business case as the basis for a multi-year hold or a divestment trigger.

### What others were saying

**The Chief Financial Officer**: *"Phased remediation on pay equity. The immediate hit is USD 5.8M. The run-rate is USD 2.1M. Phase it across three years. The CFO is not a pay-equity skeptic. The CFO is a working-capital realist."*

**The General Counsel**: *"The legal exposure under the Colombian equal-pay law is unrecoverable if the adjusted gap shows up in a union filing before we have filed a remediation plan. Immediate remediation. Published methodology. Do not wait."*

**The Head of Colombia**: *"Promotion-calibration panels in the first six months. If we can show one calibration panel where a qualified woman was elevated over a more-visible man on the same rating, the culture signal writes itself."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Rodríguez had narrowed the space to three Round-1 packages.

- **Option A — Minimum-Viable:** Unconscious-bias training, women's network, exit interviews; pay-equity phased. USD 780K. Round-6 women-at-Manager 31%, adjusted-pay-gap 5.4%. Cheapest today. Structurally weak on promotion dynamics.

- **Option B — Structural Package:** Promotion-calibration panels, hiring-rubric panels, sponsorship architecture, exit interviews; pay-equity immediate. USD 1.48M. Round-6 women-at-Manager 34%, adjusted-pay-gap 3.1%. Aligns with the General Counsel and Head of Colombia positions.

- **Option C — Maximum-Intervention:** All six interventions; pay-equity immediate. USD 1.92M against USD 1.5M envelope. Round-6 women-at-Manager 34%, adjusted-pay-gap 3.0%. Two-tenths improvement at forty-percent higher cost.

Rodríguez's private view, not yet shared with the CFO, was that Option B was the right Round-1 package and that the single subsequent decision most correlated with the Round-6 Board business-case outcome was the Round-5 resistance response — specifically, the CEO public reaffirmation paired with an evidence-based promotion analysis showing every promoted woman matched or exceeded the credentials of the men who had been passed over.
