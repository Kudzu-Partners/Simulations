# Méridien Executive Education: The Program Everyone Loved

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1809 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1809 — *Architect the Leadership Journey* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Thursday, 5 February 2026, Isabelle Marchand read the post-program evaluation for "Leading Through Complexity" twice before she believed the second number. The six-month flagship leadership journey had just closed its latest cohort at 4.6 out of 5 on enjoyment. Behaviour-change measurement, drawn from 360-degree feedback and manager reports, told a different story: only 41 percent of participants had visibly changed how they led at work, against a target of 65 percent. Three sponsors funding €1.4 million of the program's revenue had put renewal on review, and one had said plainly that people loved the program and nothing had changed at work. A rival business school, meanwhile, was already winning renewals from the same client base with a more experiential design.

She had six weeks to bring a re-architected version of the journey back to the Dean and the lead sponsor, and a calendar that could not grow by a single day to build it in.

## The Protagonist

Isabelle Marchand was the academic director of "Leading Through Complexity" at Méridien Executive Education, the leadership-development faculty group inside a major European business school outside Paris. She had directed the program for years, building her standing on the same intellectual sharpness the evaluation had just scored. That same content was now the very thing sponsors were questioning. Redesigning it meant asking a faculty body that had built the current program to give up teaching days they still considered their own.

## The Decision

The redesign would run in four stages inside a single six-week window: diagnose why a well-liked program had changed so little behaviour; re-sequence the journey and decide how much of it should be experienced rather than taught; allocate the fixed contact days and faculty-days the redesign was allowed to spend; and present the new design to the Dean and the sponsors, and how she would prove it had worked. Nothing about the calendar could grow: the same six months, eighteen contact days and 144 faculty-days would only be re-allocated.

## The Company and its Setting

Méridien Executive Education ran roughly €19 million a year of open-enrolment and custom leadership programs, reaching about 1,400 participants annually through a faculty body of seventy senior professors, practitioners and coaches. "Leading Through Complexity" was its most prestigious offering: a six-month journey aimed at developing five specific leadership capacities in senior managers sent by its corporate clients — courageous decision-making, coaching others, cross-boundary influence, leading through ambiguity, and self-awareness. Sponsors paid a premium for the promise that participants would return changed, not merely informed.

That promise was now in question: a rival business school had shifted its own offering toward simulation, live coaching and workplace projects, and was winning business Méridien had assumed was secure.

## The Situation

The numbers underneath the 4.6 satisfaction score were what worried the sponsors (Exhibit 1). Transfer-to-work — the share of participants whose managers or 360 raters could see a real change in behaviour — stood at 41 percent against a target of 65. A parallel measure, uplift in 360-degree behaviour ratings, sat at +0.4 on a five-point scale against a target of +0.8. Across the five outcomes the program was meant to build, only 62 percent were adequately served, against a target of 80. Sponsors also tracked a separate engagement measure of energy and attendance, which stood at 72 against a floor of 60 below which they were expected to walk regardless of transfer — while the design itself still spent about 70 percent of its contact days on lecture and case-based theory. Pushing too far the other way carried its own risk: a more experiential pilot Méridien had once run scored only 3.2 out of 5 on enjoyment, and attendance had fallen. If nothing changed, the €1.4 million sponsor block would stay on review, and "Leading Through Complexity" would keep losing ground to the rival school on the same terms as today.

## What Marchand Controlled

In the first stage, she had to name what had actually gone wrong. Was it a knowledge gap, where participants needed content that went deeper, sharper and more current than what they already had? A training gap, where sound ideas had never been delivered well enough to land? An application gap, where the program had never built in spaced practice or a mapping from module to outcome? Or did the fault sit with the participants, senior managers unwilling to change habits no matter how the program around them was built? Alongside that diagnosis, she had to say which outcomes mattered most: all five treated as equally urgent, with no priority set among them; the behavioural outcomes, the practical capacities built through hands-on rehearsal; the cognitive outcomes, a fuller set of frameworks for leading through ambiguity; or self-awareness on its own, built through more psychometrics and structured reflection. She also had to gauge, as a percentage, how openly her team was willing to name this as a design problem rather than reach for more content.

In the second stage, she set what share of the contact days would shift toward experience, from 20 up to 95 percent; past roughly 80 sat the pilot that had once scored 3.2 out of 5. She chose how to sequence the three on-site intensives: front-load every concept session into the first intensive and hold projects to the end; drop conceptual framing altogether and run simulations and projects back-to-back across all three; or space input, practice and reflection across all three, ending on application and commitment. And she decided how thoroughly modules would be tied to the five outcomes: map every module to at least one outcome, with assessment aligned to behaviour; map only the headline modules and leave the rest as interesting but unconnected; or skip formal mapping altogether and trust faculty to cover the right ground on their own.

In the third stage she allocated the fixed faculty-day envelope (Exhibit 2). An action-learning project, advertised as worth fifteen transfer points, would cost twenty faculty-days: she could fund it in full, cutting masterclass time to cover the cost; run a lighter half-version at ten days for a partial effect; or cut it altogether and protect the content coverage and masterclasses faculty wanted to keep. Separately, she set how many faculty-days would be protected for coaching and reflection — work that produced no lecture material but rehearsed new behaviour — anywhere from none up to forty days, and what share of the remaining teaching days would go to senior professors rather than practitioners and coaches, who cost roughly twice as many faculty-days.

In the fourth stage she carried the finished design to the Dean and the lead sponsor. On measurement, she could commit to the same end-of-program survey that had already produced this year's satisfaction score; to a 3- and 6-month re-test — 360 feedback — plus leading indicators during the program; or to a dashboard shown once at launch, before attention moved fully to the next cohort. To the sponsor worried the program would not stay enjoyable enough to keep people attending, she could promise to soften the hardest projects if the score slipped; tell the sponsor that enjoyment no longer mattered next to transfer; or design stretch with energy — cohort cohesion and early wins to keep people engaged. To faculty who said their content had been cut, she could overrule the objection and push the redesign through on her own authority; show them the outcome mapping and transfer evidence and redeploy them into coaching and project work; or restore the lecture time they had lost, reverting toward the old design. Alongside all of it, she had to state, as a percentage, how firmly she was prepared to hold the line that changed behaviour mattered more than a happy sheet.

## Closing

By nine that morning, Marchand had the current journey map spread across her desk: eighteen contact days and 144 faculty-days already spoken for by a design that was loved and forgotten. In six weeks she would stand in front of the Dean and the lead sponsor with whatever she built instead. The calendar in front of her was not going to get any bigger.
