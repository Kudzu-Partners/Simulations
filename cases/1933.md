# Meridian Graduate School of Business: Three Proposals, Forty-Five Sections

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1933 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1933 — *Portfolio Architect* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

Three proposals had sat on Ileana Duarte's desk since the Dean dropped them there at the start of the week. By Thursday, March 5, 2026, she had added up the course sections in them. Each was a complete master's degree — costed, staffed, and cleared to admit its first class for the Fall 2026 intake. Together they required 78 course sections. After Meridian had committed the teaching it already owed to the programs it was running, 45 were free.

Duarte, Executive Director of Graduate Programs, had 90 days. At the end of them the Dean would carry a portfolio to the university's Board of Trustees, and Duarte would have to have settled which of the three degrees launched, at what cohort size, at what tuition, taught by which kind of faculty, and how the school's fixed US$1,200,000 enrollment-marketing budget was divided among them. The Provost had approved the launches on one condition: no new central money and no new faculty lines this year.

## The Protagonist

Ileana Duarte, 46, had run graduate programs at Meridian since 2022. She had spent eight years before that in the school's finance office, the last three of them rebuilding the model that assigned faculty cost to individual courses, and two years running graduate admissions between the two posts. Colleagues came to her for the section-cost table and for the admissions funnel, and she was the only administrator in the building who kept both in the same spreadsheet. Under the school's budget rules, her own operating money for the following year moved with the net contribution the graduate portfolio produced.

## The Decision

Duarte had to turn three approved proposals into one portfolio: which programs ran, how large, at what price, staffed by whom, carrying which share of a fixed marketing budget — and what she would tell the Board about anything she did not launch.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Graduate School of Business, founded in 1982, was the graduate division of a large public research university in Miami. It enrolled roughly 2,400 graduate students, employed 64 full-time faculty, and produced about US$78 million of gross tuition a year. It kept the net tuition it earned, paid for its own faculty, marketing, and staff out of that revenue, and returned an overhead assessment to the central university. Nothing arrived from elsewhere to cover a launch that did not fill.

What limited the school was neither classrooms nor applicants. It was teaching capacity. Every graduate course section had to be taught by someone, and the faculty roster was fixed for the year. A fully loaded section taught by full-time faculty cost the school about US$95,000; a section an adjunct was qualified to teach cost about US$32,000. Not every course could be handed to an adjunct — several of the proposals rested on senior faculty whose names were part of the reason applicants would pay the price.

The admissions arithmetic was equally fixed. Meridian had historically admitted about 55% of the people who applied to a graduate program, and about 38% of those it admitted had gone on to enroll.

## The Situation

The three proposals were not variations of one another (see **Exhibit 1**).

Program A, an MS in Business Analytics carrying the federal STEM designation, proposed 60 seats at US$52,000 — the highest tuition of the three — and required 22 sections, the smallest teaching claim of the three, though all of them fell to senior analytics faculty, of whom the school had few. Its projected class leaned on international applicants; a federal review of student-visa issuance had been running since January with nothing announced either way.

Program B, an MS in Health Administration delivered online and hybrid, proposed 90 seats at US$38,000 — the lowest tuition of the three — and required 30 sections, the largest teaching claim of the three, though many of them the curriculum committee had already marked adjunct-eligible. It addressed the largest market, working clinicians and hospital administrators, and the most contested; no senior name of the kind that had let Meridian hold price elsewhere was attached to it.

Program C, a boutique Master in Sustainable Finance taught in person, proposed 30 seats at US$46,000 — the smallest cohort of the three — and required 26 sections of senior full-time faculty, the second-largest teaching claim. Two trustees had asked for it by name and a donor had been circling it for a year, which made it the hardest of the three to set aside without explanation.

Filling those cohorts at Meridian's historical rates meant roughly 430 applications for Program B, 285 for Program A, and 140 for Program C — about 855 in all, against a marketing budget that had not moved in three years.

The Dean's office scored portfolio proposals on four indices, each running from 0 to 100. Portfolio margin measured how much contribution each scarce section produced. Capacity discipline measured whether the plan stayed inside 45 sections without taking teaching away from programs already running. Enrollment yield measured whether seats filled at a price the school could defend. Board readiness measured whether the decision survived a trustee's questions, including the ones about what could go wrong. Entering March the four stood at 20, 25, 15, and 10. Beside them the Dean's office kept two running totals: sections committed against 45, and marketing dollars committed against US$1,200,000 (see **Exhibit 2**).

If Duarte forwarded the three proposals as written, the arithmetic closed itself in one of two ways. Either 33 sections came out of the programs already teaching — most plausibly the flagship MBA, whose student-satisfaction scores and ranking submissions underwrote the school's reputation and much of its applicant flow — or three cohorts opened smaller than modeled, with faculty committed to them for two years and a launch too public to unwind quietly.

## What Duarte Controlled

She controlled the analysis first. She chose the single number she would rank the three programs on: contribution margin per scarce section, total contribution margin per program, tuition per student, or the size of each program's addressable market. She chose which program that number placed first. And she chose how much of the funnel and section-cost arithmetic she completed before ranking rather than after.

She then controlled the shape of the launch. She could run Health Administration and Business Analytics at their proposed sizes and hold Sustainable Finance for the Fall 2027 intake; run Business Analytics and Sustainable Finance and hold Health Administration; run all three with cohorts cut down until they fit; or run all three as proposed. She set what share of the adjunct-eligible sections went to adjuncts rather than to full-time faculty. And if her plan needed more than 45 sections, she chose where the remainder came from: adjunct overload, bought and recorded in the ledger; sections taken from the MBA; or nowhere, because the plan stayed at or under 45.

She controlled money and price. The US$1,200,000 had to be divided across the launched programs to the last dollar. Tuition could hold at the modeled figures, come down about 8% through targeted scholarship, rise about 15%, or be discounted about 20% — each of which moved both what a student contributed and the share of admitted students who chose to enroll.

And she controlled the presentation. She could frame the portfolio as a single decision — programs, scale, and margin per section — or as a growth case — tuition revenue, headcount, and applicant demand — or as a reputational one — rankings, donor interest, and a marquee STEM launch. She could name the program she had deferred and what deferring it cost the school, say that it would be revisited later, or present only what was being added. She could bring a stated contingency for a disruption to Program A's international intake — a domestic-applicant backstop, a deferred start, capacity moved to another program — say the school would watch and respond, or treat the international class as settled. And she chose how much of the pitch rested on numbers she had actually computed.

## Closing

Duarte cleared her desk of everything but three headings on a single sheet — seats, sections, dollars — and under them one figure: 45. The Board met in 90 days.
