# Huertos Velandria: Three Quarters of a Moving Peso

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1216 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1216 — *The Currency Game: Why a Strong Peso Hurts Exporters* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On the morning of Monday, April 13, 2026, Valeria Montiel-Arze read the front page of Costaria's largest daily in the back of a taxi. *El peso fuerte: Costaria recovers its dignity*, the headline ran, above a photograph of the Finance Minister outside the central bank. The driver, who had seen the paper on her lap, told her that imported goods were finally cheaper and that life was getting better. Montiel-Arze, chief executive of Huertos Velandria, S.A., a mango grower, packer and exporter, did not answer. In six months the Costarian peso had moved from CO$ 5.00 to CO$ 4.00 per US dollar, and almost everything her company sold was priced in dollars.

By 08:30 her chief financial officer, Tomás Echeverri-Luna, had put the arithmetic on one page. The anchor contract, USD 1,000,000 a quarter with a US importer, had converted to CO$ 5,000,000 at the old rate. It now converted to CO$ 4,000,000. The costs behind it had barely moved. A quarter that used to earn CO$ 325,000 would now lose CO$ 319,700. The board met on Thursday, April 16. It wanted an explanation, a response for the quarter that was opening, and a plan for the two quarters after it, whichever way the peso went next.

## The Protagonist

Montiel-Arze was forty-six and had run Huertos Velandria for seven years. She had come up through operations, running the packing house through eleven harvests before the founding family asked her to take the top job. She had led the company through the fuel-shock recession that ended the previous year. She knew the orchards, the contract growers and the US buyers by name. She had never had to manage a currency. For most of her tenure the peso had moved inside a narrow band, and treasury at Huertos Velandria had meant a credit line and a monthly call with the bank.

## The Decision

The board wanted three things settled, one for each of the next three quarters (see **Exhibit 2**). For the quarter now opening, under the strong peso, she had to decide how to close the gap on the anchor contract, or how much of it to absorb. For the second quarter, the board asked what the company would do with a windfall if capital flows reversed and the peso fell back through CO$ 5.00 toward CO$ 6.00. For the third, it asked for a written hedging policy that the treasury team could follow if the peso began to swing hard in both directions. The board would judge the plan on a single figure: cumulative profit across the three quarters.

## The Company and its Setting

The Ibarrola family had founded Huertos Velandria in 1998 in Costaria's central valley, two hours by road from the Pacific port. By 2026 it employed 140 people, owned 180 hectares of orchard, bought from about 60 contract growers and shipped roughly 6,200 tonnes of fresh mangoes a season. The United States took most of the fruit; smaller volumes went to the European Union and the United Kingdom.

After the recession, Costaria's central bank had held its policy rate high to anchor inflation. Foreign capital had come back, drawn by the returns and a steadier economy, and had bid the peso up. The Finance Minister called the strong peso a vote of confidence in her reforms. The national exporters' association had begun asking her, publicly, to talk it down. In the US market, Huertos Velandria competed with Mexican, Peruvian and Brazilian fruit, and US supermarket buyers moved between origins on price.

## The Situation

Echeverri-Luna's page set out the anchor contract at the old and new rates (see **Exhibit 1**). The finance team used it as the yardstick for the whole order book. Local costs, paid in pesos, came to CO$ 2,898,500 a quarter: wages, cardboard, domestic trucking and the land lease. The exchange rate did not touch them. Imported inputs, priced in dollars, came to USD 355,300 a quarter: diesel, fertiliser, refrigerated containers and the cold-chain monitoring software. At CO$ 5.00 those inputs had cost CO$ 1,776,500; at CO$ 4.00 they cost CO$ 1,421,200. The stronger peso had saved CO$ 355,300 on inputs and taken CO$ 1,000,000 off revenue.

The company held CO$ 5,000,000 in cash. If nothing changed, the anchor contract would lose CO$ 319,700 in the quarter and the cash balance would fall by the same amount. Nothing in the order book would reprice on its own.

The CFO had sketched the two later quarters on the back of the same page. If capital flows reversed, the peso could fall past CO$ 5.00 to CO$ 6.00. Revenue would jump in pesos, and the dollar-priced inputs would cost half as much again in pesos as they did at CO$ 4.00. He added a caution: shipments contracted before a fall were paid at prices agreed earlier, and US buyers took months to switch suppliers. In the second sketch the peso swung between CO$ 4.50 and CO$ 6.50 within single months, and nobody at Huertos Velandria could plan production or budget cash.

## What Montiel-Arze Controlled

For the strong-peso quarter, she controlled four things. The first was the **dollar price** to US importers, which she could raise by up to 15%. Rafael Quintanar-Bello, head of export sales, reported that two buyers had already mentioned Peruvian fruit. The second was a **cut in local costs** of up to 5% of the peso cost base, through tighter terms with contract growers, deferred maintenance and a hiring freeze. Growers had long memories, and the company relied on them for a third of its fruit. The third was whether to **mechanise one packing line**. The equipment supplier quoted CO$ 900,000 installed and CO$ 60,000 a quarter in packing wages saved. The fourth was the **share of the order book moved toward European buyers**, up to half, paid in euros. The euro had not fallen against the peso as far as the dollar had, but European retail meant certification, listing fees and new logistics, and those costs grew with the share moved.

For the depreciation quarter, the board asked her to choose one use of the windfall from five. She could **take it as profit**. She could **reinvest in capacity**, with more orchard and a second packing line. She could **hedge forward** at the new rate, locking in a share of the next order, from none to all of it. She could **diversify the currency mix** by sourcing more inputs locally. Or she could **pre-pay six months** of imported diesel and fertiliser.

For the volatile quarter, the policy would allocate **six tools**, each as a percentage of forecast dollar revenue. **Forward contracts** with the bank fixed the rate; they cost the bank's spread and gave up any gain from a favourable move. **Currency options** protected against a bad move and kept a good one, for a premium paid either way. **Natural hedges** meant buying more inputs priced in dollars. **Currency invoicing** meant pricing part of the order book in pesos or euros. **Operating diversification** meant moving sales toward euro and sterling markets. A **cash reserve** held pesos back as self-insurance. Forwards and options together could not exceed 100% of forecast revenue. The treasury team's draft from the spring, never adopted, read 50% forwards, 15% options, 20% natural hedges, no change in invoicing, 30% diversification and 30% cash reserve.

Each quarter she would see the exchange rate, revenue, costs and profit, the cumulative profit and the cash balance.

## Closing

At 21:40 that Monday, Montiel-Arze cleared her desk and laid out three sheets: the CFO's page, Quintanar-Bello's note on the buyers, and the treasury draft nobody had signed. The board pack was due at noon on Wednesday. The peso had closed at CO$ 4.00 again, and the first quarter's shipments would leave the port in nine days.
