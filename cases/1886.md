# Sentinel Logistics: What the System Sees

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1886 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1886 — *Systems & Ethics* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On a Monday morning in April 2026, Naledi Mokoena, Chief Information Officer of Sentinel Logistics (Pty) Ltd, read the frontline adoption survey again. The number had not moved: only 38% of warehouse and driving staff said they were willing to use the new Workforce & Operations Management System, WOMS, as designed. The Johannesburg pilot had leaked two weeks earlier, and every distribution centre knew what the system's people-analytics module did — it scored individual productivity and behaviour in real time, and in a management meeting someone had said "right-sizing" out loud. Mokoena had six weeks until the national rollout, ten working days before the union's threatened dispute became real, and a board wanting one thing confirmed: that the ZAR 95 million annual efficiency gain the ZAR 180 million system was supposed to deliver still held.

A journalist had also started calling, asking how Sentinel's system decided who to flag and watch. Mokoena had the WOMS specification, the adoption survey, the union's letter, and a POPIA compliance summary on her desk — and none of them told her what to do next.

## The Protagonist

Mokoena, 44, had been Sentinel's CIO for three years. She had championed the WOMS purchase to the board, arguing the integrated system for warehouse allocation, driver telematics and productivity tracking would pay for itself within three years. She sat on the operating committee, reported to the CEO, and had never had to explain a tech decision to a union.

## The Decision

Mokoena had four things to settle before the board-and-union forum, six weeks out (Exhibit 2): how to frame WOMS and name the single biggest risk of getting that wrong; the rules for what the system's data could be used for and by whom; the shape of the rollout and how staff and the union were consulted; and one binding safeguard, funded and defended to a room holding the board, the union, and possibly the journalist.

## The Company and its Setting

Sentinel Logistics ran nine distribution centres and 640 trucks from its Johannesburg head office, moving freight nationally for clients who cared, above all, about delivery reliability. The company employed 4,800 people, roughly 3,900 of them on the floor or driving routes. Revenue in the last year had been about ZAR 7.2 billion, at an operating margin of roughly 6% — thin and typical of the sector. The workforce was unionised, operating under POPIA, South Africa's law on employers' handling of employees' personal data.

## The Situation

The WOMS purchase was not in question — the ZAR 180 million was spent, the Johannesburg pilot already ran, and switching the system off nationally was not on the table. What remained open was how it would be used; four numbers on her desk each measured a different piece of it (Exhibit 1). Frontline adoption, the share of staff using WOMS as intended rather than working around it, stood at 38%, and the promised ZAR 95 million gain held only if that climbed further. Stakeholder trust, the union's and the workforce's confidence that the company would use the system fairly, sat at 30 out of 100; trust, once spent, returned slowly. POPIA compliance — a lawful basis for the data, a completed impact assessment, a privacy notice that mentioned the telematics — stood at 20 out of 100: barely started. The net financial outcome, the annual gain or loss once change and safeguard spending were counted, stood at zero: a promise the same arithmetic could carry down toward a loss of roughly ZAR 40 million if adoption collapsed instead. Mokoena had ZAR 25 million for training, transparency and whichever safeguard she chose, none of it committed.

If nothing changed before the forum, every number pointed the same way: the survey would still read 38%, the union's ten days would run out, and the board would want a figure nothing in the plan yet supported.

## What Mokoena Controlled

Her first task was framing WOMS. It carried four competing descriptions: a technical project aimed at a stable integration, the people side left to follow; a system reshaping power, visibility and trust between managers and workers, not just coordinating their tasks; a cost programme where the promised gain was the point and surveillance worries were noise to manage; or a neutral efficiency tool where adoption was treated as just a training problem to solve later. She also had to name the biggest risk of choosing wrong: the workforce rejecting the tool through gaming and work-to-rule, turning gain into loss; a technical failure at go-live, the integration breaking down before anyone could fix it; adoption, the union and the paperwork as three separate problems, none serious alone; or a bad week of press coverage about surveillance, uncomfortable but survivable on its own. And, as a percentage, how much she would listen to frontline staff before deciding.

Her second task set the data-use rules: purpose limitation first — performance management and headcount decisions taken together; coordination and safety only, with both ruled out; or performance management alone, stopping short of headcount. Access came next: aggregated by default, individual data restricted, nothing automated; open to every manager, with automated flags alone triggering discipline; or restricted to line managers, with a required human sign-off. Then the data-protection posture: complete the assessment, fix the lawful basis and update the telematics notice; skip the paperwork, trusting the internal rules felt fair enough; or update the privacy notice and name a lawful basis while deferring the full assessment. And the fatigue-and-safety detection the analytics enabled: leave it running exactly as it was, full capture, governance deferred to later; keep it running but narrowly governed, minimal data, safety purpose only; or switch it off, losing the safety upside with the risk.

Her third task was the rollout: a phased start with the willing centres, asking the board for more time; a big-bang launch across all nine centres on the original schedule, momentum over caution; or three fast waves with barely a pause, meeting the same deadline. Consultation meant skipping consultation entirely and briefing the union only after go-live; consulting early and letting workers help shape the safeguards and the score's uses; or informing the union with a feedback channel but a fixed design. A training budget went alongside: nothing, trusting staff to adopt the system once it was simply switched on; ZAR 4 million for basic e-learning modules and a launch communications campaign; or ZAR 9 million for coaching, data demonstrations, worker champions and a transparency portal. Then a single number for the board: the adoption target she would be held to.

Her fourth task was the safeguard: an independent ethics review with worker representation in governance; a charter on the intranet stating company values; no formal safeguard, relying on the good intentions in place; or human review of individual decisions with a formal appeal process. She then had to decide how binding it was — an enforceable appeal, an independent reviewer and a real worker say; a statement of intent with no enforcement or appeal; or an appeal that existed on paper though management could still override it — and how much of the budget to spend making it real: nothing, running it on goodwill inside teams that already existed; ZAR 3 million for a part-time function and a basic appeals process; or ZAR 6 million for a fully staffed oversight function with an independent reviewer. Her defence at the forum could reassure the board the full gain would arrive regardless, ethics a side note; reconcile the efficiency target, the law, the union relationship and staff trust into one account; or tell the union and the journalist WOMS was simply a productivity tool — held, in the end, with a conviction she would have to put a number on.

## Closing

At eight that Monday morning, Mokoena set the adoption survey down next to the union's letter and the POPIA summary, and turned to a blank page to start drafting what the board was waiting on. The forum was six weeks out. The union wanted an answer in ten working days. She had a ZAR 180 million system already running, a ZAR 95 million promise attached to it, and thirty-eight percent of the people who were supposed to use it.
