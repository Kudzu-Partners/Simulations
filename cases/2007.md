# Cobalt Coatings: The Renewal Portfolio

**Case Code:** STR-2026-2007 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2007 — *The Innovation Bet* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Wednesday, 14 January 2026, the board of Cobalt Coatings NV adopted a target that had not been on the agenda when the meeting opened. By 2031, at least 25% of the Brussels company's revenue would have to come from products or business models that did not exist in 2025. A non-executive director had put the case in a presentation nobody interrupted, and the resolution passed the same afternoon.

Marleen Devriendt, appointed General Manager the previous autumn, had eight weeks. The board wanted a portfolio plan at its March meeting: not a strategy paper, but a list of what the company's €21M annual innovation envelope would pay for and what it would stop paying for. It had also let her know, without putting it in the minutes, that a plan it did not find credible would be answered by appointing a Chief Transformation Officer above her.

## The Protagonist

Devriendt, 46, had spent her career inside the coatings industry and most of it inside Cobalt. She had run the automotive business unit for six years and had built her reputation there on delivery: reformulations shipped on the date promised, customer qualifications passed first time, margin held through two raw-material shocks. It was an operating record, not an innovation record. The projects she had personally signed off over that period had all been improvements to chemistries Cobalt already sold — which was, as she was aware, exactly the behaviour the board had just voted to stop rewarding.

## The Decision

Devriendt had to decide what Cobalt would fund for the coming year, what it would cut to pay for it, how the company would get to a bio-based coating, and what would have to be true twelve months later for that programme to continue.

## The Company and its Setting

Cobalt Coatings NV made protective and functional coatings for automotive, construction and electronics customers across Europe from plants in Antwerp and Liège. It employed 1,180 people and had recorded €420M of revenue in 2025 at a 16% EBITDA margin (see **Exhibit 1**).

The distinguishing fact about that revenue was its chemistry. Some 78% of it — €328M — came from solvent-based coatings. REACH restrictions were tightening, VOC-emission limits were tightening faster, and Cobalt's larger customers had begun writing solvent-free requirements into their own supplier roadmaps. The solvent lines had been declining between four and six per cent a year: on a €328M base, €13M to €20M of revenue leaving the company annually, compounding, with the highest-margin automotive accounts moving first. That business remained the cash engine. It paid for the plants, the people and the €21M innovation envelope itself. Cobalt's planning team tracked its health on an internal index scaled from 0 to 100; entering January it read 62.

## The Situation

The pipeline Devriendt inherited held eleven active projects, and 92% of the spending in it was incremental — reformulations, cost-downs and line extensions of products already on the price list. Against the new mandate, the arithmetic was blunt. A quarter of €420M was roughly €105M of revenue that had to come from things Cobalt did not sell in 2025, and almost none of the eleven projects would produce any of it.

Four claims competed for the envelope, and they did not fit inside it (see **Exhibit 2**). The first was inertia with a contract attached: €8M of committed sustaining projects, some defending named customers and real margin, others running because they had always run. The second was defence: a water-based reformulation programme at €4M, with an 85% technical success probability, revenue in eighteen months and €14M of NPV — incremental work on the lines the company already sold.

The third was the bet. Cobalt's R&D group had a route to a bio-based resin platform, addressing a European market put at €1.2B by 2030. Built in-house it would cost €9M a year, carried a 45–55% technical success probability, and would not produce revenue for four years or more. The fourth claim was a partnership. NovaResin, a spin-off from Ghent with a working bio-resin already at TRL 6, would co-develop the platform with Cobalt for €6M a year, cutting roughly two years off the timetable — in exchange for control of the intellectual property and 40% of the platform's margin. NovaResin's founders had separately offered 30% of the company for €18M.

Inside Cobalt the argument had already sorted itself into two camps that were not really arguing about the same question. The commercial organisation wanted the water-based programme funded in full and the rest deferred, because customers were asking for water-based products now and nobody was asking for bio-resin. The R&D group wanted the platform built in-house, on the grounds that a company that licensed its next chemistry from a spin-off in Ghent would end up as that spin-off's factory. Neither camp had proposed anything to cut.

What would happen absent a decision was not dramatic. The solvent lines would go on shedding €13M to €20M of revenue a year. The eleven projects would deliver improvements to products already declining. The envelope would be spent, in full, on the pipeline that already existed, and the 2031 mandate would be met by nothing at all.

## What Devriendt Controlled

She controlled the reading and the money. How she characterised the pipeline and sized the gap set what the March meeting would be about; so did her treatment of the €8M sustaining book, which she could defend entirely, triage project by project, or clear out. So did the sentence she chose to put at the top of the paper — whether Cobalt's problem was that it was not growing fast enough, that it was spending too much on research for a shrinking business, or that it had to keep a declining cash engine alive and build its replacement out of the same envelope.

She controlled the route to the platform: build it, co-develop it with NovaResin, do both, or do neither. If NovaResin was involved, she set the terms — a co-development agreement carrying a board seat, a change-of-control clause and an IP licence-back; the €18M equity stake; or a plain arms-length contract signed quickly and cheaply. She also decided what would protect Cobalt if a competitor bought NovaResin or the resin chemistry failed to scale.

Then she committed the money, and the total could not exceed €21M: sustaining at €8M, €5M, €3M or nothing; the water-based defence at €4M, €2M or nothing; the platform at €9M as a full build, at €6M as a first tranche with a decision point at month 12, at €6M routed to the co-development, or not at all; and a reserve of €3M, €1M or nothing held back for the year.

Finally she decided how to stand in front of the board in March — what she led with, how she justified her largest single commitment and named the conditions under which she would stop it, whether she had modelled what a successful bio platform would do to the solvent business that funded it, and how much of the plan she was willing to have written down as binding for the next twelve months.

The board had said it would judge her on four measures: how much of the ~€105M renewal gap the funded plan closed; the blended risk-adjusted NPV, in millions of euros, of the portfolio she chose; the core resilience index, at 62 and falling; and the value the plan created per euro of the €21M.

## Closing

That evening Devriendt wrote €21M at the top of a sheet of paper and the four claims underneath it, and the sheet came to €27M. Somewhere below the line were customers who had been promised a reformulation, a chemistry Cobalt could not yet make, and a spin-off in Ghent that could already make it. The March meeting was eight weeks away.
