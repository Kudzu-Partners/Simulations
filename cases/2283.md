# Sonoran Ridge Community Bank: FDIC Branch

**Case code:** FIN-2026-2283 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2283 — *FDIC Branch* · **v1.0 · October 2026**
**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday 4 January 2027, her first morning as manager of Sonoran Ridge Community Bank's Maryvale branch in Phoenix, Arizona, Marisol Quintero Ybarra opened the regional retail plan for the year. Craig Ostrander, the regional director, had doubled every branch's account-opening goals and wanted a Spanish-language campaign for the bank's free checking account. By the end of the day she had to settle two things: what her six personal bankers would be paid to do in 2027, and in which language the branch would serve its customers.

She would make eight decisions in all, two at each of four points in the year (see Exhibit 2).

## The Protagonist

Marisol Quintero Ybarra, 36, had grown up a few blocks from the branch. She had started at the bank as a teller and spent eight years as a personal banker. Craig Ostrander set the region's targets. Denise Okafor-Hale, the bank's compliance and Bank Secrecy Act officer, worked from the main office in Tempe.

## The Decision

The branch's bankers opened more accounts per month than the regional average, and the previous manager had paid them for it, with a board in the break room showing who was ahead. Fewer people checked whether the accounts were used. "Some of those accounts never see a deposit," the assistant manager told her. "The bankers say the customers change their minds." Each decision would be taken on its date and could not be revisited.

## The Company and its Setting

Sonoran Ridge Community Bank was an Arizona state-chartered bank with 24 branches; as it was not a Federal Reserve member, its federal supervisor was the FDIC. Its last rating under the Community Reinvestment Act, under which supervisors assess how a bank serves the credit needs of its whole community, including low- and moderate-income neighbourhoods, had been Satisfactory, and the FDIC's next evaluation was scheduled for November 2027.

The Maryvale branch, on West Indian School Road, and its small office on 35th Avenue had 16 employees. In 2026 they held about $180 million of deposits and $55 million of loans. The branch earned about $5.6 million: a credit the bank paid branches for the deposits they gathered, the spread on their loans and fees. After about $1.4 million of servicing costs and about $3.3 million of staff, occupancy and overhead, its contribution was about $900,000 (see Exhibit 1). Most of the census tracts it served were low- or moderate-income, and about two in three of its households spoke Spanish at home.

## The Situation

A February review of the branch's 2026 mortgage data showed that the loan officers' discretionary pricing exceptions had left Hispanic borrowers on average 0.30 percentage points worse off than non-Hispanic white borrowers with similar credit, and that the branch's own tracts had the fewest mortgage applications of any branch. Regional was also looking at the cost of the 35th Avenue office.

If Marisol ran the branch the way her predecessor had, she would keep the product-count goals, serve customers in English with an interpreter on request, leave pricing exceptions to the loan officers, have no neighbourhood lending plan, leave cash to the teller system, send large depositors to the brokerage partner, let regional close the 35th Avenue office with the legal notice, and fire any banker caught gaming the goals.

## What Quintero Controlled

In January she would choose the incentive plan: salary only, with no incentives; a scorecard that paid bankers on balances still active after 90 days, customer-survey results and a compliance quality check, with incentives clawed back for accounts closed unused or opened without the customer's consent, for about $60,000; the regional plan, which doubled the goals and added a daily leaderboard; or the bank's product-count goals with monthly payouts per account opened. And how the branch would serve Spanish speakers: steer customers to the mobile app and close the teller line on Saturdays, saving about $90,000; English service and documents, with a telephone interpreter on request; a written language-access policy with two certified bilingual bankers, a bilingual teller on every shift and key documents and complaints in Spanish, for about $70,000; or the Spanish radio and billboard campaign, with staff and documents in English only.

In April she would choose how loans were priced: leave the loan officers' discretion as it was; set a 680 minimum credit score and make no mortgage or small-business loan under $100,000; a written rate sheet, with exceptions only for documented reasons such as a competing offer in writing and a monthly comparison of exceptions by borrower group; or centralised pricing with no exceptions at all. And the lending plan for the neighbourhood: buy $4 million of mortgages originated by another lender that would count toward the bank's Community Reinvestment Act record; a first-time homebuyer programme with down-payment grants through the bank's Federal Home Loan Bank membership, homebuyer education in Spanish with Casa Adelante, a HUD-approved counselling agency, and SBA-guaranteed microloans, for about $90,000; no specific plan; or move the loan officer and the mailers to the higher-income ZIP codes outside Maryvale.

In July she would decide what to do about Arturo Celaya, owner of Carnicería El Saguaro, who on Thursday 20 May had deposited $9,500 in cash for the fourth time in two weeks after asking a teller what happened above $10,000, the level at which banks report cash: nothing, since he was a good customer and no single deposit reached $10,000; close his account and tell him it was because of the reports; have a teller tell him to keep each deposit under $10,000 so there would be no paperwork; or have the tellers add up same-day cash for the bank's cash reports, refer the pattern to Okafor-Hale, who would decide whether to file a suspicious activity report, and tell the customer nothing. And how to answer Efraín Ledesma, 67, a customer for thirty years, who had sold his tire shop for $780,000 and asked whether all of it would be FDIC insured at the branch: one $780,000 certificate of deposit in his name, without discussing the limit; explain coverage by ownership category with the FDIC's estimator — $250,000 in his name, $250,000 in his wife Rosaura's name and $280,000 in a joint account — and put it in writing; tell him everything at the bank was FDIC insured and open one account in his name; or send the couple to the brokerage partner or tell them to spread the money across banks.

In October she would decide on the 35th Avenue office, used by about 1,900 households in a moderate-income tract: close it with the 90-day notice to the FDIC and to customers and the ATM kept, saving about $180,000 a year; convert it to a full-service branch for about $220,000; keep it open with shorter hours, an interactive teller machine and a bilingual banker three days a week, for about $60,000; or close it in 30 days with a letter, without the 90-day notice. And she would respond to an internal audit of Wednesday 15 September that had found personal accounts and debit cards opened in 2025–2027 without customers' signatures by two bankers chasing the goals: fire the two bankers and refund the fees the audit found; close the accounts quietly, with no refunds and no report; close every such account, refund all fees with interest, review every banker, report it through the bank's compliance process and change the incentive; or keep the accounts open and reclassify them as customer-requested to protect the branch's numbers.

After each decision point she would see six figures: the branch contribution, what the branch earned for the year after all its costs; the CRA index, a 0–100 measure of the branch's lending, investment and service in its own community; the compliance findings, the problems an examiner or auditor would raise; the households, an index of the households the branch served with 2026 as 100; the year's revenue; and the penalties and remediation paid. She would also receive a score out of 100 after each decision point.

## Closing

At nine the doors opened. Two bankers were wiping December's numbers off the leaderboard. A woman with a toddler stopped at the greeter's desk and asked, in Spanish, whether someone could explain the letter she had received about her account. The greeter looked over at Marisol.
