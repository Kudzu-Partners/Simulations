# Trinity Quad Executive Learning: Co-Creating the Programme

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1819 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1819 — *Co-Creating the Programme* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday 14 September 2026, Naomi Sinclair, Custom Programmes Director at Trinity Quad Executive Learning, opened the file on the biggest contract of her year. Helvern Industrial Group, a £3.8 billion FTSE-250 engineering firm, had just committed £680,000 for a custom programme to build strategic leadership capability across its top ninety senior managers ahead of a major restructuring. The brief itself ran to one paragraph of generic ambition. Helvern's chief executive wanted the first of three cohorts, thirty managers, live within ten weeks.

Over those ten weeks and the months that followed, Sinclair would have to work out what Helvern actually needed before designing anything against it, build a learning journey to that diagnosis inside the fixed £680,000, defend what it cost against a rival number the client's procurement team already had on the table, and keep the programme working once delivery met the reality of a company mid-restructuring.

## The Protagonist

Sinclair, 44, had been Trinity Quad's Custom Programmes Director since 2023. She had built her reputation on a simple discipline: never let a client's stated request substitute for a diagnosis of what the client actually needed, even when saying so risked the relationship. Her own performance review, like every director's at the unit, weighed two things above everything else: the contribution margin on the engagements she ran, measured against the unit's 32% benchmark, and whether the sponsor came back to commission a second engagement — the repeat business that made up 60% of Trinity Quad's revenue.

## The Decision

The shape of the task was not one decision but four, in sequence. Sinclair first had to decide how to read a client that was describing three different problems at once. She then had to configure a learning journey against that reading without breaking the budget. She had to defend whatever that journey cost once procurement produced a cheaper number. And she had to keep the programme alive, and provably working, once real delivery began.

## The Company and its Setting

Trinity Quad Executive Learning was the custom-programmes unit of a leading business school in Cambridge, England, built around a single premise: a bespoke executive programme should be designed jointly with the client's sponsor, starting from a diagnosis of the organisation's real challenge rather than a catalogue of existing courses. The unit generated £14.2 million in annual revenue at a 32% contribution margin, and 60% of that revenue came from clients who had commissioned more than once. Its average client-loyalty score stood at 71 out of 100 — a strong number in a market where faculty were increasingly oversubscribed, lead times were tightening, and clients were starting to ask for evidence of business impact rather than well-reviewed teaching days.

## The Situation

The contract covered ninety senior managers in three cohorts of thirty, for £680,000 — a cost per participant of £7,556. Helvern's procurement team had already benchmarked rival providers at £5,500 per participant, 27% lower, and would raise it. A tenth of the contract, £68,000, was structured as a fee payable only if Helvern judged the programme to have produced a measurable impact after cohort 1. If confidence collapsed, Helvern's agreement let it cancel cohorts two and three outright, forfeiting £453,000 of what remained (Exhibit 1).

Underneath those numbers sat a disagreement her own sponsor interviews had surfaced: the chief executive read the challenge as realigning leadership thinking after the restructuring, the chief HR officer read it as a risk that high-potential managers would leave before the restructuring finished, and the line directors read it as a failure to deliver projects on time. Only two of the five senior faculty Trinity Quad had proposed were free inside the ten-week window, and the format chosen for reaching ninety managers across four UK sites would move the design's cost by as much as £90,000 either way. Left alone, a programme designed straight off the one-paragraph brief would cost £7,556 a head against a £5,500 quote Helvern had not forgotten, the £68,000 would rest on whatever evidence happened to get collected, and a poor first cohort gave Helvern grounds to walk away from two-thirds of the contract's value.

## What Sinclair Controlled

The first decision was what to make of three sponsor accounts that did not agree. Sinclair could treat the chief executive's account as the operative one; reconcile the three accounts into a single prioritised statement before designing anything; or set the interviews aside and start drafting a programme immediately. She then had to decide what the programme should build toward: settle on a broad menu of standard leadership competencies, covering every plausible reading; assemble strong content first, trusting relevance to emerge once good faculty were teaching it; or name three or four capabilities tied directly to the restructuring outcome and Helvern's success measure. And she had to decide how hard to push, before quoting anything, for a half-hour call with Helvern's sponsors to test which reading was closest to right.

Configuring the journey added three more choices inside the same £680,000. Sinclair could anchor it in on-the-job application, a workplace project and peer coaching around a few teaching days; keep it to faculty-led teaching days, on the reasoning that direct instruction was what Helvern was paying for; or run a balanced mix of case discussion and a simulation, mostly inside the classroom. Faculty time then had to split between core staff at £2,200 a day and star practitioners at £4,500 a day — only two of Helvern's five preferred names among them — every day given to one unavailable to the other. And the format for reaching ninety managers across four sites had to be set: modular-virtual delivery only, with no in-person residential time, at £80,000; five residential days per cohort, entirely offsite, at £260,000; or a hybrid of residential anchor days and virtual modules, at £170,000 — a swing of £90,000 either way, before the fixed £120,000 build was added (Exhibit 2).

Defending the design brought a third set of choices. When procurement produced a rival quote of £5,500 a participant — 27% below Trinity Quad's £7,556 — Sinclair could match £5,500 by removing the same elements that made up the difference in cost; defend the value in business terms, explaining what they were meant to produce; or hold the original price and decline to discuss any of the trade-offs. Alongside price sat the £68,000 success fee, a tenth of the contract: she could tie the fee to a measure of behaviour or business change, built in from day one; renegotiate the fee down to an end-of-course satisfaction score, simpler to collect; or drop the success fee altogether rather than chase a hard-to-earn number. Procurement also wanted a concession in the room on the spot, its size hers to set.

Delivery brought a fourth round of choices. When a faculty member who anchored the programme's relevance dropped out the week before cohort 1, Sinclair could delay the cohort to keep that faculty member in place; swap in whoever was free and say nothing to the sponsor; or bring in a relevance-matched substitute, brief them properly, and tell the sponsor. She then had to design the measurement that would decide the £68,000 fee: collect end-of-course satisfaction scores alone, a simple number to gather; combine before-and-after behavioural ratings with a business KPI tied to the restructuring; or rely on a single hard business KPI, with nothing alongside it. For cohorts two and three, worth £453,000, she could proceed with named adjustments drawn directly from cohort 1's evidence; pause cohorts two and three entirely until that evidence was fully in; or repeat cohort 1's design exactly, without changing what had already shipped. Throughout, with attendance patchy from restructuring firefighting, she had to decide how hard to protect the sessions that did run.

## Closing

By mid-morning, Sinclair had the one-paragraph brief, three sponsor accounts that did not agree, a faculty list with two of five preferred names still free, and a calendar reading ten weeks to cohort 1. Whether Helvern's board would judge £680,000 well spent depended on choices she had not yet made, and procurement's £5,500 number was already sitting in someone's inbox.
