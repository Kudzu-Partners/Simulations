# Verano Alimentos: Conscious Capital Under Fire

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1850 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1850 — *Conscious Capital* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of March 11, 2026, Marina Dutra's phone would not stop buzzing before she finished her coffee. A respected Brazilian NGO had published a report accusing Verano Alimentos S.A. of greenwashing: water stress and effluent problems at two plants in a drought-affected region, precarious pay among the delivery riders who moved its products, and a deforestation-linked risk in part of its fruit supply. A consumer's post amplified it within hours, and the hashtag was trending nationally. Dutra, three months into her role as Verano's Sustainability Manager, had a board-and-retailer review in forty-five days and, for now, no plan to bring to it.

Her CEO, approving her R$28 million annual sustainability budget, had told her to make it count — to prove the "people and planet" language Verano's founders built into the brand since 1989 was a real, measurable strategy, not the marketing copy the NGO was now using against it. Verano's internal brand-trust index had fallen from 72 to 61 out of 100 in two weeks. Two retail chains representing 22% of revenue — roughly R$750 million — held ESG-compliance clauses and had given Verano forty-five days to produce a credible, verified remediation plan before delisting its premium lines.

## The Protagonist

Dutra had spent eleven years in sustainability and CSR roles at two multinational consumer-goods companies before Verano recruited her, drawn by its reputation as one of Brazil's first food companies to talk openly about purpose and stakeholders. She had joined in December 2025, hired to professionalize a function that had produced glossy brochures rather than audited numbers. Her mandate was explicit: the R$28 million existed to create real value, not positive coverage, and she would be judged on whether brand trust recovered on substance rather than a louder claim.

## The Decision

Over the following weeks Dutra had to make four connected calls: which stakeholder group would lead Verano's response, and by what reasoning; how to divide the R$28 million among the CSR initiatives competing for it, knowing that funding all of them fully would cost closer to R$52 million; how transparently Verano reported its performance, including numbers that made it look bad; and how to present the finished plan to the board, the retailers and a skeptical journalist without inviting the accusation that started the crisis.

## The Company and its Setting

Verano Alimentos S.A. was a Brazilian food and beverage company headquartered in Campinas, São Paulo state: roughly R$3.4 billion in annual revenue, seven plants, about 5,800 employees, and a portfolio of juices, dairy and packaged snacks sold across Brazilian retail and increasingly abroad. Its fruit and milk supply chain ran through some 2,300 smallholder farmers, and its distribution depended on contracted delivery riders nationwide. Verano's founders had marketed the company as purpose-led from its earliest years, and that history was now under scrutiny: a brand claiming to serve "people and planet" invited a harsher standard than one that never made the claim.

## The Situation

The NGO's report was specific: water stress and effluent readings at two plants in a drought-restricted region; pay and working-condition complaints from gig-economy riders; and an unsubstantiated risk that part of the fruit supply touched deforested land. None of the three claims, alone, was catastrophic; together, amplified by a viral post, they cost eleven points of brand trust in two weeks and put a quarter of Verano's revenue base on notice.

Beyond brand trust, the board wanted two further measures tracked: a stakeholder-value score, capturing real benefit created for farmers, employees, communities and investors together, as distinct from how good initiatives looked in a press release; and a reporting-credibility score, capturing whether outside observers — an assurance provider, a journalist, the NGO — would call the disclosures verified fact or spin. Both started low: real value creation had been thin for years, and the existing report was a brochure with no independent verification.

The R$28 million budget was fixed, with no top-up available (Exhibit 1). An internal costing exercise priced fully addressing every issue the NGO named — retrofits, traceability, rider welfare and assured reporting — at roughly R$52 million. Moving the report to a recognized standard with strong assurance would cost up to R$5 million more, R$1.5 million for the standard and up to R$3.5 million more for the highest assurance tier. The NGO had signaled a follow-up report in sixty days; whatever Verano did in the next forty-five would still be standing when it arrived.

If nothing changed — the budget unallocated or scattered thinly, the report still a brochure, the messaging as confident as before the NGO published — the retailers' clock would run out, the day-60 report would find the same plants and supply chain untouched, and nothing would pull trust back toward 72.

## What Dutra Controlled

Dutra's first decision was which stakeholder concern would lead Verano's response: the water and effluent problems at the two named plants and the deforestation-linked sourcing risk; the pay and working-condition complaint raised by the delivery riders who moved its products; the retail customers and consumers, whose pressure was the most immediate commercial force; or the 2,300 smallholder farmers, whose livelihoods and traceability were directly at stake. Attached was the logic behind the choice: ranking stakeholders strictly by power to affect revenue quickly; weighing each issue by its impact on business, people and the environment; applying no formal lens and responding to pressure as it arrived; or following whichever concern generated the most social-media attention at the time. Separately, on a 0-to-100 scale, she had to decide how much effort to spend seeking a priority serving several stakeholder groups at once, rather than ranking them against each other.

Her second decision was how to divide the R$28 million among four candidate initiatives (Exhibit 2): a water-treatment retrofit at the two named plants, a smallholder traceability and fair-sourcing program, a rider-welfare scheme for delivery workers, and a community and education program near Verano's plants. Nothing fixed the split; the only constraint was that the four allocations together could not exceed R$28 million. Alongside it came a discipline choice: spreading money evenly so every group received visible support; directing funds toward options that cost least and drew the most favorable attention; or concentrating funds on initiatives she judged most likely to create measurable stakeholder value.

Her third decision covered how Verano reported what it had done: report against the recognized GRI standard at R$1.5 million, or continue the voluntary brochure at R$0.3 million; commission the strongest tier, reasonable assurance, at R$3.5 million; a mid-level third-party review at R$2 million; or no external assurance of any kind; limit the reporting boundary strictly to Verano's own manufacturing operations, or extend it across the full supply chain, including the farmers and deforestation-linked sourcing. Independent of format, on a 0-to-100 scale, she had to decide how fully to disclose the unflattering numbers, including effluent data not yet verified.

Her fourth decision was how to present the plan to the board, the retailers and the journalist: present a funded, verified plan described conservatively relative to the evidence; describe Verano, boldly, as Brazil's most sustainable food company, backed by the forty-five-day plan; or describe it confidently, as a clear leader in responsible sourcing. She also had to decide how the pitch tied priority, funding and reporting together: presented as a set of loosely connected pieces that were not quite joined up; a strong communications campaign in its own right; or fully integrated with Verano's stated purpose across priority, funding and reporting. And she had to decide how to handle the NGO's threatened sixty-day follow-up — prepare a rebuttal to contest the claims if attacked; share the verified plan and unflattering data with the NGO early; or monitor quietly and respond only if it appeared — while setting, on a final 0-to-100 scale, how firmly every claim would stay inside what Verano could verify.

## Closing

By the second week after the report, Dutra had the budget spreadsheet, the stakeholder map and the reporting-cost table open in three windows on the same screen, and forty-five days had already become thirty-eight. The board wanted a plan it could defend to two retail buyers and, before long, to the journalist who had asked for an interview. She had R$28 million, four stakeholder groups pulling in different directions, and one chance to make Verano's oldest promise sound true.
