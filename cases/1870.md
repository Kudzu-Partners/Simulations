# Greenfield Beverages Nigeria: The Naira Beachhead

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1870 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1870 — *Inside the Informal Market* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 8 June 2026, the half-year commercial review at Greenfield Beverages Nigeria Ltd. delivered a verdict nobody at the Lagos head office wanted to hear aloud. Modern-trade volume, the supermarkets and organized chains the commercial team had spent three years mastering, had flatlined. A regional competitor selling almost entirely through the open-air markets had just overtaken Greenfield on national volume. The board's response was one number and one deadline: capture meaningful share of the Idumota/Balogun and Oyingbo market clusters in Lagos, prove the model in a tier-two test in Onitsha, within two quarters, on a ₦900 million route-to-market budget — or the mass-market mandate would go to someone else.

Adaeze Nwosu got the assignment that same afternoon. At 38, she had spent six years running key accounts for Greenfield's modern-trade business, where a price list, once agreed, stayed agreed. She had asked for this posting over the polite objections of two colleagues who doubted a channel built on cash and bargaining could be planned like a supermarket account. Her new title, Trade Marketing & Channel Director, came with the ₦900 million budget, the two-quarter clock, and a board that had made clear it would not extend either.

## The Protagonist

Nwosu had joined Greenfield nine years earlier from a regional distributor, building her reputation on discipline rather than charisma: she hit forecast, never over-promised a listing, and was the manager other directors asked to explain why a number had moved. None of that had prepared her for a channel where the same product could sell at three different prices on the same street within an hour, and where the biggest competitive threat was not a rival's advertising spend but the fourteen days of credit it quietly extended to traders Greenfield had never met. Her performance review, like every director's at Greenfield, was tied to the commercial priorities the board set each year — and this year the board had set exactly one.

## The Decision

Nwosu had to make four linked decisions across the two quarters, each narrowing her options for the next. First, before spending a naira, she had to diagnose the channel rather than assume it. Was the barrier landed cost, too high to survive once a trader haggled it down? Was it physical reach, the difficulty of stocking thousands of tiny stalls? Was it awareness, a name consumers had never heard from anyone they trusted? Or was it trust — the incumbent's decade of relationships, and credit no trader ever had to repay in cash? And did power sit with Greenfield's own two distributors, who already owned every other route to market? Did it concentrate with the senior wholesalers, who could welcome a brand in or freeze it out? Was it spread evenly enough across the hierarchy that no single tier controlled access? Or did it sit with the hawkers, the tier that touched the consumer and decided what actually got carried?

Second, she had to price for a channel where terms were bargained, not posted. Should she publish one national trade price and hold it everywhere, regardless of channel or city? Should she stack margin through every tier so each layer earned enough to push the brand, with haggling room built in? Should terms instead flex trader by trader, tied to loyalty and cash? Or set a volume-tiered wholesale price with retail guidance, rewarding bigger buyers without that room? She still had to fix the trade margin as a share of street price, set the bargaining room, and pick a guardrail against arbitrage: leave buyers to sort undercutting out themselves, police one street price and penalize deviation, or zone the clusters and cap volumes so no reseller could flood the market.

Third, relationships and distribution were coupled, both drawing on the same ₦900 million. Should she build a broad base of smaller resellers from day one — slower, but less exposed to any one trader? Should she court the two or three biggest market leaders first, buying fast credibility at the cost of depending on them? Or win a handful of gatekeepers for a fast entry, then broaden the base afterward to dilute that dependence? Against the incumbent's fourteen-day credit she needed her own policy: stay cash-only, protecting working capital and taking no credit risk; extend a short, selective float to traders who had already shown loyalty; or out-lend the incumbent to every trader who asked, to buy the shelf fast.

Fourth — a certainty in the board's brief — the incumbent would counter a beachhead forming under it: a trade-price cut, a stocking bonus to freeze Greenfield off the shelf, or a gatekeeper demanding exclusivity and a bigger cut. Nwosu would have to choose whether to hold price and defend on the loyalty and bargain satisfaction she had built, match the cut street-price for street-price to hold volume, grant the gatekeeper the exclusivity and cut it demanded, or pull back to a higher, steadier price band and protect margin instead. She also had to decide whether the Lagos plan should travel to Onitsha unchanged, be adapted city by city with each gatekeeper re-mapped, or have every remaining cluster open at once before the board's next review.

## The Company and its Setting

Greenfield Beverages Nigeria Ltd. was the Lagos-headquartered subsidiary of a mid-tier consumer-goods group, manufacturing affordable powdered and ready-to-drink beverages for the mass Nigerian market out of a plant in Ikeja. Group revenue ran to ₦31 billion a year, carried by a 220-person commercial organization (see Exhibit 1). Its modern-trade business was thin: after listing fees and promotional spend, gross margin sat at 9%.

The channel the board wanted her to win was nearly the opposite kind of business. Roughly 85% of Nigeria's fast-moving consumer goods moved through informal trade — the open-air markets, kiosks and table-top stalls where Greenfield, after three years of trying, held under 3% share. Volume there ran through a layered hierarchy of big wholesalers, semi-wholesalers, table-top retailers and hawkers, each buying from the tier above on relationship and cash rather than contract, at a price bargained stall by stall rather than printed on a list. The incumbent that already owned the shelf gave every trader in that hierarchy fourteen days of credit — a term Greenfield's own finance function had never approved for an unlisted market.

## The Situation

Nwosu inherited four numbers she would be judged on across the two quarters. Informal-channel penetration — Greenfield's share of volume in the target clusters — stood under 3%. Net margin in the channel stood at 9%, the same figure modern trade had delivered. Reseller loyalty, a 0–100 read on whether traders would actually push the brand rather than merely stock it, stood at 20. Bargain satisfaction, a 0–100 read on whether buyers felt they had won the haggle, stood at 25. If those numbers did not move materially inside two quarters, the board had been explicit about the consequence: Greenfield would step back from the mass market and cede it to the competitor already winning it nationally.

## What Nwosu Controlled

Each round, Nwosu committed a named set of decisions against the clock. In the first, a diagnosis of the channel's barrier and where power sat, plus a stated percentage of how far she was willing to set aside the modern-trade playbook. In the second, a pricing architecture — the approach, the trade margin, the bargaining-room percentage, and the anti-arbitrage guardrail. In the third, the relationship and credit strategy, and the ₦900 million split across market-based sub-distributors, company van-sales routes, existing wholesale partners and a trade-credit float, detailed in Exhibit 2. In the fourth, her response to whichever counter-move the incumbent made, the scale path into Onitsha, and how firmly she was prepared to defend that plan in front of the board.

## Closing

Nwosu opened the field-research pack on her desk on the morning of 9 June and read the trader-hierarchy diagram twice before writing anything down. Somewhere in Idumota, a wholesaler who had never heard of Greenfield was already deciding, on credit and relationship alone, which new brand from Lagos was worth the shelf space. She had two quarters, ₦900 million, and one diagnosis to get right before she spent any of it.
