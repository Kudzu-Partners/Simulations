# Shannon Mutual: The Fourth Programme

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1937 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1937 — *Value from Tech* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

At 7:40 on the morning of Tuesday, 24 February 2026, Niamh Callaghan stood at the window of a fourth-floor meeting room in Shannon Mutual's Dublin office while the operations director talked her through the weekend. The new online-renewals portal had gone live on Friday evening. By Saturday afternoon it had corrupted 14,000 policyholder records. Renewals stayed frozen for four days, and the first estimate of the cost — remediation, goodwill credits, overtime — was €2.1 million. The letter from the Central Bank of Ireland arrived at 9:12. It asked for a remediation plan within 90 days.

Callaghan had been Chief Digital Officer for seven weeks. The board had given her €30 million over two years, and had recorded in the minute that appointed her what it would do if the money produced what the previous three programmes had: outsource the whole information-technology (IT) function to a vendor. She had until late May to answer the regulator, two years to answer the board, and in that time to decide what the €30 million would buy, in what order, and who would answer for the benefit.

## The Protagonist

Niamh Callaghan, 41, had joined Shannon Mutual in 2019 to run operations for the broker channel and had been appointed Chief Digital Officer in January 2026, the first person in the mutual to hold the title. She had sat on the steering committee of the third transformation programme, through 2023 and 2024, and had signed the paper that closed it out as delivered.

## The Decision

Callaghan had to commit to four things in front of the board, in order: why three funded programmes had produced so little; the order of work for the portfolio; the shape of the IT function and the division of the €30 million; and the account of how the benefit would arrive. Each commitment narrowed the next.

## The Company and its Setting

Shannon Mutual was a 90-year-old member-owned life-and-pensions insurer in Dublin: €18 billion of assets under management, 640,000 policyholders, €1.4 billion of annual premiums, 1,200 employees, and an operating profit of €112 million in 2025.

Underneath it ran a policy-administration platform 34 years old. Seventy per cent of operations still depended on that mainframe, and four decades of bolt-on applications sat around it. The estate produced roughly 18 production incidents a quarter, and the engineering group spent about 60% of its time keeping it alive.

A digital-only competitor had entered the Irish market, serving policyholders at about €25 per policy against Shannon Mutual's €42 and taking roughly 3% of new younger-customer business a year.

## The Situation

Three transformation programmes had run in eight years. The most recent over-ran by €9 million; of what it delivered, 22% was in active use, and the other 78% had been built, accepted and left alone (see **Exhibit 1**).

The board reviewed the same four measures each time Callaghan came back to it. **Value realized** was the board's estimate, on a scale of 0 to 100, of how much business benefit had arrived; it stood at 12, and the board expected 65 by the end of the two years. **Adoption** was the share of what had been delivered that the business used, and it stood at 22%. **Platform health** was a reading from 0 to 100 of how much of the core estate's accumulated technical debt had been paid down, and it stood at 20. **Budget committed** was the running total against the €30 million cap, at €0.0 million. Beside those the board kept a plain record of each round: the decision taken, and the three scores that stood after it.

The arithmetic of doing nothing was not in dispute: the incident rate and the engineering drag would continue, the competitor would keep serving at €25 against €42 and taking about 3% of the younger new business each year, the regulator's ninety days would expire, and the board would act on the position it had already minuted.

The costing team had attached an estimate to each candidate initiative on the shortlist (see **Exhibit 2**). The €30 million itself was committed separately, as a single two-year allocation.

## What Callaghan Controlled

She began with the diagnosis. Four explanations sat on the table for why three funded programmes had delivered technology and little benefit: that the wrong vendors and platforms had been chosen; that the programmes had been under-funded, and €30 million was finally enough; that the organisation had never changed, and nobody had owned the outcome; or that the business had resisted what was asked of it. In the same paper she had to fix what *value* would mean in the board's reporting: systems delivered on time and on budget; or the count of new features and applications launched in the year; or the availability of the core platform; or cost-to-serve, retention, incident reduction and time-to-benefit. She also had to settle, on a scale of 0 to 100, how plainly the diagnosis would be written as an organisational finding rather than a technical one — a paper that would read as an accusation if it went too far, and be filed if it did not.

Then the order of the work. One foundation would be built before anything customer-facing: mobile self-service, at €7 million; AI underwriting, at €8 million; modernising the core platform and its data layer, at €11 million; or no foundation at all — six customer-facing systems bought in parallel and integrated afterwards, at €16 million. Against it she had to name the one visible result the board would see inside the first year: a broker self-service portal requiring no change to the core, at €3 million; a full mobile application on the existing core, at €6 million; a re-launch of online renewals on a repaired foundation, at €4 million; or nothing in the first year, with the whole period given to platform work. And she had to say how strictly the roadmap would hold to its dependencies, again from 0 to 100 — each month of strictness a month in which nothing reached a customer.

Then the two decisions the board wanted in one paper. The first was the shape of the IT function: IT left as a delivery function that took requests from the business and shipped them; the whole function handed to a vendor immediately; IT itself accountable for the benefit of what it delivered; or product-aligned teams with named business owners accountable for the benefit. The second was the money — the €30 million divided, in steps of €500,000 and summing to exactly the cap, across three lines: paying down technical debt in the core estate, building new capability, and change and adoption, meaning process redesign, training, and the people in the business who would carry the benefit. Coupled to it was the core platform itself: modernise it incrementally with a partner while keeping architecture control in-house; replace it with a packaged insurance platform, configured rather than customised; or build a new core in-house.

Last, the account she would give. She had to state how the benefit would be made to arrive: named business owners for each initiative, with benefit measures tracked after go-live; a training day at go-live; IT reporting delivery milestones to the board; or delivering the systems to a high standard and letting use build on its own. She had to answer the Central Bank: by attaching a standalone remediation memo to the plan; by showing how the roadmap removed the conditions that had produced the renewals failure; by patching the renewals portal and reporting it fixed; or by handing the request to the legal team as a compliance matter separate from the transformation. She had to answer the chair's question — benefit, or another technology spend — with the six modern systems the money would buy; with the record of the team she had assembled; or with the named owners, the adoption plan, the leading indicators and the reasoning behind the split. And she had to decide, on the same scale, how hard she would hold that line when the board pushed back.

## Closing

At 8:15 Callaghan wrote four words on the whiteboard — diagnose, prioritise, allocate, commit — and under them two dates: late May, for the Central Bank, and February 2028, for the board. The renewals portal was still frozen. Twelve out of a hundred. Twenty-two per cent. Twenty out of a hundred. Thirty million euro.
