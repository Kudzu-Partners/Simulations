# Empaques del Trópico: Ops Under Pressure

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1930 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1930 — *Ops Under Pressure* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

Two reports reached Mariela Quesada Rojas on February 16, 2026. They covered the same eight weeks and seemed to describe two different factories. The first said that Empaques del Trópico had missed so many January shipments that on-time delivery had fallen to 81% against a contractual target of 96%, and that two anchor export clients had invoked late-delivery clauses worth USD 240,000. The second said the plant was now holding USD 3.1 million of finished goods and compostable resin against no orders — 52 days of stock where the plan called for 30.

Quesada had been operations manager for eleven days. The board had released a USD 6 million modernization budget and given her two quarters to put on-time delivery back at 96% and inventory back at 30 days. Inside the same two quarters she had to answer a third anchor client, which had offered a two-year contract worth USD 14 million and wanted 20% more capacity and tighter service terms with it.

## The Protagonist

Quesada, 41, was an industrial engineer who had spent nine years in scheduling and plant management at a medical-device manufacturer in the Alajuela free zone, the last four as production manager. That plant ran to a demand signal that varied by single digits month to month. Empaques del Trópico did not. She was hired in February 2026 after her predecessor left at the end of January, and reported to the CEO alongside the supply-chain director, who had held his post since 2014 and had written the safety-stock policy still in force.

## The Decision

Quesada had four commitments to make, in order, across the two quarters, each narrowing the ones that came after it: the diagnosis of the missed quarter, the capacity call, the technology spend, and the buffers.

Four numbers followed her through all of it. On-time delivery, the share of orders shipped complete on the promised date, at 81% against 96%. Inventory, in days of stock on hand, at 52 against 30. Capacity utilization, the average share of available line hours actually producing across three lines and two shifts, at 78%. And the cumulative effect of her decisions on cost and profit against doing nothing, in US dollars, starting at zero on a plant that earned about USD 11.2 million.

## The Company and its Setting

Empaques del Trópico S.A., founded in 2009 in the Cartago industrial corridor, made sustainable food-grade packaging: molded-fiber trays, compostable films and corrugated solutions. It employed 540 people, ran three production lines across two shifts at an average of 78% capacity utilization, and shipped from one plant and a regional distribution hub in Panama. Revenue in 2025 was USD 86 million at a 13% EBITDA margin, or about USD 11.2 million.

Fifty-five per cent of that revenue moved with the agricultural export calendar — pineapple, banana and melon — a seasonal, weather-driven cycle that swung monthly volumes by as much as 40% either way. The balance came from regional food processors and a growing base of e-commerce and quick-service-restaurant customers whose orders were comparatively steady. Against all of it, the plant planned production once a month, on a spreadsheet.

## The Situation

January orders had come in 38% above forecast. The plant could not flex, on-time delivery fell to 81%, and the USD 240,000 in penalties followed. Planners then doubled production and resin orders. February demand came back to plan, and the doubled output stayed in the warehouse: 52 days of stock against the 30-day target and USD 3.1 million of finished goods and resin on hand (see **Exhibit 2**). The finance controller costed every day of stock on hand at roughly USD 60,000 in working capital and financing charges, and the charge ran as long as the number did.

Upstream, the compostable resin used in the fastest-growing product family came from a single supplier in Asia on a six-week lead time. That supplier had flagged a three-week shipment delay the week Quesada arrived. The safety-stock policy covering it had been set years earlier against volumes and volatility that no longer applied.

Nothing in that position corrected itself. Left alone, on-time delivery stayed at January's 81%, with two penalty clauses already triggered; inventory stayed at 52 days and kept drawing the carrying charge; utilization stayed at 78% with peak months still breaching capacity; and the next export peak arrived against the same monthly spreadsheet that had failed in January (see **Exhibit 1**).

## What Quesada Controlled

The first of the four was a written diagnosis for the CEO. Four readings of the missed quarter circulated in the plant, and she had to put her name to one. The plant was short of capacity, and a fourth line would end the problem. Or the quarter was weather and coincidence — an exceptional pineapple season on top of a competitor's plant outage — and nothing structural had happened. Or the resin supplier was the binding constraint and every downstream failure traced back to it. Or a monthly plan and order-by-order reaction had turned ordinary seasonality into the swings the plant had lived through. Alongside the diagnosis she had to commit two further judgments: what share of the swing, as a percentage, she attributed to Empaques del Trópico's own decisions rather than to the market, and which repeating pattern she intended to break — the view that January and February had been independent events with no common cause, the relationship with the two clients now charging late-delivery penalties, or the shortage-then-surplus cycle in which a stockout produces heavy ordering, the ordering produces a glut, and the glut produces cuts.

The second commitment was capacity, and it carried the contract with it. She could sign the USD 14 million contract immediately; decline it and leave the revenue with a competitor; or sign it against operating preconditions she would have to specify. She then had to say where peak-month capacity would come from, as a split between permanent capacity — a third shift or an additional line carried on the payroll and the balance sheet all year — and flexible capacity bought by the month as overtime, temporary lines and contract manufacturing. And she had to state what she was sizing against: the single most likely monthly demand number, the highest month the plant had ever recorded, or a service level read off the distribution of monthly demand.

Her third commitment was the technology half of the budget: USD 3.0 million of the USD 6 million, to be split in increments of USD 250,000 across four candidates: demand-sensing analytics, which would replace the monthly forecast with a continuously updated demand signal; a supplier-integration platform giving order-by-order visibility of supplier lead times and shipment status; a manufacturing execution system with line sensors, reporting machine state and output in real time; and an automated finished-goods warehouse adding storage capacity and cutting handling and put-away time. She also had to say what would change in how decisions were actually taken: nothing, with the tools installed and the monthly spreadsheet retained; better month-end reporting on the same monthly cadence; or a weekly demand-and-supply review replacing the spreadsheet.

Her fourth commitment redesigned the buffers and was defended in person to the CEO and the supply-chain director. She had to reset safety stock toward the 30-day target — holding one level across every SKU, cutting every SKU's buffer hard to reach 30 days quickly, or setting each buffer from that SKU's demand variability and lead-time risk. She had to answer the resin exposure — leave sourcing as it stood and manage the finished-goods buffer, hold a large strategic stockpile of resin against the delay, or qualify a second supplier, size a strategic buffer and rewrite the contract terms. She had to name the scorecard she would be managed against: on-time delivery alone, cost per unit alone, or on-time delivery, inventory cost, capacity utilization and cost per unit together. And she had to put a number on her own conviction — the percentage chance, as she saw it, that the plan she was defending would hold through a repeat of the 38% month.

## Closing

Quesada had walked the finished-goods warehouse at 7:15 that morning, counting pallets of trays produced in January for a peak that had already passed. The shift supervisor asked what to run next week. The board's two quarters had started.
