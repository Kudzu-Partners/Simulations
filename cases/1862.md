# Maison Velluti: Cash in the Chain

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1862 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1862 — *Cash in the Chain* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Friday, 12 June 2026, Alessandra Bertoli read two numbers on the same page. The first: the CFO wanted EUR 30 million of working capital freed within two quarters, having floated the fastest route — stretch payment terms from 75 to 120 days across all 220 suppliers. The second: a notice from Conceria San Frediano, a Tuscan tannery supplying 22% of the company's certified leather, six weeks from a liquidity default and asking for faster payment or it would stop shipping. Bertoli, the company's Supply-Chain Finance Lead, had the same quarter to reconcile the two.

## The Protagonist

Bertoli, 43, had led Maison Velluti's supply-chain finance function since 2022, after eight years in corporate treasury at a Milan industrial group and two years advising manufacturers on receivables. She reported jointly to the CFO and the Chief Procurement Officer, with authority over financing terms but not sourcing decisions. She had joined believing that a fashion house's balance sheet and its supplier relationships were the same problem seen two ways.

## The Decision

Bertoli had to design, within the quarter, a single working-capital programme reconciling the CFO's cash target, San Frediano's survival and the sustainability codes two luxury clients had just tightened — against three deadlines closing within the same thirteen weeks.

## The Company and its Setting

Maison Velluti S.p.A., founded in 1968 and headquartered in Florence, made leather goods, footwear and ready-to-wear for its own label and, under contract, two global luxury brands. It reported EUR 540 million in 2025 revenue at a 16% EBITDA margin and employed 1,900 people. Its 220 suppliers — tanneries, textile mills, hardware makers, small artisanal workshops — were 70% Italian SMEs concentrated in Tuscany and Veneto (Exhibit 1). Roughly 35% of supplier spend went to small Tuscan workshops with little access to financing: the tier behind the "Made in Italy" provenance its clients paid for. Days payable outstanding stood at 75.

## The Situation

Three pressures had converged on the same quarter. The CFO's mandate — EUR 30 million freed within two quarters — was the most visible, and the CFO had floated extending terms to 120 days across the full supplier base as the fastest route. San Frediano, one of only two tanneries certified chrome-free and animal-welfare compliant, supplied 22% of the company's certified leather and had six weeks of cash runway left. The two luxury clients whose contracts were worth EUR 190 million had tightened their sustainability and traceability codes — chemical management, certified leather, living-wage audits — with the compliance audit falling in the same quarter; the certification upgrades required were estimated at roughly EUR 200,000 per small workshop, more than most of the artisanal tier could fund alone. Extending terms uniformly would free the EUR 30 million, but it also pointed toward San Frediano's default inside the same window and a likely audit failure across the fragile tier — with EUR 190 million of contracts at risk. If Bertoli changed nothing, the target went unmet, San Frediano's clock ran out regardless, and the audit arrived with the upgrades unfunded.

## What Bertoli Controlled

Bertoli's programme took shape across four decisions, one per quarter of the review.

The first was a posture toward the CFO's cash target. She could stretch terms only with the handful of large mills financially able to absorb them, leaving the smaller workshops on their original terms; extend payment terms to 120 days for every one of the 220 suppliers at once, rather than adjusting terms supplier by supplier; leave payables untouched entirely and instead pursue the same cash target through tighter inventory management and faster collection of customer receivables; or pursue it through a supply-chain-finance instrument, in which Maison Velluti extended its own payment terms while a financing partner paid suppliers early. She also chose how to segment the supply base — by spend alone, addressing the largest contracts first regardless of any other factor; by criticality, financial fragility and how easily each supplier could be substituted; or not at all, applying one uniform approach to every one of the 220 suppliers — and set, as a percentage, how explicitly her team stated that the cheapest route to EUR 30 million could put EUR 190 million of contracts at risk.

The second decision was the financing instrument itself (Exhibit 2). Dynamic discounting used Maison Velluti's own cash to pay each supplier early, in exchange for an agreed discount on the invoice. Classic factoring left each individual supplier to arrange its own sale of receivables to a financier, on whatever terms that financier offered. Reverse factoring routed early payment to suppliers through a bank, drawing on Maison Velluti's own credit rating, while the company itself extended its payment terms. Direct renegotiation extended Maison Velluti's payment terms with each supplier one at a time, without involving any financing partner at all. She also set an effective days-payable figure anywhere between 75 and the CFO's proposed 120, priced the financing rate purely by each supplier's own credit standing, giving the best terms to the financially strongest mills; as one flat rate applied to every supplier in the programme, regardless of size or sustainability performance; or on a sliding scale tied to each supplier's sustainability performance, so meeting the new client codes earned a cheaper rate — and committed any amount of a EUR 12 million ESG and supplier-finance fund toward onboarding and certification costs.

The third decision concerned specific suppliers and the sustainability codes. On San Frediano: let its situation play out on its own timeline while fast-tracking qualification of the one other certified tannery as an alternate source; bring it into the early-payment programme immediately, paired with a certification-upgrade plan for the tannery; or exit the relationship altogether and rely on that second tannery as the sole certified source. On the smaller workshops: build a simplified onboarding path with field support and a platform adapted to a micro-supplier's scale; route every supplier through the same standard bank platform already used by the large mills; or limit the programme's eligibility to suppliers who already met the bank's existing criteria. On the codes themselves: part-fund the certification upgrades directly while mandating the remaining requirements from each supplier on a noticeably tighter deadline; fund the roughly EUR 200,000 per workshop of certification cost through the sustainability-linked discounts, on a realistic timeline; or set a single compliance deadline tied to the client audit and require every supplier to meet it using its own resources. She also set, as a share of the fund, how much was steered to the fragile artisanal tier rather than the stronger mills.

The fourth decision was how to present the finished programme and who would own it afterward. On the programme itself: present a single reconciled case in which the financing instrument, the supplier decisions and the certification funding were shown to work together against all three deadlines; prioritise closing any remaining gap to the EUR 30 million target, extending days payable further if the number came up short; or prioritise supplier survival across the board, even if that meant asking the CFO for more time and finishing under target. To the CFO, San Frediano's owner and the client's audit lead: lead the presentation with the cash figure achieved this quarter, and keep the supplier and compliance detail to a brief closing mention; present the plan as fully resolved and assume that suppliers and the audit would fall into line on their own schedule; or present the plan as a single reconciled outcome — the cash freed, San Frediano's status and the certification timeline — naming every trade-off in the room. Governance afterward: name a standing owner for the programme spanning finance and sourcing, with supplier scorecards and an annual rate review; leave ongoing monitoring to treasury's cash reporting, with sourcing handling supplier relationships separately; or treat the crisis as closed once the quarter ended and redeploy the team to other priorities. Finally, she set, as a percentage, how firmly she would defend her recommendation.

## Closing

Bertoli set the CFO's EUR 30 million target, San Frediano's six-week runway and the audit date on one page. All three closed within the same thirteen weeks, and none of it yet reconciled.

**Exhibit 2. The four working-capital financing instruments under consideration**

| Instrument | How it worked | Effect on Maison Velluti's cash | Effect on suppliers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reverse factoring / approved-payables finance | A bank paid suppliers early, drawing on Maison Velluti's own credit rating, while the company extended its own payment terms | Frees cash by extending the buyer's DPO | Suppliers paid early, using the buyer's credit rating |
