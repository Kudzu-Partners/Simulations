# Make It Stick: The Sepsis Pathway at Brookfield NHS Trust

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1946 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1946 — *Make It Stick* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 2 February 2026, Ruth Menzies set two documents side by side on the table of the Brookfield boardroom. The first was the closing evaluation of a six-month pilot: a clinician-designed digital sepsis-detection and response pathway, run on two wards, had cut sepsis mortality by 18% and average length of stay by 1.4 days. The second was a single sheet the programme office kept and rarely circulated. It listed fourteen digital tools piloted at Brookfield NHS Foundation Trust over five years. Every one had produced a result worth writing up. Eleven were still switched on at six months. All fourteen were dead by twenty-four.

The board had given Menzies one attempt at the fifteenth and a date to hit. The trust-wide rollout business case had to clear the board on 30 April 2026. What the board would then judge, and had said so in writing, was not the launch but the twenty-four-month sustainability review.

## The Protagonist

Ruth Menzies, 46, had been Brookfield's Clinical Innovation Lead since 2023. She had trained as a nurse, run an acute medical ward for six years, and moved into quality improvement after a deteriorating-patient audit she wrote was adopted across both hospital sites. She had not built the sepsis pathway. That was the work of Dr. Ahmed, a consultant in acute medicine who had designed it, run the pilot on his own wards, and by common account carried it there personally. Menzies had been given the pathway, the money, and the register of fourteen.

## The Decision

Menzies had to settle four questions in sequence, and each one narrowed the next.

The first was diagnostic and had four defensible readings of the same register. The evidence explanation held that the fourteen tools had not worked as well off the pilot wards as on them, and that the trial conditions had flattered every one of them. The technology explanation held that the software had been clumsy and that the integrations had broken as soon as they left a single ward. The funding explanation held that each tool had died in the month its pilot grant ended, and that the funding cliff was the whole story. The behavioural-and-structural explanation held that all fourteen had shipped with a guideline and none with an owner or a recurring budget line. Menzies had to commit to one.

She also had to name the single largest threat to the sepsis pathway itself, from four candidates: that the 44% of pilot nurses who used the tool inconsistently and the 18% who used a paper workaround would drift back once the attention moved elsewhere; that the 18% mortality reduction would not repeat beyond two wards; that the £1.8 million would never be released against the trust's deficit; or that the register overstated the danger for a pilot with this signal.

Then she had to define what "sustained at twenty-four months" would mean when the board tested it — the pathway still switched on in the electronic patient record; a clean go-live and consistent use above 60% at nine months; or 75% consistent use with a named clinical owner and a standing audit. Last, she had to decide how much of the programme's opening weeks to spend on that diagnosis before releasing a pound of the rollout money.

## The Company and its Setting

Brookfield NHS Foundation Trust was a 720-bed acute trust in the English Midlands, serving a population of 540,000 across two hospital sites and a community network, with 7,400 staff and an annual budget of £610 million. It ran a £12 million deficit. The finance director had already circulated a note saying that every line of the rollout ask would be challenged, and the Care Quality Commission, the sector's regulator, was due to look again at deteriorating-patient care.

## The Situation

The pilot had produced three groups of nurses rather than one. Across the two wards, 38% used the pathway consistently, 44% used it inconsistently, and 18% used a paper workaround. Trust-wide, the level of consistent use at which the benefit would actually be realised was 75% (see **Exhibit 1**).

Rollout had a price and a shape. Capital of £1.8 million covered integration with the electronic patient record, devices and training, with £420,000 a year to run it afterwards. Against a £12 million deficit, that was the ask Menzies had to carry to the board on 30 April.

Two gates sat between the board date and the verdict. At nine months, if consistent use was below 60%, central funding would be withdrawn and the pathway would revert to opt-in — the same reversion that appeared in the register beside most of the fourteen. At twenty-four months, the board would ask whether the pathway was still in routine use.

Menzies tracked four numbers, and had reported all four to the board. Consistent adoption stood at the pilot's 38%. A sustainability index, the programme office's own 0–100 estimate of the probability the pathway would still be in routine use at twenty-four months, stood at 20. A clinician engagement score, also 0–100, measuring staff support against resistance, stood at 40. The investment case — the annual cost-avoidance she could evidence against the £1.8 million plus £420,000 a year — stood at £0.0 million, because nothing had been costed yet.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was not dramatic. The pilot wards would keep their result while Dr. Ahmed stayed on them, consistent use trust-wide would stay near the pilot's 38%, the nine-month checkpoint would withdraw the funding, and the pathway would become the fifteenth line on the sheet.

## What Menzies Controlled

She committed the £1.8 million across five lines and had to allocate the envelope in full (see **Exhibit 2**). Alongside the money she set how the pathway would sit in the electronic patient record: as the default with a clinical override, alongside champions and feedback; hard-wired so that alerts could not be overridden; or left opt-in, carried by champions and training alone.

For the board she chose how to argue the money — pricing the clinical benefit out into freed bed-days, avoided litigation and mortality; putting the clinical benefit forward on its own terms; or presenting the pathway as a patient-safety obligation without a costed figure. She chose the route — staged as invest-to-save against adoption milestones, requested as central capital in full, or deferred to the next budget year. She chose whether to put a number on regulatory and reputational exposure, mention it without sizing it, or leave it out. And she set the adoption rate down to which she would commit that the case still held, knowing the finance director would ask what it was worth at 40% rather than 75%.

For the nine-month checkpoint she set ownership after the project team disbanded: a named clinical owner backed by a sepsis governance group, standing audit, record default and induction; Dr. Ahmed; or the project team extended a year with handover settled later. She set how the checkpoint would be presented: the evidence together with the mechanisms carrying use toward 75%; the gate declared cleared and the effort stood down; or the number defended as it stood. And she set her answer to a senior consultant who had begun disputing the algorithm's false-positive rate in open meetings and had said he would take his department out: put him on the review group and retune the thresholds while keeping the record default; hold the default and harden the alerts; or withdraw the default and let departments opt out. Beside that answer she recorded, on a nought-to-hundred scale the programme office used for commitments, how firmly she was undertaking to hold the record default and the pathway's clinical credibility together rather than trade one for the other.

## Closing

At 9:15 a.m. Menzies took the register of fourteen off the table and pinned it to the wall of the programme office where the team would see it. Eighty-seven days to the board. Nine months from go-live to the checkpoint.
