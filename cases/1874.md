# The Controller's Seat: A Controlling Opinion at Hellweg Antriebstechnik

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1874 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1874 — *The Controller's Seat* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Tuesday, September 22, 2026, Katrin Bergmann read the supervisory board's letter twice before she reopened the plan behind it. Two weeks earlier, President Stefan Reuter had presented the board a three-year plan for the Mobility Drives division: revenue up from €640 million to €1.3 billion, margin up to 14 percent, built on winning two electric-vehicle platform contracts that were not yet signed. The board liked the plan enough to consider releasing €180 million of capital for it — but wanted an independent controlling opinion first, due in three weeks.

Bergmann was the division's controller, and the plan was not hers to write or block. It was hers to test: what in Reuter's numbers was solid and what was hope; what the control system governing the plan should measure; where to set the budget targets under it; and what recommendation to put to the board, with Reuter in the room to hear her defend it.

## The Protagonist

Bergmann was 44 and had held the divisional-controller seat for Mobility Drives for three years, having come up through six years in Hellweg's group FP&A function and, before that, an auditor's training contract in Cologne. Her reporting line ran two ways: a dotted line to Reuter, whose division she served, and a solid line to the group CFO, who had ordered this opinion. She and Reuter had run three annual planning cycles together without a real collision. This was the first plan of his she had been asked to formally test rather than simply consolidate.

## The Decision

One opinion was due, but reaching it meant answering four questions in turn: how to read the plan and the forecasting history behind it; what the division's control system should measure once the plan was running, and how those measures should be used with Reuter; where to set the budget targets under the plan and how much contingency to build in; and how hard to challenge the plan, how to structure the €180 million release, and what to formally recommend to the board.

## The Company and its Setting

Hellweg Antriebstechnik AG was a Dortmund-headquartered maker of industrial drive systems, founded in 1958 and listed on the Frankfurt exchange's SDAX, with group revenue of €1.9 billion and an EBIT margin of 9.2 percent. Mobility Drives, at €640 million of current revenue, was the division the group had marked as its growth engine for electrification. Reuter had run it for several years and, by reputation inside Hellweg, had the supervisory board's confidence; the plan he had just submitted had gone down well with the board two weeks earlier, before it reached divisional controlling for a second look.

## The Situation

The plan asked the division to add €660 million of revenue over three years, and 62 percent of that growth depended on the two unsigned EV contracts (Exhibit 1). Mobility Drives' own forecasting record over the prior three years had averaged ±22 percent error, and there was a group precedent the CFO had not forgotten: another division had once hit every milestone on its plan and still missed its final numbers by around 30 percent. Underneath the revenue bridge, two cost centres had submitted budgets that looked padded — costs set high and volumes set low against what the centres were likely to do — while the top-line targets built into the same plan were stretched thin in the other direction. Left as it stood, the plan would put €180 million in front of the board against a growth number that was majority-unsigned, from a division with a recent history of missing its own forecasts by close to a quarter.

## What Bergmann Controlled

On the revenue bridge, she could split the €660 million into a defensible committed base with the two unsigned contracts flagged separately as speculative upside; treat the contracts as probable and fold most of the growth into the base case; or pass the full €1.3 billion through as Reuter had presented it. Alongside that sat a read on the ±22 percent forecasting record itself: build a realistic-volume scenario against it as the outside view, treat this plan as an exception to that record given the shift to electrification, or set the forecasting question aside as a matter for sales rather than controlling. A third dial set how openly the opinion would name what she found on the two padded cost centres and the stretched top line, from saying very little to naming both in full, by evidence rather than blame.

The second question was what the division's control system should measure: a set combining lagging financial measures — revenue, EBIT margin, ROCE on the new capital — with a handful of leading indicators; the financial measures alone; or a wide dashboard of roughly forty metrics. Five candidate leading indicators were on the table, of which two or three would be chosen: pipeline conversion on the two EV platforms, platform-qualification milestones passed, time-to-ramp and scrap/quality on the new lines, monthly revenue actuals against plan, and the company's share price and analyst sentiment. She then had to decide how the chosen measures would be used with Reuter: reported up the line while he ran the strategy himself, tracked against target through the division's normal reporting cycle, or also raised with him directly and regularly to surface the unsigned-contract risk while the plan was still in motion.

Setting the budget targets raised a third set of choices. She could negotiate Reuter's top-down ambition against the cost centres' bottom-up numbers, stripping out padding and stretch on both sides; anchor on Reuter's own €1.3 billion figure and set the division to reach it; or anchor on the cost-centre budgets as submitted. Wherever she anchored, a contingency buffer against the risk that one or both contracts did not land could run anywhere from zero to sixty percent of the anticipated upside. The two padded cost centres could be re-based openly against evidence and comparable benchmarks without naming individuals, left as submitted without adjustment, or named publicly with the padding treated as a disciplinary matter. And the targets needed an achievement probability attached — a number from zero to a hundred that Bergmann would state to the board as her own estimate of the odds the realistic-scenario targets would be hit.

The opinion itself had to be delivered with Reuter present. On stance, she could act as a business partner and reshape the plan collaboratively, keeping her place in the division's strategy discussions; record the plan and its caveats in full and leave the judgment to the board; or contest the plan directly and publicly in the board session itself. She also had to decide how to release the €180 million: in tranches tied to milestones — committed volume first, a second tranche on the first EV contract signing, a third on qualification — in full up front, or held back entirely until both contracts were signed and volumes proven. Her formal recommendation could take one of four forms: approve the release with the tranche structure attached and recast Reuter's targets as conditional upside; approve the plan and the €1.3 billion figure as presented; send the plan back on the grounds that the targets and the capital case were not yet defensible; or set out the risk analysis and leave the choice to the board without a recommendation of her own. Whatever she chose, she still had to decide how firmly — on a scale of zero to a hundred — to defend it out loud, with Reuter beside her.

Across all four rounds, the opinion she produced would be judged on four things: whether the control system helped the board reach a rational decision, whether the board trusted the opinion as independent and right, whether she kept a genuine seat at the strategy table with Reuter, and how much of the €180 million request went out the door before the two contracts were actually signed (Exhibit 2).

## Closing

Bergmann closed the plan and opened a blank template for the controlling opinion. Three weeks remained until the board hearing. Reuter would be in the room when she presented it, the CFO would be reading every line before then, and the €180 million had not moved yet.
