# Nordlys Learning Lab: The Twenty-Minute Budget

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1998 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1998 — *Module in 20* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Tuesday 9 June 2026, Ingrid Halvorsen opened the brief Havstrøm Energi had sent to Nordlys Learning Lab overnight and found that it ran to a single page. Havstrøm wanted a flagship leadership module for its managers: twenty minutes long, mobile-first, live in six weeks. The last paragraph named two rival providers invited to build modules of their own, and the three measures on which all three would be compared at week six: how many learners finished, how much they could demonstrate they had learned, and how they rated the experience.

Havstrøm paid Nordlys NOK 9 million a year, a fifth of the Lab's revenue. Halvorsen, who ran programme design, had six weeks to decide how twenty minutes would be spent, and no way of buying a twenty-first.

## The Protagonist

Ingrid Halvorsen, 41, had led programme design at Nordlys Learning Lab since 2021. She had joined in 2016 from the corporate learning function of an Oslo shipping group, where she watched two-day workshops be replaced by things people did on a train. At Nordlys she built most of the micro-catalogue herself and set the house format its sixty modules still used: an explainer film, a short case, a knowledge check at the end. Her standing inside the school rested on that catalogue. The Havstrøm module would go out under the Lab's name and into the client's board pack under hers.

## The Decision

Halvorsen had to decide what the twenty minutes would try to teach, how they would be divided and in what order, what evidence of learning the module would produce and what that evidence cost in minutes, and what she would put in front of Havstrøm at week six.

## The Company and its Setting

Nordlys Learning Lab, the short-format executive-education unit of a Norwegian business school, was founded in Oslo in 2014. It reached roughly 14,000 learners a year through a blended platform and generated NOK 46 million of annual revenue at a 22% contribution margin. Its catalogue of sixty micro-modules was what the school sold into corporate accounts; Havstrøm was the largest of them.

A twenty-minute module had no slack in it. Four minutes of anything was a fifth of the product, and any block that overran took its minutes out of whatever came after it.

Three years of platform data said two things. Format mattered more than material: the same leadership content, cut two ways, had rated 4.6 out of 5 in one version and 3.1 in the other. And attention had a clock — passive engagement fell away sharply after roughly six or seven minutes, and learners on phones were answering email within about ninety seconds of a dull segment. The two rivals — a Stockholm micro-learning platform and a London content house — would be measured on the same three numbers.

## The Situation

The baseline was one Halvorsen could quote from memory, because it was what her own house format produced. On the catalogue's current standard leadership module, 50% of learners still had attention on the module through the middle, 48% could demonstrate the skill when tested afterwards, 62% reached the end, and the average rating was 3.4 out of 5 (**Exhibit 1**). Those figures had been acceptable for four years, because nobody had put them next to anyone else's.

Inside the Lab the argument had already started. The faculty content lead wanted the module to survey transformational leadership properly: a serious school did not sell a twenty-minute skim. The platform team wanted the budget spent on production — film, animation, a studio day — because Havstrøm's managers watched broadcast-quality video all day.

Two items were fixed before she began. Havstrøm's procurement team had written an accessibility standard into the contract renewal — captions, mobile layout, screen-reader support. And the Lab always ran a pilot cohort in week three and read the drop-off curve minute by minute, so Halvorsen would get one set of real numbers, arriving late, and had to settle in advance what she was willing to change when they came.

If Nordlys submitted the module its house format would produce, the six-week comparison would run with Exhibit 1's figures on the Nordlys line and two unknown sets beside them, with NOK 9 million attached.

## What Halvorsen Controlled

**The objective, the principle and the first ninety seconds.** She had to commit to one statement of what the module was for: a comprehensive treatment of transformational leadership across the major theories and models; one applicable skill provable inside twenty minutes; or a general awareness pass letting executives sample ideas and keep what resonated. She had to name the principle the design would obey — content coverage, on the argument that explaining more left more behind; experiential work with frequent retrieval, on the argument that doing and recalling were what fixed learning; or production quality, on the argument that film and animation carried attention by themselves. She had to fix the opening: the concept defined in the abstract, a slide listing what the module covered and in what order, or a sixty-second dilemma from the learner's own week. And she set, as a percentage, how far the material was written into each learner's own situation rather than presented generically.

**The storyboard.** Eighteen of the twenty minutes went into the storyboard; two were held back for the assessment (**Exhibit 2**). She divided the eighteen in whole minutes across four blocks that had to total exactly eighteen: passive content — explainer film and slides delivering the concept; experiential work — a case, a scenario decision or an application to the learner's own situation; retrieval and interaction — a mid-module check, a quick recall or a discussion prompt; and hook and transitions — the opening beat and the signposting between segments. She set the order too: the concept taught in full first and applied at the end if the minutes survived; a hook, a short content scaffold, the experience, then retrieval; or one long experiential block the whole module built on, with no separate content.

**Assessment and attention safeguards.** The module had to produce evidence of learning, and each option cost minutes. A sixty-second confidence self-rating on a one-to-five scale returned one self-reported number per learner; a six-minute knowledge quiz returned item-level results across the concept; a two-to-three-minute applied scenario returned the choice each learner made in a realistic situation. Halvorsen set the method and the minutes it consumed, anywhere from none to eight; the reserve was two, and anything past it put the module over the twenty-minute cap. She set segment length and interaction cadence: uniform five-minute segments back to back; one long uninterrupted segment for the main concept; or segments under four minutes with something for the learner to do between each. And she settled in advance how the design would answer the pilot's drop-off reading and the accessibility standard: leave the storyboard as it stood and treat both as the platform's problem, not the design's; add a dedicated accessibility segment and take its minutes out of the experiential block; or place a re-engagement beat wherever the pilot's curve dipped and build accessibility into every block, thinning each to pay for both.

**The week-six review.** She had to choose what to lead with: the volume of content and production packed into the twenty minutes; the satisfaction rating alone; or the three benchmark measures with the design choice that moved each. She had to write one page of rationale arguing that coverage was the proof of rigour, that every block mapped to a named learning principle, or that the design rested on the team's accumulated judgement. Havstrøm's head of leadership development would ask what she had traded away, and she could answer that the design involved no trade-off, that any shortfall turned on how far managers engaged with training, or that what she had cut was named and justified. And she had to decide how firmly to hold that position under challenge.

## Closing

Halvorsen drew twenty boxes across a sheet of A4, one for each minute. The pilot cohort was booked for week three and the Havstrøm review for week six. Sixty modules in the catalogue had been built the same way; this one would be measured against two providers she had never seen work.
