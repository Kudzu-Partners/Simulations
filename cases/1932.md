# Café Cordillera: The Relaunch of Cordillera Verde

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1932 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1932 — *Brand Builder* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

Two documents lay side by side on the desk in Heredia, where Valeria Solís had set them on Monday, April 6, 2026, her first morning as Chief Marketing Officer of Café Cordillera S.A. The first was the sourcing audit. Every kilogram of green coffee in the Cordillera Verde line came from certified cooperatives in the Tarrazú highlands, and the premium paid to those farmers above the reference price had been verified for the seventh consecutive year. The second was the quarterly brand tracker. Asked whether they recognised Cordillera Verde, 31% of Costa Rican coffee buyers said yes. Asked the same question about the multinational that had launched a campaign called Planet Positive eleven weeks earlier, 58% said yes. On the tracker's sustainability-perception scale, where 100 described a brand consumers believed was doing everything it could, Cordillera Verde scored 38.

The board had given her four quarters. By the review it wanted Cordillera Verde established as the region's trusted sustainable-coffee brand, on one condition it would not negotiate: contribution margin, at 22.0% of revenue, could not fall below 18%. How the brand got there was hers to choose. Her budget was CRC 480 million, to cover the repositioning, the campaign and every colón of media.

## The Protagonist

Solís, 44, had spent eleven years in brand roles at a regional dairy group and three running marketing for a Central American retail chain before Café Cordillera recruited her. She had never worked in coffee. What the board had bought, the chairman told her, was the dairy relaunch: a mid-tier brand that held its shelf price through two years of private-label expansion. Her variable pay was tied to the same measures the board would read at the review.

## The Decision

Solís had to commit, quarter by quarter, to four things that could not be unwound: who Cordillera Verde was for, what the campaign would claim, where the ₡480M went, and how the result would be put to the board. The first commitment fell in the opening quarter and the last at the review, and none of them could breach the 18% floor or the budget.

## The Company and its Setting

Café Cordillera S.A., founded in Heredia in 2009, roasted and sold premium and mainstream coffee into Costa Rican and Central American retail. It employed 140 people and posted 2025 revenue of CRC 9,600 million at a gross margin of 38%; after trade spend, contribution margin came to 22.0%. Cordillera Verde, a mid-premium roast, was the flagship, sold through supermarkets, convenience chains, foodservice and a subscription site that shipped direct to households.

Two figures framed the competitive position (see **Exhibit 1**). Planet Positive had taken four points of category share in its first quarter, on a sourcing base that certified a minority of the multinational's volume. And Cordillera Verde's shelf price, 35% above private label in 2023, now stood 22% above it, because shoppers had traded down and the trade had narrowed the gap to hold them.

## The Situation

Four numbers would be read at the review, and Solís would see all four move quarter by quarter. Aided awareness — the share of coffee buyers who recognised the brand when prompted — opened at 31%. The brand-equity index, a composite the tracker built from recognition, meaning and stated willingness to pay and reported on a 0–100 scale, opened at 42. Sustainability perception, on the same 0–100 scale, opened at 38, against sourcing that was fully certified. Contribution margin opened at 22.0% against the 18% floor. The tracker also carried a return measure on the campaign spend, and a running meter showed how much of the ₡480M had been committed: at the start, none of it.

No covenant was about to trip and no listing was at risk. If Solís changed nothing, the tracker would keep printing 31% against 58%, the price premium would keep compressing toward the private label's shelf price, and equity and perception would arrive at the review roughly where they sat in April. The board had said what happened then: Cordillera Verde would be repositioned downmarket as a private-label fighter, and the premium would not come back.

## What Solís Controlled

Each quarter Solís committed one set of decisions and could not revisit it.

The first quarter fixed the position. She had to name the segment the brand would be built for — the conscious premium drinkers who already paid up for quality; the whole coffee-buying population, without narrowing to any group; the price-sensitive shoppers moving to private label; or the buyers the multinational already had. She had to name the point of difference: sustainability as the brand's meaning, on the ground that no other Costa Rican coffee sourced 100% from certified cooperatives at a verified farmer premium; the taste of the Tarrazú roast, a mid-premium cup the tracker scored above both rivals, with sourcing as a supporting line; the best premium coffee for the money, held up directly against the private label; or the roastery's Costa Rican origin, a heritage claim the multinational could not make either, running from the first Heredia roast in 2009. And she had to settle how much documented evidence the position would rest on, anywhere between one anchored entirely in verifiable specifics and one anchored in general intent.

The second quarter fixed what the campaign said. Four claim sets were available: the specific version — *100% sourced from certified Tarrazú cooperatives, farmer premium paid* — naming the number and the standard behind it; the broad version, *the planet's most sustainable coffee*, category language that shifted perception faster than any qualified sentence; the general version, *responsibly made*, carrying no figure that would have to be defended; or a claim led by taste and premium quality, with sustainability held to one short line and the argument kept on ground the brand already won. Each needed a mechanism to carry it: farmer-level traceability with third-party certification and a published annual impact report; the certification seal on pack and nothing more; farmer storytelling without certification detail; or no mechanism, with the creative carrying the claim alone. Solís also had to set the distance between what the campaign asserted and what the file could document. Her agency put both edges plainly in its brief: pushing past the evidence invited a complaint, a journalist and a collapse in credibility, and staying far inside it left the certified-sourcing story unsaid.

The third quarter committed the money across five channels (see **Exhibit 2**), and settled how the media team would use the AI personalisation layer it had licensed for audience segmentation and mix optimisation — as a co-pilot, proposing the segments and the mix with every recommendation checked against Cordillera's actual buyer file; unused, the plan built by hand by planners who had worked the category for years; or as the planner of record, the mix it produced run as it came out, in a fraction of the planning time.

The fourth quarter was the review itself. Solís had to choose how to present the four measures: reporting what ₡480M had actually moved and naming the one that moved least; presenting the full set at the levels the mandate had implied — awareness up twenty points, equity up eighteen, category-leading sustainability perception and a positive return on the campaign; or leading with whichever measure had moved most and keeping the rest brief. She had to state how the price premium would be defended against the private-label trade-down: on the substantiated sustainability meaning; by matching private label on price, holding the volume now and accepting that the premium eroded; or by leaving it alone, on the expectation that awareness growth would restore it without a price move. And she had to decide how firmly to defend the position, the campaign and the media plan as a single connected argument rather than three separate ones.

The record of those commitments filled in a row at a time — the quarter, the move she had made, and the four measures as they stood at the end of it: awareness, equity, sustainability perception and margin.

## Closing

Solís squared the two documents into a single pile, the audit on top, and pulled the phone across to call in the brand team. Two numbers were fixed before she said a word to them — 480 and 18 — and the review was four quarters out.
