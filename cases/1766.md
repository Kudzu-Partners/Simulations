# Brightwell Insurance: Two Models, One Firm

On a Thursday morning in April 2026, Grace Halloran, chief executive of Brightwell Insurance plc, opened the board pack her chairman had sent overnight. Swift Cover Direct, an app-only motor and home insurer, had just passed 600,000 customers with prices Brightwell could not match, and new business among Brightwell's under-35 drivers had fallen 22% over the past year. The instruction at the top of the pack was a single line: over the next four board quarters, Halloran was to decide whether Brightwell would run a second business model — a direct-digital business — alongside its broker-led core, and if so, how, without ever letting the combined ratio cross the board's 97% ceiling.

Halloran would report her decisions to the board one quarter at a time, against a scorecard the board read every quarter: the combined underwriting result, in pounds, for the core business and any new venture together; the combined ratio measured against the 97% ceiling; how many customers the new venture had won; what share of those customers had come from Brightwell's own brokers rather than from the wider market; and an index, running from calm to open conflict, of how the broker network and the new venture were getting along.

## The Protagonist

Halloran had run Brightwell for four years, promoted internally after a decade leading the commercial-lines underwriting team that had held the company's combined ratio near 94% through two hard markets. She had built her reputation on underwriting discipline: quote carefully, price the risk, and never let volume growth buy a worse book. Her own incentive plan, like every Brightwell executive's, paid out against the combined ratio and against growth in gross written premium — two numbers a second business model could move in opposite directions at once. She had not built a digital business before, and neither, for that matter, had anyone currently on her executive team.

## The Decision

Each quarter, Halloran had to decide something different. In the first, whether Brightwell would compete with a second, direct-digital model at all, or continue as a single, broker-led company. If she chose to compete, the second quarter required her to decide how independent the new digital unit would be from the broker core, and which of the core's assets it would draw on rather than rebuild. The third quarter required her to put money, people and her own attention behind whatever she had built, inside the margin the board would tolerate. The fourth required her to respond to developments in the business and defend the whole plan to the board in a matter of minutes.

## The Company and Its Setting

Brightwell Insurance plc had been founded in 1988 and had grown into a broker-led motor and home insurer writing £1.2 billion of gross premium a year, distributed almost entirely through a national network of brokers who accounted for 88% of everything Brightwell sold. That distribution model, and the underwriting discipline built around it, had produced a combined ratio of 94% — a six-point underwriting margin on £1.2 billion of annual premium — in a market where many competitors ran closer to break-even.

Swift Cover Direct sold motor and home cover through an app, with no broker commission and an expense ratio near 14%, against Brightwell's roughly 31%. That cost gap let it price about 18% below Brightwell on comparable policies, and it was winning disproportionately among younger, digitally native customers — the segment in which Brightwell's own new-business volume had fallen 22% over the previous year (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The board's constraint was explicit: whatever Halloran did, the combined ratio — core business and any new venture, measured together — could not exceed 97% at any point across the four quarters. A credible digital build was expected to cost in the region of £45 million over two years, funded from the same balance sheet that funded the broker business, and it would compete for a limited supply of digital talent and for Halloran's own time, which the broker business also needed.

If Brightwell changed nothing — kept selling only through brokers, at the current 94% combined ratio — this year's margin stayed intact. What was not protected was the under-35 book: with new business in that segment already down 22% and Swift Cover Direct still growing, the segment Brightwell would inherit as its future customer base was the one it was currently losing fastest.

## What Halloran Controlled

The first decision was whether to compete with a second model at all. Halloran could decline the second model altogether, keeping Brightwell as a single broker-led business and protecting this year's margin without changing course. She could authorize a quiet pilot: a limited, low-commitment test of a direct-digital offer, run to gather real evidence on price and demand before either committing further capital and people or standing down. Or she could commit fully, standing up a second business model to run alongside the broker core for as long as the board allowed it.

If she went beyond declining, her second decision was how independent the new unit should be — a dial, not a switch, running from a digital operation fully embedded inside the existing broker profit-and-loss account, to a fully separate brand and legal entity with its own governance. Alongside that dial, she chose, asset by asset, whether the new unit would draw on four things the core business already had: 38 years of pricing and claims data, the balance sheet and capital that funded underwriting, the Brightwell brand name itself, and the underwriting and claims operations that processed every policy and every claim (see **Exhibit 2**).

Her third decision was how to resource whatever she had built, inside the board's margin ceiling. She could commit up to £45 million of build capital in the current year, move up to 120 digital specialists onto the venture, and direct up to 80% of her own and her leadership team's attention toward it — three separate dials, each of which could be set high, low, or anywhere between. Alongside them, she had to state how much cannibalization she would tolerate: what share of the new venture's customers she was willing to see come from Brightwell's own existing broker book, rather than from customers Brightwell had never had before, up to a stated cap of 90%.

Her fourth decision came at the board meeting that closed the year, after the broker network pushed back against the new venture and Swift Cover Direct cut its price again. She would choose among three responses: push further into the digital build despite the pressure, add further separation between the two models to protect both from it, or pull the new unit back toward the core organization. Independently of that choice, she could offer Brightwell's brokers a share of revenue earned on direct-channel policies, a step that would cost margin in exchange for calming a channel that wrote most of the company's premium.

## Closing

Halloran closed the board pack. She had four quarters, a combined ratio that could not cross 97%, and a decision the board wanted stated in a single sentence at the next meeting: was Brightwell going to compete with a second business model, or not. She picked up her pen.
