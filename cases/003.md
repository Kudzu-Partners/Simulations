## Abstract

Latina Consumer Brands managed three Mexican personal-care brands with sharply different profiles. VIVE, the Cash Cow, held 28% share of the mass body-wash segment on a quarterly baseline of 1,050,000 units at USD 8, a 45% gross margin and an equity score of 72. NATIVA, the Star, held 8% of the premium natural body-wash segment on 178,000 units at USD 18, a 62% gross margin and an equity score of 81. FORTE, the Dog, held 4% of the mid-tier men's body-wash segment on 109,000 units at USD 11, a 42% gross margin, an equity score of 54 and a Q4 2025 contribution margin of negative USD 110 thousand. A new Category Director, Isabela Quintero, inherited the three-brand portfolio in January 2026 with a quarterly marketing budget of USD 5 million and full P&L accountability. The case puts Quintero at her desk the Friday before the Q1 go-to-market plan locks, deciding (a) how to split the USD 5M across the brands, (b) how deep to run trade promotion on VIVE and on NATIVA, (c) how much of NATIVA's non-trade money to put behind brand building and how much of its volume to steer direct-to-consumer, and (d) which of three treatments to apply to FORTE — harvest, maintain, or a turnaround bet.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate trade-promotion as a short-run volume lever with medium-run equity cost, and quantify the compounding.
2. Analyze brand-portfolio allocations against a full P&L accountability view rather than a BCG-style quadrant description.
3. Recommend a structured decision on a loss-making brand and articulate the hurdle it must pass under each treatment (harvest, maintain, turnaround).
4. Design a direct-to-consumer pilot for a premium brand and assess the cannibalization and channel-conflict risks.
5. Defend an allocation recommendation under challenge from sales, supply chain, and corporate finance.

On the afternoon of **January 30, 2026**, **Isabela Quintero** sat at the long glass desk of the Latina Consumer Brands office in Polanco, Mexico City, with four printed one-pagers laid out in a row. The first three were brand briefs for VIVE, NATIVA, and FORTE. The fourth was the Q4 2025 contribution-margin report, annotated in red where FORTE had returned negative USD 110,000 on a national distribution base. A fifth document, stapled shut, was the email from the General Manager that had landed at 11:47 that morning: *"Q1 plan must be on my desk Monday at 9. Your call on FORTE."*

---

## The Protagonist

Isabela Quintero, 36, had been named Category Director three weeks earlier after seven years at Latina as brand manager for NATIVA and, before that, as trade-marketing lead for VIVE. She had an MBA from a regional school and a reputation inside the company for two things: a quantitative streak unusual in marketing ("she brings models to brand reviews") and an unwillingness to defend trade-spend escalation without a return calculation. She had inherited the three-brand portfolio from a predecessor who had resigned in December after three consecutive quarters of flat revenue. Her compensation carried a quarterly bonus tied 50/50 to category revenue and category contribution margin.

---

## The Decision

Quintero had to finalize, by 9:00 a.m. on Monday, February 2, a single Q1 plan. The plan had five dimensions:

1. **Budget split:** set VIVE's share of the USD 5 million (between 10% and 70%) and NATIVA's (between 10% and 60%). **FORTE takes the remainder** — the finance system allocated the full budget every quarter, so any money not assigned to the two larger brands landed on the Dog whether Quintero intended it or not.
2. **Trade depth on VIVE (0–80%) and on NATIVA (0–70%):** the share of each brand's allocation routed through retailer trade-promotion, as distinct from brand-building — media, packaging, R&D-adjacent investment. FORTE had no separate trade line; its split was fixed by the treatment chosen in (5).
3. **NATIVA brand-building split (0–100%):** of NATIVA's non-trade money, the proportion behind equity-building work rather than general consumer marketing.
4. **NATIVA direct-to-consumer share (0–25%):** percentage of NATIVA's forecast Q1 volume targeted through direct channels.
5. **FORTE treatment:** *Harvest* (minimal support, roughly 15% of its allocation spent, volume runs down), *Maintain* (full support at a 35% trade split, the standing posture), or a *Turnaround Bet* (20% trade, the balance behind reformulation, repackaging and a 12% price increase).

The General Manager would review the plan Monday morning. Sales VP Carla Mendoza had asked, late Friday, for a copy "in advance" — Quintero had not yet replied.

---

## Company Background

Latina Consumer Brands was a Mexican personal-care company founded in 2003, with 2025 revenues of USD 120 million across body-wash, hair-care, and oral-care. The body-wash category was the largest P&L and was the category Quintero now owned. The company was majority-owned by a regional consumer-focused private-equity fund that had acquired it in 2022 at a 9x EBITDA multiple; the GP's hold period was expected to close between late 2027 and mid-2028.

Latina operated three manufacturing lines at a single plant in Querétaro, distributed through national grocery (Walmart, Soriana, Chedraui), regional grocery, pharmacy (Farmacias del Ahorro), and — newly, via a NATIVA pilot — direct online. Sales and trade teams reported to Carla Mendoza, VP Sales. R&D and packaging reported to a shared COO function. Marketing, which Quintero now ran, held brand P&L accountability — a 2024 reorganization that was not yet fully accepted by the sales function.

## Industry and Market Context

The Mexican body-wash category was worth roughly USD 1.1 billion at retail in 2025, growing at 3.5% year over year. Three price tiers dominated. The mass tier (USD 4–10) was the largest at roughly USD 620M and grew at population rate; retailer-private-label had risen from 12% to 21% share in five years. The premium tier (USD 15–30) was smaller at USD 170M but growing at 11% per year, driven by natural-ingredient positioning; direct-to-consumer had begun to take share, reaching an estimated 8% of the premium tier. The mid-tier (USD 10–14) was the most contested space with the highest category-level trade intensity and the slowest organic growth.

Retailer bargaining power in grocery had strengthened. Monthly trade-promotion windows had moved from three-week to two-week cycles; lost-listing risk on brands with flat share had risen materially. Promotional response differed sharply by tier: Latina's internal work put VIVE's volume uplift at 1.3% for every point of trade intensity and NATIVA's at 0.9%, the premium buyer being materially less deal-sensitive. Against that lift the brand-equity team had quantified a compounding cost. Above 30% trade intensity, VIVE's baseline volume eroded by approximately 0.4% per point per quarter; above 25%, NATIVA's eroded by 0.3%. Below those thresholds the effect switched off entirely. The erosion applied to the following quarter's base and compounded from there, so it never appeared in the quarter that caused it — which is why two years of escalation on VIVE had shown up in the numbers as nothing worse than a slow drift in the equity panel.

## The Situation

### How we got here

VIVE had been the company's engine for twelve years. Its 28% share of the mass segment had been defended through escalating trade-promotion intensity — from 28% of brand spend in 2023 to 41% in 2025. The brand had generated revenue and margin every quarter. Its equity score, tracked by an external panel on a 0–100 scale, had declined from 79 in 2023 to 72 in Q4 2025. The decline had begun after Quintero's predecessor, under sales-team pressure, had authorized a five-point lift in VIVE trade during the 2024 holiday window.

NATIVA had been Quintero's own project. Since 2021 the brand had grown from a 3% to an 8% share of the premium segment. A direct-to-consumer pilot launched in Q3 2025 had reached 6% of NATIVA volume with a 38% contribution margin — roughly 11 points above the channel-blended brand average — but had triggered a letter from Walmart's category buyer warning of a "channel-conflict review" in Q2 2026.

FORTE had been acquired in 2019 from a smaller Mexican company as a bolt-on for USD 4M. The brand had never returned above 5% share. In 2024 and 2025 it had run at a combined cumulative contribution margin of negative USD 380,000.

### What Quintero knew

A five-page internal model had quantified, for each brand, the approximate elasticities of unit volume to trade intensity and the behaviour of brand equity under investment and under promotional pressure. Equity moved on a lag: brand-building spend in one quarter raised the score in the *next*, by up to six points on VIVE and eight on NATIVA, while trade spend depleted it in the quarter it was committed — 0.07 points per point of trade intensity on VIVE, 0.05 on NATIVA. Equity in turn fed volume, though with a dampening exponent, so a brand could show a rising equity score and a falling volume base at the same time.

The direct channel had its own arithmetic. Up to 15% of NATIVA volume, going direct added to both equity and the baseline. Above 15%, the model flagged retailer retaliation — a step change of roughly 22% off NATIVA's shelf volume — and, once triggered, the penalty did not lift until direct share was pulled back to 10% or below. Overshooting by a single point therefore cost more than the entire programme was worth.

The model had known weaknesses at the edges — it was unstable above 60% trade intensity and did not model competitor response at all. But for the Q1 window it was directionally usable.

### What Quintero did not know

Whether Walmart would, in fact, convert the channel-conflict warning into a delisting threat. Whether a competitor launch (rumoured, from Unilever's regional unit) would hit VIVE's shelf space in Q2. Whether the private-equity owner would read a harvested FORTE as discipline or as an admission that the 2019 bolt-on had failed, with the hold-period tail already in sight.

### What others were saying

**Carla Mendoza**, VP Sales, pushed for maximum VIVE trade intensity and a "steady-hand" NATIVA plan. "Walmart rotates its shelf set in March. Miss the window, lose the facing. Stop romanticising equity."

**Diego Hernández**, CMO, pushed for a NATIVA-forward plan. "NATIVA is a twelve-year option, not a twelve-week lever. We fund the brand that earns the multiple at exit."

**Rodrigo Alcántara**, CFO, pushed for a disciplined FORTE exit. "Harvest FORTE and give it nothing. Every peso we route to that brand this quarter comes back as fifteen centavos of support spend against a volume line that is going away regardless. Starve it to zero and it is the only quarter in three years FORTE returns cash. The number is small and it is real."

---

## Options Under Consideration

Quintero had narrowed Q1 into three coherent packages.

- **Option A — Defend-the-Engine (the Mendoza plan):** 60% VIVE, 28% NATIVA, 12% FORTE (USD 3.0M / 1.4M / 0.6M). VIVE trade intensity 50%; NATIVA trade 20% with the standing 60% brand-building split; NATIVA DTC held at 6%; FORTE harvested. The strongest quarter of the three: modelling put Q1 portfolio contribution margin at USD 4.37 million, with VIVE alone at USD 3.08M. VIVE equity dips to 68.5 in Q1 before the brand-building spend lands. VIVE's volume base, however, begins to compound down — 966,000 units by Q2 against 1,050,000 at the start.

- **Option B — Rebalance and Decide (the Hernández plan):** 44% VIVE, 46% NATIVA, 10% FORTE (USD 2.2M / 2.3M / 0.5M). VIVE trade intensity held at 30% — the level below which the base does not erode at all; NATIVA trade 18% with the brand-building split lifted to 75%; NATIVA DTC lifted to 12%; FORTE put on the turnaround bet. Q1 portfolio contribution margin falls to USD 3.28 million, roughly a quarter below Option A, and NATIVA's own contribution margin lands at almost exactly zero — USD 9,000 — because USD 2.3M is close to the whole of the gross margin NATIVA generates in a quarter. VIVE's base is untouched and both equity trajectories turn upward.

- **Option C — Cut-and-Concentrate (the Alcántara plan):** 50% VIVE, 50% NATIVA, 0% FORTE (USD 2.5M / 2.5M / nil). FORTE harvested on no budget at all — which is the only configuration in which the Dog returns its best number, USD 443,000 in Q1, because harvest support scales with the allocation it is given. VIVE trade 35%; NATIVA trade 18%, DTC 10%. Q1 portfolio contribution margin USD 3.15 million, the lowest of the three, because NATIVA at USD 2.5M is pushed past the point where its own gross margin covers its support and returns a contribution margin of **negative USD 194,000**. Channel-conflict risk at Walmart remains open.

(See **Exhibit 3** for the modeled Q1–Q4 contribution margin and brand-equity trajectories under each option and **Exhibit 4** for the FORTE payback analysis under Harvest, Maintain, and Turnaround.)

---

Quintero stacked the one-pagers, slid them into her bag, and opened the Q1 planning file on her laptop. The General Manager would open the file at 9:00 a.m. Monday. She had until then to choose among Option A, Option B, and Option C — and, for FORTE, among Harvest, Maintain, and the Turnaround Bet. The category revenue number would be hers either way. The equity number, five quarters out, would also be hers — whether anyone asked for it by then or not.
