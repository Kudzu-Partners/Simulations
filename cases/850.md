# Andes Tech Trading: Three Files and Six Business Days

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-850 · **Companion simulation:** Express 850 — *DIAN Customs — Imports, Exports & Tariff Regime* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, April 27, 2026, at 8:10 in the morning, Laura Camacho Rincón opened three notices from Colombia's tax and customs authority, the Dirección de Impuestos y Aduanas Nacionales (DIAN),. The first flagged an inbound import declaration for physical inspection. The second opened an audit of three import declarations filed in 2025. The third held a container of coffee bound for Hamburg. By nine, the chief financial officer of Andes Tech Trading S.A.S. had called a war room and put her in charge of it.

Camacho, the company's import and export manager, had six business days. The inspection window on the flagged shipment closed on Wednesday, May 6. Before then she had to decide which of the three fights Andes Tech would lead with, how hard it would push back, and what the board would hear from her once the files were closed. The customs broker said "we'll fix it." The tax and compliance lead said "we'll appeal." Nobody had yet asked which fights were worth fighting.

## The Protagonist

Camacho was thirty-eight. She had joined Andes Tech in 2019 as import coordinator, after five years at a freight forwarder in Cartagena, and had run the import and export book since 2023. She knew the tariff lines of the company's top twenty products by heart and the people at the broker by first name. She had handled routine inspections, never three disputes with the authority at once, and never one that reached the board.

## The Decision

The chief financial officer, Hernando Quintero Salas, gave her a short brief. The war room would sit four times before the board readout, and at each sitting she would settle one main question and one supporting move. First, which fight led and what posture the team took. Second, how the company would defend the tariff classification of its lead product. Third, whether to accept or contest DIAN's reconstructed valuation, and how to correct the filing in Muisca, the authority's import system. Fourth, how to free the Hamburg coffee and what to change in the relationship with the customs broker so that it did not happen again.

## The Company and its Setting

Andes Tech Trading was founded in Bogotá in 2016 by two former procurement managers. It imported industrial electronics, sensors and connectivity modules from Shenzhen and Seoul through Buenaventura and Cartagena, about 70% of revenue, and consolidated specialty coffee and processed cacao for buyers in Europe and the United States. It had 42 employees, 2025 revenue of COP 38,600 million and net income of about COP 2,860 million (see Exhibit 1).

About 58% of its imported products sat in the two chapters of the Harmonized System (HS) that covered machinery and electrical equipment. In those chapters two neighbouring subheadings could carry the same headline tariff and still differ in origin paperwork, in the base for value-added tax (VAT) and in the audit trail they left behind. Filings went through an external customs brokerage firm; Andes Tech employed one in-house import coordinator. Before DIAN, the importer, not the broker, carried primary liability.

## The Situation

Each file ran on its own clock (see Exhibit 2).

The flagged shipment was three containers whose lead item was a multi-protocol industrial gateway. Andes Tech had declared it as telecommunication apparatus under HS 8517.62, as it had on 14 earlier declarations. DIAN's risk engine suspected it belonged under HS 8471.50, automatic data-processing machines. The device did both things: it routed traffic between protocols, and it ran a general-purpose operating system on which users installed their own applications. Each day in port added storage and demurrage at about USD 180 per container. Finance had budgeted fourteen days of it.

The valuation audit was larger. DIAN argued that the transaction values declared on three 2025 imports were below comparable transactions and proposed a reconstructed value that added COP 612 million to the dutiable base. Retroactive duties and VAT came to about COP 178 million. The sanction, at 200% of the underpaid duty, came to about COP 356 million. The worst case was COP 534 million, roughly 19% of a year's profit. The law offered a reduction ladder on the sanction: 80% off if the reconstructed value was accepted within the inspection window, 50% off if accepted at the resolution stage, nothing on a full appeal. Whatever happened, the broker would have to file a corrected import declaration in Muisca, and the import VAT the company deducted had to reconcile in the May tax returns.

The export hold was the smallest in pesos and the loudest on the phone. A coffee shipment worth EUR 142,000 free on board (FOB) was stopped in Siglo XXI, DIAN's export system, because the certificate of origin carried a regional supplier code from the coffee growers' federation that did not match the exporter's tax registration. Uncorrected, the shipment would lose its European tariff preference, and about EUR 10,650 of additional duty would land in Hamburg, a sum the buyer could claim back from Andes Tech. The buyer had said it would cancel if the coffee was not moving by Saturday, May 2.

If Camacho did nothing, none of it waited. The inspection window would close with the reduction ladder unused, the full COP 534 million would stay open, the containers would sit in port on the demurrage clock, and the Hamburg order would lapse.

## What Camacho Controlled

At the first sitting she chose the lead fight: the valuation defence, where the money was largest; the classification dispute, where 14 earlier declarations rode on the answer; or the export unblock, the smallest file and the fastest. She also set the team's posture: defensive, paying and documenting to keep exposure small; balanced, defending on substance and conceding where the file was weak; or aggressive, appealing everything.

At the second sitting she chose the classification strategy. She could hold the line at HS 8517.62. She could reclassify to HS 8471.50 and re-paper the origin documents, which settled the product's status but told DIAN the earlier declarations had been wrong. Or she could request a binding advance ruling from DIAN and post a bond, which bought legal certainty at the cost of thirty days or more and of the preference on the next two shipments. The supporting move was one of three: re-papering the origin certificates for the affected products, a legal memo built on the general rules of interpretation and the chapter notes, or a letter from the broker.

At the third sitting she chose the valuation strategy: accept in the inspection window, accept at the resolution stage, or appeal and defend the declared transaction value under the World Trade Organization (WTO) valuation rules. She also chose the Muisca correction: full, with the VAT reconciled at once; partial, with the VAT reconciled the next quarter; or deferred while the team concentrated on the sanction.

At the fourth sitting she chose the export fix: a corrected certificate, fastest if the document could be amended; a supporting declaration from the federation tying the supplier code to the exporter's registration; or a new certificate issued from scratch. And she chose the change in broker governance: an annual audit of the broker's filings with a classification-review gate, a renegotiated service-level agreement with accuracy targets and shared-liability clauses, or a new broker.

Quintero would read four figures on the war-room board after every sitting. The cash buffer, which started at COP 2,860 million, the size of 2025 net income. The open sanction exposure. A compliance score from 0 to 100 that tracked the quality of the company's governance and filings, which stood at 50. And the days burned on the demurrage clock. A decision log beside it recorded each sitting's two choices and what they had done to cash, exposure and the compliance score. The board would judge the close on how much of the exposure was gone, where the compliance score ended and how much cash the whole episode had cost.

## Closing

At 9:40 the broker's account lead called to ask which file to start on. The inspector was due at the Buenaventura yard within the week, and the Hamburg buyer had written twice since breakfast. Camacho laid the three notices side by side on the war-room table and told him she would call back in an hour.
