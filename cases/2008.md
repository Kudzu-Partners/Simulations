# Meridian Executive Learning: Architecting the Halford Bid

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-2008 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2008 — *Custom Program Architect* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Tuesday, 3 February 2026, Nadia Farrell read Halford Industrial's request for proposal twice, and then read the diagnostic interview notes filed underneath it. The RFP asked for modules in finance, strategy and communication for 220 newly promoted managers. The interviews — the board sponsor, two line managers, and two of the promoted managers themselves — described a different company: people who were not making decisions across functional boundaries and were not holding their own teams to account. Nobody in the interview transcripts had asked for a finance module.

Farrell had three weeks to submit a proposal and ten weeks to begin delivering it. Three competitors were writing against the same brief. The budget was £480,000, fixed by Halford's board, and Ian Prothero, the group's chief operating officer and the bid's sponsor, had closed the kick-off call by saying that the number did not move and that what he wanted were "leaders who can run a P&L, not just attend a course."

## The Protagonist

Nadia Farrell, 44, was a programme director at Meridian Executive Learning, the custom-programmes unit of a London business school, where she had worked since 2016 after eight years designing leadership curricula inside a global engineering contractor. She had built her standing at Meridian on the quality of what she put in the room: bespoke cases, senior faculty, cohorts small enough that people spoke. Two of the programmes she was proudest of had scored highly with participants and had not been renewed. Neither client had ever asked her for evidence, and she had never volunteered any.

## The Decision

Farrell had to settle four things in three weeks, and they had to hold together as one proposal: which problem the programme was for, what shape it took for 220 people, how much of the fixed envelope went to proving it had worked, and what she would commit to in front of Prothero's board.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Executive Learning had been founded in 2004 and ran at arm's length from its parent school as a commercial unit. In 2025 it turned over £14.2 million across 38 client engagements at a 22% contribution margin, drawing on a contracted pool of 140 faculty and practitioners.

The unit was admired for the quality of its design. Its commercial record was less flattering. Only 41% of its clients could produce evidence that a Meridian programme had changed anything about how people behaved back at work, and renewals ran at 58%, against roughly 70% at the strongest custom providers. Meridian's own scorecard, opened on the morning the RFP arrived, put needs alignment — how closely a design tracked the client's diagnosed business problem rather than its requested topic list — at 50%. Projected behaviour change, the share of participants expected to be doing the target things differently on the job, stood at 30%. Client satisfaction, the measure of how the experience was rated in the room, stood at 60%. Nothing had yet been committed against the £480,000. The unit graded its own renewal risk on the account as High.

Halford Industrial was a £2.1 billion engineering group that had restructured and promoted 220 mid-level managers into general-management roles in a single wave. Its HR function had written the brief. Its board had written the cheque and the deadline.

## The Situation

The arithmetic was unforgiving in three directions at once (see **Exhibit 2**).

On money: £480,000 across 220 people came to £2,182 a head. A fully residential design ran at about £3,400 a head — £748,000 for the full cohort, or 141 of the 220 served and 79 left out. Self-paced digital with coaching ran at about £950 a head, £209,000 across the cohort; Meridian's own costing sheet described it as the cheapest and most scalable option and the weakest on behaviour change. Between them sat a blended architecture of cohort digital, in-person intensives and workplace application projects, which the costing sheet put at a floor of roughly £407,000 for the full 220 and described as balancing cost, reach and impact. Whichever route she took, design, delivery and measurement together had to land at or under £480,000.

On people: Meridian's three strongest general-management faculty could clear 18 teaching days between them inside the window. A premium small-cohort design for 220 managers needed 40 or more.

On evidence: the cheap way to evaluate a programme was to ask people at the end whether they had enjoyed it and to quiz them on what they had retained. Prothero had asked for the two questions beyond those — whether the managers behaved differently once they were back in their jobs, and whether the business numbers moved — and answering those meant manager-once-removed ratings, outcomes from the application projects and business KPIs read at 90 days. That build cost roughly £45,000 out of the same envelope. Every pound spent on it was a pound not spent on content, and content was what clients praised in the room.

Prothero had been explicit about the gate. If the first cohort showed no measurable behaviour shift at 90 days, the £480,000 would not be renewed, and an account worth about £1.4 million over three years would go to one of the three firms bidding against Meridian.

If Farrell simply built the programme Halford's HR function had asked for, the design would be well reviewed and the unit's numbers would not move: 41% of clients able to evidence behaviour change, 58% renewal, another engagement won on design and lost on proof (see **Exhibit 1**).

## What Farrell Controlled

Farrell controlled the architecture and nothing else. The budget, the headcount and the dates were Halford's.

She had to name the problem the programme was solving — the stated brief of finance, strategy and communication, a longer list of leadership topics, or the cross-functional decision-making and accountability gap the interviews described — and then write the target outcome the way Prothero's board would judge it, as understanding of frameworks, as a well-rated experience, or as an observable change in what managers did at work. She had to decide how much of the requested content the programme would fund and on what test, and how much diagnostic rigour to spend scoring brief against need before she drew a single module.

She had to pick the delivery format, reconcile the 18 available faculty days to whatever that format demanded — selling the premium design and finding the days later, replacing her strongest faculty with cheaper associates, or anchoring the top three on the in-person intensives and letting trained facilitators and digital carry the rest — and set what went to bespoke design and what went to delivery and logistics.

She had to decide whether the evidence plan Prothero wanted was funded in full, replaced by end-of-course happy sheets and a knowledge quiz, or cut outright to buy two more modules; whether workplace application was built in as required P&L projects with line-manager involvement and spaced practice, offered as an optional add-on, or left to the managers; which metrics the proposal would be judged on; and what share of the uncommitted budget went to measurement and application.

And she had to decide how to stand in front of the board: what to lead with, what outcome to commit to at the 90-day checkpoint, and what to say when Prothero asked what she had not funded and why.

## Closing

At 6:20 p.m. on 3 February, Farrell wrote two lines on the whiteboard in the design studio — *what they asked for* and *what the interviews said* — and drew a column beside each headed £480,000. Proposals were due on the 24th. Delivery started in ten weeks. Prothero's checkpoint was 90 days after that.
