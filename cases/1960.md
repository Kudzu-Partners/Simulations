# Instituto Cumbre de Alta Dirección: Ninety-Six Hours

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1960 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1960 — *Curriculum Architect* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Tuesday, April 14, 2026, Renata Ovalle laid two documents side by side in Monterrey. The first was a signed contract. Grupo Verazco, an industrial group with MXN 60 billion in annual revenue, had bought a custom executive program for 35 of its directors — *Leading Enterprise Transformation* — with the first cohort starting Monday, June 1. The second was her design team's module menu: sixteen candidate blocks, 140 contact hours. The contract released the directors for 96.

The quality and accreditation committee met on Friday, May 8. Whatever Ovalle put in front of it that morning — the modules, their order, the professors who would teach them, and the instruments measuring what the directors learned — was what Instituto Cumbre de Alta Dirección (ICAD) would deliver. She had seven weeks to the launch and three and a half to the sign-off.

## The Protagonist

Ovalle, 46, had been Academic Director for Custom Programs at ICAD since 2021, after six years running its open-enrollment portfolio. She had joined in 2015 from the corporate university of a steel group, where she built a leadership academy and watched it closed: a new chief financial officer asked what it produced and received an attendance report. Her unit's budget moved with custom-program margin and with the renewal of ICAD's three anchor accounts, Verazco among them.

## The Decision

Ovalle had to decide what the program was: which of the sixteen candidate blocks survived the cut to 96 hours, in what order they ran and how they sat across four months, who stood in front of the room on each of the twelve teaching days, and how each of the six contracted outcomes would be measured rather than merely covered. It had to be one document by May 8.

The outcomes had been written into the contract by Verazco's chief human resources officer, Sergio Madrigal: strategy execution, financial acumen, leading change, customer and market, digital, and personal leadership. Twenty per cent of ICAD's fee was tied to a post-program assessment showing measurable capability gain against them. Ileana Bracho, who chaired the committee, had refused two custom designs in eighteen months whose assessments did not evidence their stated outcomes.

## The Company and its Setting

ICAD, founded in 2004, was the executive-education arm of a private university in Monterrey. It billed roughly MXN 210 million a year across some 40 cohorts, taught by 28 core faculty and about 60 practitioner instructors (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two facts shaped Ovalle's room to move. The parent school held AACSB accreditation and was preparing for an EQUIS review; both required an auditable trail of stated outcomes, the assessments mapped against them, and evidence the loop had closed. She had learned that an outcome a program taught but never measured was, in an auditor's file, an absence. Second, the work was contested: a rival business school had been pitching Verazco for two years. The relationship was worth about MXN 14 million a year, and the contract carried a late-delivery penalty if June 1 moved.

## The Situation

The arithmetic was fixed at both ends. Verazco would release its directors for 12 days of 8 hours each — 96 contact hours — spread over four months. Forty-four hours had to come out of the 140-hour menu, and no module ran under a day.

Faculty were scarcer than hours. Nine of ICAD's 28 core professors were free between June and September, and the two with the strongest record in change leadership were committed to other cohorts throughout. The practitioner roster had capacity, and its teaching evaluations ranged from ICAD's best scores to its worst. Paulina Ríos, the faculty coordinator, argued that days a practitioner led were days the accreditor would not count; the practitioner liaison, that the directors had asked for people who had run a transformation.

The committee scored four readings on every custom design, and Ovalle's file already carried them (see **Exhibit 2**). Outcome coverage — the share of the six outcomes both taught and measured — stood at 17 out of 100: one qualified, the rest were at best taught. Faculty utilization, the sustainability of the load across the roster where 100 was healthy, stood at 50, as did projected participant satisfaction. Accreditation compliance, the state of the assurance file an auditor would open, stood at 20. Contact hours committed stood at zero of 96.

Nothing resolved itself by waiting. If the committee did not sign on May 8, the launch moved, the penalty clause opened, and the rival school's pitch stayed live with nothing from ICAD beside it. If it signed a design in which five outcomes were taught and none measured, the fifth of the fee riding on the post-program assessment had nothing behind it.

## What Ovalle Controlled

She controlled how the team framed the brief: as one joint problem in which hours, faculty, coverage and audit evidence all bound at once; as a content problem, of picking the best twelve modules; as a scheduling problem, of filling twelve days; or as a committee problem, of producing a signable design.

She controlled which outcome the team treated as its weakest: leading change, whose two strongest professors were committed elsewhere; financial acumen, which the 16-hour finance module already served; strategy execution, which several blocks touched; or none, each of the six having a candidate module against it. And she controlled what she told the board about the 44-hour gap — that whole modules had to come out, that every module could be shortened until the content fit, that Verazco should be asked for more days, or that the design should be built first and reconciled later.

She controlled the shape. The 96 hours had to be split across four blocks in any division totalling exactly 96: foundations (strategy execution and financial acumen), application (customer and market, and digital), an integration project worked in teams on Verazco's own transformation, and personal leadership (individual reflection and change work). She set the order — foundations, application, integration, personal leadership; the integration project and personal leadership first with the taught material after; whatever order the free professors' calendars produced; or the finance module first, which the directors had asked for by name. And she set the spacing: the twelve days across four months with directors back at work between blocks, compressed into three consecutive weeks, or wherever Verazco's calendar put them.

She controlled the staffing: the share of the twelve teaching days led by core faculty rather than practitioners, from none to all; and how those days were distributed — spread across the roster so no professor carried more than about three, concentrated on the two strongest professors available, or withheld from those two and given to newer members.

She controlled the measurement. The outcomes could be evidenced by a pre- and post-program diagnostic paired with an applied project on Verazco's transformation, graded outcome by outcome; by one capstone project graded as a whole; by attendance records and an end-of-program survey; or by the teaching itself, six outcomes covered thoroughly in class. She set how many of the six carried a dedicated graded instrument, from none to all.

And she controlled the defence: three minutes with Bracho, Madrigal in the room, and a choice of what to lead with — the design as one story, the coverage of all six outcomes, the client's experience and the names teaching it, or the completeness of the compliance file. Bracho would ask where leading change was assessed rather than taught, and Ovalle could answer with the graded change-project deliverable and the diagnostic behind it, with the three modules in which it appeared, with the survey question asking directors whether they felt readier to lead change, or with an undertaking to build the instrument after launch. If pushed to stake the contract on two of the four readings, she could stake coverage and accreditation, satisfaction and utilization, or refuse to trade any. And she had to decide how hard she would hold her design if the room preferred another.

## Closing

At 6:20 p.m. Ovalle printed the sixteen candidate modules onto one sheet and the six outcomes onto another, and set both beside the June-to-September faculty grid. The committee met on May 8; the first cohort arrived on June 1. She had 96 hours to spend, 140 hours of material, and nine professors.
