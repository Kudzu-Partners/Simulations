# Hibernia Climate Systems: Closing the Mandate Gap

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1939 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1939 — *Strategy in Action* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

The single page of numbers Niamh Cregan carried out of the Boyne Industrial Group strategy offsite on Monday, 14 September 2026, had not been open to negotiation. Boyne's new group chief executive had told every divisional managing director that "managing for the average" was over. For Hibernia Climate Systems, the building and energy systems division Cregan ran, the demand was arithmetic: lift return on invested capital from 11% to 16% within three years, and grow revenue at 8% a year against a trailing 2%. In the same session group cut Hibernia's three-year capital envelope 20%, to €48 million, and reweighted divisional bonuses toward growth.

Cregan had one planning cycle. Boyne's board reviewed divisional three-year plans at its capital-allocation meeting in December 2026, and the group's term for a plan the board did not find credible was "strategic review" — which at Boyne meant divestment candidate. Between the offsite and that meeting she had to settle a direction, decide how the division would be measured and paid, and commit the €48 million against €78 million of live investment cases.

## The Protagonist

Niamh Cregan, 49, had been Managing Director of Hibernia Climate Systems since 2022, having joined in 2011 to run its commercial function. Her standing inside Boyne rested on reliability: eleven consecutive quarters of cash delivered to plan. She had run Hibernia through a price war and a supply shock; she had not run it through a change of direction. Her bonus, and those of her three functional directors, had until the offsite moved on the divisional operating result and on delivery performance.

## The Decision

Cregan had to choose one direction for Hibernia, build the measurement and pay arrangements that would carry it into daily work, fund it inside the reduced envelope, and defend all three to the board as a single plan.

## The Company and its Setting

Hibernia Climate Systems designed, manufactured and installed commercial heating, ventilation and air-conditioning equipment, heat pumps and building-control systems. Founded in 1981 and acquired by Boyne in 2009, it ran plants at Cork and Galway, employed 1,850 people, and sold through contractors and facilities clients in Ireland, the United Kingdom and continental Europe. Revenue was €480 million; Boyne itself was a Dublin-listed diversified industrial group.

Hibernia was the slowest-growing business in the group and the most cash-generative, and had been managed for stability. Its planning process was an annual budget built from the prior year's actuals; its divisional scorecard carried a short list of financial measures unchanged in six years.

Revenue came from three streams. Equipment was the core: cash-generative, growing at low single digits. Installation carried high volume and thin margins and absorbed more working capital than the other two combined. Service and controls contracts carried the highest margins and the lowest capital intensity and renewed year after year; they were also the smallest stream, with no platform behind them.

Three functions would execute whatever Cregan decided, and each was paid on a different number. Declan Moore, commercial director, was bonused on volume. Aoife Brennan, finance and control director, on margin variance against budget. Tomás Kilbride, who ran both plants, was measured on utilisation.

## The Situation

On its current trajectory Hibernia would end the three-year period at about 2% growth and an 11% return — roughly €95 million of revenue and five return points short of the mandate (see **Exhibit 1**). Nothing in the September budget book closed that distance, and the budget book was the only plan the division had.

The capital position was tighter than the headline cut suggested. Three investment cases were written and costed: a heat-pump capacity expansion at €34 million, a services and recurring-revenue platform at €22 million, and a plant automation programme at €22 million (see **Exhibit 2**). Together they asked €78 million against the €48 million group had left her, and the group finance director had twice said the cut would not be reversed.

Boyne ran its divisions on a small number of financial measures reported quarterly. Cregan had seen divisional scorecards fail both ways: one sister division's too crowded for the centre to read, another's too bare to tell the board anything.

## What Cregan Controlled

Cregan worked the cycle in four sittings, each closing in a commitment she could not withdraw.

She began with the diagnosis. She had to name the lever that could carry the return from 11% to 16%: margin alone, by pricing the existing equipment book harder; capital turn alone, by sweating the assets already installed; both at once, shifting the revenue mix toward higher-margin work while releasing working capital; or neither, on the view that the gap closed by selling more units at today's margins. She had to name the stream with the most growth-and-return potential, and why the division grew at 2% — the planning habit of extrapolation; the maturity of the market; or the size of the capital budget. And she had to size the Year-3 revenue shortfall in millions of euro.

Second, she had to commit to one direction and name what the division would stop doing. She could grow the core equipment book by adding heat-pump capacity, cash-generative but the heaviest call on capital; shift the revenue mix toward recurring service and controls contracts, higher-margin but slower to build; move up-market into integrated low-carbon building systems, premium-priced but demanding brand and engineering depth; or fund a share of all three, flexible but thin across every case. She had to choose what to cut: exit the lowest-margin commodity installation contracts and release the working capital they held; trim overhead across the board and keep every line; or cut nothing and grow into the mandate. She also had to state, from 0 to 100, how firmly the division was committed to whichever direction she named.

Third came measurement and pay. She had to size the divisional scorecard: four to six measures covering return, growth and the direction's own drivers; twelve or more covering every function and perspective in the division; or two, return and cash, with no drivers behind them. She had to decide what became of the three bonus schemes: retire the volume, margin-variance and utilisation arrangements and pay all three functions on the value driver of the new direction; keep them and add a growth bonus on top; or leave pay exactly as it was. She had to pick the planning rhythm — the annual budget cycle the division had always run, or a rolling forecast with milestone reviews. And she had to decide what to ask of the group centre: a quarterly return and cash figure and nothing else, as now; a negotiated plan built on a few return and cash numbers plus a handful of milestones; or group inside the division, co-owning the operating detail.

Fourth, she allocated. She split the €48 million across the services platform, the heat-pump expansion and the automation programme in whatever pattern she chose. She had to say what was deferred or killed: name the case and the return points the deferral cost; defer without naming it; or fund the full €78 million on the assumption group would restore the cut. And she had to characterise what she took to the board — money that went where the strategy went, with the arithmetic walkable; money spread to keep flexibility across all three cases; or a plan that said one thing while the capital did another.

Four numbers followed her through the cycle. Return on invested capital — operating profit for every euro of capital tied up in the division — stood at 11.0% against a mandate of 16%. Revenue growth stood at 2.0% against a mandate of 8%. Capital committed, the running total against the €48 million ceiling, stood at zero. The fourth was a composite the group planning office kept, scored 0 to 100, of how far a division's direction, its measurement and pay arrangements and its capital pattern told the same story; Hibernia's stood at 20.

## Closing

On the Tuesday morning Cregan wrote three lines on her whiteboard: the direction, the scorecard, the money. Moore, Brennan and Kilbride were due at eleven, each carrying a bonus scheme that pointed somewhere different. The board met in December, and the page from the offsite was still on her desk.
