# Helio Verde: Eight Days to the Pre-Read

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1404 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1404 — *Helio Verde Cross-Border M&A — Cárnicos del Pacífico* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At 06:14 on Wednesday, May 6, 2026, Tomás Ulloa, chief of staff at Helio Verde, forwarded a message to Renata Coralles. It came from the chair, Manuel Beltrán-Rojas, and held three numbered questions. Why pay 12.0 times earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA) when Latin American meat processors traded at a median of 9.2 times? Would a filing in Washington make Helio Verde a US-regulated company? And could the group integrate a Mexican business while its own meat subsidiary, Helio Cárnicos, was still inside the cure period of its restructuring under Costaria's Ley Concursal?

Coralles, chief operating officer and lead on the acquisition of Cárnicos del Pacífico, a Mexican meat processor, had eight working days. At 14:00 on Friday, May 15, Beltrán-Rojas would sit down to a pre-read of the deal, two weeks before the formal board meeting. By then her team had to lock the architecture of the share purchase agreement (SPA), settle its approach to regulators in the United States and Europe, and be ready to answer his three questions.

## The Protagonist

Coralles was 52 and the only member of the cabinet who had negotiated a cross-border meat acquisition before. The chief executive had made her deal lead in March. Daniel Olmos-Sandoval, group general counsel and a former New York mergers lawyer, owned the contract and the filings. Esther Bracamonte-Núñez, a former audit partner, was interim head of finance; the chief financial officer's seat had been empty since February, and she would have to defend the price. Ulloa ran the deal calendar and, separately, the programme office for the Helio Cárnicos turnaround.

## The Decision

The team split the eight days into three blocks (see Exhibit 2): the spine of the contract, the approach to regulators, and the answers Beltrán-Rojas would hear. Each block would end with decisions recorded and dissent logged, not reopened.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde, S.A., was a listed Costarian conglomerate with about 13,800 employees. A founding-family vehicle held about 41% of its shares. Among its businesses were Helio Cárnicos and an agribusiness arm that sold about €340 million a year into the European Union. Beltrán-Rojas, 71, had chaired the board since 2008. He had distrusted cross-border meat deals since a Honduran bolt-on failed in 2014.

Cárnicos del Pacífico, S.A. de C.V., had been founded in Guadalajara in 1972 by Jorge Mendoza-Galindo, now 84. In 2025 it had revenue of US$680 million, 4,400 employees and six plants: five in Mexico and one in McAllen, Texas, approved by the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) for beef exports. EBITDA over the last twelve months was US$76.6 million. The family held about 78%; a family-office consortium that had invested in 2018 held 22% and wanted to sell. Jorge's son Carlos, the chief executive, wanted a 24-month transition role and then to leave. His sister Sofía, the chief financial officer, ran the seller's numbers with the family's adviser, Marchetti Rowe México.

Helio Cárnicos needed a Mexican base for North American beef exports by the fourth quarter. McAllen was the asset that mattered: on Coralles's estimate, a new USDA-approved plant would take 36 to 48 months and US$180 million to US$240 million, with single-digit odds of approval.

## The Situation

On April 24 the two sides had signed a letter of intent with sixty days of exclusivity. It valued the target at US$920 million of enterprise value: US$720 million in cash at closing and an earn-out of up to US$200 million tied to expanding McAllen over 24 months (see Exhibit 1). The minority consortium had signalled that a US strategic buyer would pay about 11.0 times and close in ninety days.

Sofía Mendoza-Galindo wanted a locked-box price fixed on the audited balance sheet of December 31, 2025, and no arbitration in New York over Mexican inventory. Bracamonte-Núñez preferred completion accounts, trued up after closing. Between the balance-sheet date and closing, about US$45 million to US$50 million of cash would build up on her estimate; who kept it depended on the mechanism. The founder's foundation had received US$2.4 million a year for fifteen years, not strictly an ordinary-course payment. Diligence had flagged two known issues: a log of USDA inspection variances at McAllen and environmental remediation at Querétaro.

McAllen meant a foreign buyer was taking control of a US business, which put the deal within reach of the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS). Olmos-Sandoval read food processing as outside the categories that made a filing mandatory; without a filing, the Committee could still open a review after closing. In Europe the target sold about €55 million a year, mostly in Spain and Germany. Whether the deal fell under the EU Merger Regulation, national regimes or neither depended on a 72-hour exercise allocating both parties' turnover by member state, not yet started. The long-stop date would fall fourteen months after signing.

Ulloa set up a tracker for the pre-read. A deal score out of 100 would average the quality of each block as it closed; it stood at zero. Beside it he kept the team's reading of the chair's confidence, at 50%, and of residual regulatory risk, also at 50% while no regulatory plan existed. After each block he would log its score, the chair-confidence reading and a one-line review note.

If the team did nothing, Beltrán-Rojas would walk into the pre-read with three questions and no answers. Exclusivity would lapse in late June with the 11.0-times bidder waiting, and Helio Cárnicos would enter the fourth quarter without its Mexican base.

## What Coralles Controlled

In the first block the team settled five terms, each with three drafts on the table (see Exhibit 2). The **purchase-price mechanism**: a locked box with a ticker and the foundation payment as permitted leakage, completion accounts, or a locked box without a ticker. The **protection for representations and warranties**: an insurance policy, a traditional escrow, or a hybrid. The **indemnification caps and survival**: tiered caps with special indemnities for the known issues, a single cap, or a seller-light package. The **material-adverse-change clause**, to be read under Delaware law, where only one buyer had ever been allowed to walk on such a clause, after earnings fell about 86%: a durational test, a broad clause with no number, or a narrow one set at that 86% mark. And the **termination fees**: reasonable best efforts with a cap on burdensome regulatory conditions and a reverse fee; a hell-or-high-water commitment, the posture of the Grauholm Brau–Keldermark Breweries combination; or pure best efforts. Bracamonte-Núñez had argued for completion accounts and Olmos-Sandoval for tighter exclusions in the clause. Coralles herself had been tempted by hell-or-high-water as the surest way to beat the rival bid.

In the second block, two decisions. The **approach to CFIUS**: a short voluntary declaration on the first day after signing, costed by counsel at US$250,000 to US$400,000; a full voluntary notice; or no filing. The **approach in Europe**: national filings in Spain and Germany, a simplified EU notification with the long form drafted in parallel, the long form from the outset, or no filing anywhere.

In the third block, three more. **How to defend 12.0 times**: on three legs, namely the McAllen option, a cost-of-capital advantage that Bracamonte-Núñez put at 60 basis points, and synergies of US$18 million a year by year three; on the McAllen option alone; or on synergies alone. **How to describe the reach of CFIUS**: as conditions on one plant, or as broader US regulatory exposure. And **how to run the integration** during the cure period: a sister subsidiary, standalone for 24 months under a dedicated Mexican lead; a merger into Helio Cárnicos; or one programme office under Ulloa for both the turnaround and the integration. The merger promised one management team, the shared office used the one person who knew both agendas, and the standalone route added a senior hire and a second reporting line.

## Closing

Coralles laid the chair's message beside the letter of intent. Eight working days, ten decisions, three questions. She opened the draft term sheet at its first line, the purchase price, and called in Olmos-Sandoval and Bracamonte-Núñez.
