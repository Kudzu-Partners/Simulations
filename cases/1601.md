# Brezalia Comunicaciones: Six Weeks in the Chair

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-1601 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1601 — *Chairman / Lead Director Playbook: Chairing, Not Running* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

At minute eighteen of a twenty-five-minute agenda item, in Brezalia Comunicaciones, S.A.'s ordinary March board session, the room turned to the chairman. On the table was a €420 million multi-year capital expenditure for the Portuguese fibre rollout, and the eleven-member board was split five to four in favour, with the chairman and one independent uncommitted. The chief executive had finished, and so had the audit chair; the meeting now expected the chairman to say whether he was putting the point to a vote.

Two further matters reached the chairman, Dr. Ignacio Berástegui-Cuevas, inside the same six weeks. Two weeks later the chief executive asked for a private meeting and told him she had been offered another company's chief executive seat; three weeks after that, a London fund published a fourteen-page open letter that named the chairman eleven times. In each room the same question arrived in different clothes: what, precisely, was the chairman's to settle, and what belonged to somebody else?

## The Protagonist

Berástegui-Cuevas, 65, was Brezalia's non-executive chairman, five years into a six-year mandate renewable once. He had been chief executive of a listed industrial group before taking the chair and still sat on two other European boards. He also chaired Brezalia's nominations committee. His reputation was that of a facilitator: in five years he had never taken an item to a block vote without exhausting deliberation first.

## The Decision

In each room Berástegui-Cuevas had to settle how the matter would be handled — the order of business, who spoke and when, whether a point went as drafted or reformulated, who else was told and how soon, where he stood back, and what was left in writing. None of the three substantive questions was his. The investment belonged to the board; the career decision to the chief executive; the answer to the fund to the board acting through its committees and its executives.

## The Company and its Setting

Brezalia Comunicaciones, S.A. was a converged telecommunications operator — mobile, fibre, business services and cybersecurity — Madrid-based and listed since 2004. It closed 2025 with €3,800 million of revenue, an EBITDA margin of 31.2%, net debt at 2.4 times EBITDA and about 14,000 employees in Spain, Portugal and minority-held Andean operators. Market capitalisation at the end of Q1 2026 was roughly €5,100 million, with a 58% free float (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two reference shareholders held the rest: an Iberian insurer with 14%, represented by Mauricio Pelayo-Solís, and a Gulf sovereign fund with 9%, represented by Aisha Bouchama. The eleven seats were eight independents, those two proprietary directors, and the chief executive, Francesca Romaní-López, 51, seven years in post; total shareholder return had run at 11.4% a year since her appointment against a sector average of 6.8%. Andrés Mompó-Hernández was lead independent director, and Eulalia Fontán-Carbonell, a former audit partner, chaired audit.

## The Situation

The capex had been presented at February's capital markets day. In favour were the chief executive, the two proprietary directors, the technology and innovation chair Ng-Cabrera and Mompó; critical were Fontán-Carbonell, the remuneration chair Iglesias-Bardají and two further independents, who argued that funding it required a bond at 215 basis points over mid-swap, 45 above the 2024 issue, with interest cover falling from 5.8 times to 4.1 times. Fontán-Carbonell had just put on the record that the briefing pack went out 48 hours before the session, not the 72 the board's rules required. Pelayo-Solís wanted a longer payback horizon; Iglesias-Bardají had suggested splitting the authorisation in two.

The second matter came without a meeting. Romaní-López said a United States peer had approached her unsolicited, that she would take up the post in 90 days and resign from Brezalia "when the chairman considers appropriate, but before month-end". She asked what he would do in her position. Neither the board, the nominations committee nor the regulator knew; the lead independent director had not been briefed; the secretary held no record. The duty to open an insider list attached once information became price-sensitive, and there was a respectable argument that it already had.

The third arrived on a Monday morning (see **Exhibit 2**). Brackenfold Capital Partners, signing through its partner Henry Whitfield, declared a 4.9% economic position in shares and synthetic instruments, below the notification threshold, and demanded board renewal with two Brackenfold nominees, an €800 million accelerated buyback and the sale of the Andean stakes. The stock opened 5.8% higher and two columnists called for an extraordinary general meeting. Within sixty minutes a Tidewell News reporter wanted a statement from the chairman, Whitfield asked for a bilateral in London inside 72 hours, the chief executive asked for a green light to respond herself, and the two proprietary directors requested a separate meeting before the next board.

Left alone, each room had a default. The March point would be put as drafted and the five-four split would land in the minutes. The succession would stay a conversation between two people, with no committee, no record and no insider list. The letter would be answered by whoever reached a microphone first.

## What the Chairman Controlled

Brezalia's annual board evaluation assessed the chair on process, not outcome. It counted *intrusions*, occasions on which he settled what was not his to settle; *omissions*, occasions on which he left unsettled what was; and *sole-channel events*, material information he alone held. It carried two indices, each starting the year at 100 points: board cohesion, whether proprietary and independent directors stayed equally informed and equally heard, and procedure, compliance with the board's rules and the regulator's. It also recorded how each matter was framed and sequenced, where he stood back, and what was left in writing.

Fourteen calls fell to him. Four in March: the 48-hour pack — note it informally, record it and continue by consent, or suspend and reconvene at 72 hours; his own voice in the debate — argue the case for fibre beside the chief executive, order the speakers and put the open questions, or stay silent; minute eighteen — put the point as drafted, reformulate it as two tranches (about €220 million now, the rest in September against cost-of-debt metrics), or defer it; the minutes — dissent in the dissenters' own words and at their own length, a smoothed unified conclusion, or a short entry for the secretary.

Five in the private office: her question — his own view on whether to accept, a refusal on the substance with the matter sent to the nominations committee under the lead independent director, or no discussion until she had decided; the committee he chaired — stand aside for the succession, remain but avoid leading it, or carry on as normal; the lead independent director — brief him that day and fix a calendar for the full board inside 72 hours, wait three weeks for the ordinary board, or proceed alone meanwhile; the insider list — open it now through the secretary and general counsel, wait for her firm decision, or leave it while the information was tentative; custody of what he knew — hold it alone, share it that day with the lead independent director and the secretary, or share it that night with all eleven directors.

Five on the Monday: Tidewell News — a personal quote on the record, a referral to investor relations and the chief executive, or a joint statement; Whitfield — meet him alone in London, decline, or send the lead independent director and the chief executive with the corporate broker; the extraordinary board — in 24 hours, in 48 to 72 hours on a short agenda of noting the letter, appointing a committee and retaining advisers, in seven days, or at the ordinary meeting three weeks out; the proprietary directors' request — accept it with no equivalent for the independents, decline bilaterals and offer one joint pre-board session, or accept and replicate it with the independents; the response itself — an ad hoc committee with a written mandate and advisers, the chairman day by day, or the chief executive and her team.

## Closing

The secretary's pen had stopped moving. Fontán-Carbonell's objection was on the record, the five-four split was on the table, and the agenda clock said minute eighteen of twenty-five. Behind that room were two more. In each, the first words out of the chairman's mouth were his alone to choose.
