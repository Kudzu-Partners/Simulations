# Banque Méridienne Crédit: The Corrected Book

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1799 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1799 — *Banking for Tomorrow* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 14 September 2026, Camille Duthoit read the European Central Bank's letter twice before she forwarded it to anyone else. A supervisory desk review of Banque Méridienne Crédit's climate-and-environmental risk disclosures had found them materially inconsistent with the composition of the bank's own loan book: 31% of the loans booked in the first half of the year under a "sustainable" label did not meet the bank's own taxonomy criteria. BMC had 45 days to re-screen EUR 2.1 billion of recent originations and report a corrected portfolio sustainability score.

Duthoit chaired the Credit Committee that had approved those loans. The numbers were not close to the bank's own public commitments: a weighted ESG score of 58 against a Charter target of 70, and green or transition lending running at 24% of new origination against a 40% commitment due by 2027. Two borrowers the committee had classified as transition financing the year before — a logistics operator and a textiles group, EUR 145 million of exposure between them — were now missing payments, their combined probability of default risen from 1.2% to 3.8%. If the ECB concluded that the bank's governance of ESG-related risk was weak, it could add 0.4 percentage points to BMC's required capital, tying up EUR 95 million the bank could otherwise lend. The committee had to repair the score, defend the bank's profitability, and show the mandate was real — without simply refusing to lend.

## The Protagonist

Camille Duthoit, 51, had been Head of Credit at Banque Méridienne Crédit since 2019 and chaired its weekly Credit Committee. She had spent eight years before that running the bank's SME lending desk, after joining BMC in 2004. She had signed off, with the rest of the committee, on the loans the ECB now questioned, and on the sustainability Charter that made the 40% and 70 targets binding rather than aspirational.

## The Decision

Duthoit had to decide, in stages, how the committee would respond: how to re-establish the true state of the loan book and where the committee's attention went first; what to do with that quarter's live loan applications; how to explain the corrected book to the regulator, the bank's own chief financial officer, and the NGO partner to the Charter, and what to do about the two borrowers already in arrears; and how to change the way the bank made these decisions going forward, so the same failure could not recur. Each choice would move the bank's sustainability score, its lending mix, its return and its capital position together.

## The Company and its Setting

Banque Méridienne Crédit was a commercial bank headquartered in Lyon, with EUR 38 billion in total assets and a corporate and SME loan book of EUR 14.2 billion, serving businesses from family-owned manufacturers to newer cleantech firms across the region. The bank's supervisory board had committed that by 2027, at least 40% of new corporate lending would qualify as green or transition financing under its own taxonomy, and the loan book's weighted ESG score would reach 70 out of 100. Relationship Managers were paid on loan volume, as they always had been. The Credit Committee met weekly to approve, decline or price every material application, and until the ECB's letter arrived, its habit had been to apply the sustainability label generously and worry about volume and margin first.

## The Situation

The ESG score stood at 58 against the 70 target, a gap of twelve points with the Charter's 2027 deadline approaching. Green and transition origination stood at 24% against the 40% commitment. Return on risk-weighted assets, the metric the CFO tracked most closely, sat at 0.9% against a 1.1% hurdle. The bank's capital buffer against the ECB's threatened add-on stood at zero, with EUR 95 million of lending capacity at stake if the regulator's view of the bank's governance did not improve (Exhibit 1). If nothing changed — if the committee kept booking loans on commercial momentum and let the mislabelling stand — the ECB's own letter already showed where that led: a score moving further from 70, a Charter the bank could not honestly claim to be meeting, and a capital penalty landing on a book that still needed EUR 100 million of new lending that quarter just to keep operating at its normal pace.

## What Duthoit Controlled

In the first meeting, the committee had to choose how to re-screen the EUR 2.1 billion in question: apply the bank's own taxonomy rule by rule and strip out every loan that failed verification, defend the existing labels to the regulator as broadly consistent with their intent, or reclassify the failing loans as transition financing so the reported green share held. It also had to decide where its first attention went that week — to the EUR 145 million in arrears, to the ESG score the regulator had just challenged, or to the current quarter's profit — and how rigorously to document the evidence behind every retained label, on a scale running from minimal to fully substantiated.

That same week, four loan applications were waiting for a decision (Exhibit 2): EUR 60 million for a cement producer with high current emissions and a stated plan to cut them; EUR 12 million for a highly profitable single-use-plastics packager that failed the bank's ESG screen outright; EUR 25 million for a community solar cooperative with a strong sustainability profile but thin margins and modest collateral; and EUR 40 million for an established, profitable agri-processor with weak governance disclosure. For each, the committee could approve it outright, approve it with a condition addressing that borrower's specific weakness — emissions for the cement producer, the failed ESG screen for the plastics packager, thin collateral for the solar cooperative, weak disclosure for the agri-processor — or decline it. On whichever loans were approved, it also had to set a single pricing adjustment of up to 60 basis points, positive or negative, widening or narrowing the spread according to each borrower's sustainability profile, while keeping total new lending at or above the EUR 100 million floor the bank needed to stay open for business.

Next, Duthoit had to represent the committee's decisions to three audiences who would not accept the same argument. To the ECB, she could present the corrected score with full documentation, ask for more time, or contest the finding directly. To the CFO, she could walk through each loan's economics individually, lead with the sustainability narrative, or promise higher volume to lift the average return. To the NGO partner, she could point to conditions like the cement loan's as how the bank intended to keep lending to high emitters, concede the point and pledge to exit all high emitters, or decline to discuss lending decisions with an outside party. Separately, she had to choose a path for the EUR 145 million already in arrears: restructure both exposures with tighter covenants and provision for the higher default risk, leave them on their existing covenants and pricing without additional provisioning, or sell both positions immediately at a steep discount.

Finally, Duthoit had to set the committee's path to 2027. She could redesign the Relationship Manager bonus to reward loan volume, portfolio sustainability quality and risk-adjusted return together, leave it tied to volume, or shift it to reward the ESG score exclusively. She could make screening mandatory with an independent sign-off before any loan closed, issue new guidance for Relationship Managers to apply, or add no new control. She had to commit a green and transition origination-mix target, somewhere between 20% and 60%, to the supervisory board, and state — on a scale of conviction from zero to a hundred — how firmly she would argue that sustainability belonged inside the credit decision itself, not attached to it afterward as a label.

## Closing

Duthoit closed the ECB's letter, opened the folder holding the week's four loan applications, and walked into the conference room where the rest of the Credit Committee was waiting. Forty-five days remained on the clock, and the meeting had not yet started.
