## Abstract

Empresa Catalina, a Barcelona-headquartered mid-cap consumer brand with EUR 92M in 2025 revenue across Iberia and selected European Union markets, approved a USD 14M, four-year LATAM expansion budget in February 2026. Inés Castellví, Director of International Expansion since 2023, had to commit by April 30 to exactly three of six candidate markets — Mexico, Brazil, Colombia, Chile, Peru, Argentina — and to one entry mode per chosen market, drawn from joint venture, distributor partnership, or greenfield own-operation. The CAGE distance scores from Spain ranged from 28 (Mexico, lowest) to 62 (Brazil, highest); the currency volatility ranged from low (Chile) to extreme (Argentina); the retail-channel structure varied from highly consolidated (Chile) to highly fragmented (Peru). The case opens at 7:14 a.m. on Tuesday, April 14, 2026, as Castellví prepares the package for the April 30 Executive Committee. Year 1 would commit the first 75% of the budget; Year 2 would face the first currency event and the first distributor underperformance; Years 3–4 would optimize the portfolio with switch-mode and follow-on decisions. The brief was simple: pick three, do not lose money in any of them, exit Year 4 with at least one self-sustaining operation.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Apply the CAGE framework (Cultural, Administrative, Geographic, Economic distance) to a six-market candidate set and rank entry attractiveness.
2. Match entry mode (joint venture, distributor, greenfield) to country characteristics and to firm-level capability constraints.
3. Evaluate a USD 14M budget allocation across three markets under uncertainty in currency, partner performance, and retail channel structure.
4. Diagnose the Year-2 events — currency devaluation and distributor underperformance — and recommend re-tuning interventions.
5. Recommend a Year-1 portfolio (three markets, three entry modes, allocation percentages) and articulate the Year-3 portfolio review trigger that would force a mode switch.

On the morning of **Tuesday, April 14, 2026**, **Inés Castellví** sat in the international expansion office at Empresa Catalina's Barcelona headquarters and read, for the seventh time, the one-page CAGE-distance summary her team had prepared on the six candidate markets. Sixteen days remained until the April 30 Executive Committee. The Chief Executive had asked, in their Friday call, for a two-page recommendation. The CFO had asked for a sensitivity table. The Chair of the family-controlled board had asked, in a single sentence at the March meeting, for the answer to "the question your father did not solve."

## The Protagonist

Inés Castellví, 44, had been Director of International Expansion at Empresa Catalina since June 2023. A graduate of ESADE in Barcelona and the holder of an MSc in International Business from the Stockholm School of Economics, she had spent fourteen years at a Spanish multinational consumer-electronics company, finishing as Country Manager for Brazil from 2018 to 2022. The Brazil tenure was the part of her résumé the Empresa Catalina board had hired for: she had built a EUR 70M business in Brazil from a EUR 8M starting point in three years, through a joint venture that she had — privately — described as the hardest professional experience of her career. Her father, Joan Castellví, had been Empresa Catalina's first international expansion director in 1998 and had attempted Mexico and Argentina entries in 2001–2003; both had been wound down by 2006. The Chair's sentence in March had referred to that history.

---

## The Decision

Castellví had to commit, by 9:00 a.m. on April 30, to four sub-decisions:

1. **Three markets** from the six candidates — Mexico, Brazil, Colombia, Chile, Peru, Argentina.
2. **Entry mode per market** — Joint Venture (JV), Distributor, or Greenfield.
3. **Budget allocation** — percentage of the USD 14M to each market in Year 1.
4. **Brand-architecture posture** — Spanish parent dominant, local endorser, or pure local brand under a multi-brand portfolio.

The four sub-decisions would compound across four years. Year-2 events — a currency devaluation in one market, a distributor underperformance in another — would test the entry-mode choice. Year-3 portfolio optimization would offer a mode-switch decision (e.g., distributor to JV) and a follow-on capital decision. Year-4 expansion or consolidation would close the four-year window.

## Company Background

Empresa Catalina had been founded in 1973 in Barcelona by Joan Castellví's father as a regional household-and-personal-care manufacturer; it had expanded into the rest of Spain during the 1980s, into Portugal and France in the 1990s, and into Italy and Benelux in the 2000s. By 2025, the company had EUR 92M in revenue (approximately USD 99M at the year-end spot rate), operating margin of 11.8%, and a portfolio of seven brands across three categories: home cleaning (54% of revenue), personal care (32%), and beverages (14%). The largest brand, *Catalina Home*, contributed 38% of revenue. Distribution was through three large European modern-trade chains (60% of revenue), pharmacy and drugstore channels (22%), and direct-to-consumer e-commerce (18%).

The company was 64%-owned by the founding family; the Chair was the founder's daughter and Joan Castellví's sister. The remaining 36% was held by two Spanish institutional investors and a small float on the Madrid junior market. The 2025 board had approved the LATAM expansion as the first non-European entry in the company's history and as the test case for a planned 2030 Asian expansion.

## Industry and Market Context

The Latin American consumer-goods market in 2026 was characterized by six structural features the entry decision turned on. First, retail-channel structure varied widely: Chile was the most consolidated (top three chains controlled 78% of modern-trade volume); Peru the most fragmented (top three at 41%); Mexico in between (top three at 62%). Second, currency volatility ranged from low (Chilean peso, 5% annualized) to extreme (Argentine peso, 38% annualized over the prior 24 months). Third, regulatory complexity for foreign consumer brands was lowest in Chile and Mexico; highest in Brazil (Anvisa for personal care, distinct labeling for the Portuguese language) and Argentina (FX controls and import restrictions). Fourth, distributor reliability — measured by the proportion of category-leader brands that had switched distributors at least once in the prior five years — was highest in Colombia (61% of leaders had switched) and lowest in Chile (12%). Fifth, joint-venture availability — defined as the count of credible local consumer-goods partners with annual revenue over USD 100M open to a JV — was highest in Mexico (eleven candidates) and lowest in Peru (three). Sixth, greenfield capital intensity ranged from USD 1.8M at minimum viable scale (Chile, single-warehouse model) to USD 6M (Brazil, multi-warehouse multi-state model).

Empirically, the post-2015 LATAM-entry literature converged on three findings. Distributor entry into a market with high distributor-switching rates produced a 38% probability of underperformance in Year 1; the same entry into Chile produced a 14% probability. JV entry succeeded in 62% of cases when the entrant brought a complementary capability (logistics, brand strength, channel access) to the partner; in 28% of cases when it did not. Greenfield entry succeeded in 48% of cases at MVP scale, 71% at full scale; the difference came from the willingness to fund through Year 3 against early losses.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Empresa Catalina's CEO, Pere Ribalta, had commissioned the LATAM market study in October 2025 and had pre-screened the six candidate markets with the help of an external advisory firm. The study had ranked Mexico, Chile, Colombia, and Peru as low-CAGE entries; Brazil and Argentina as high-CAGE. The board had approved the USD 14M budget on the basis of a "three-market base case." The CEO had also signalled, privately, that he would want the operating result of the LATAM portfolio to be break-even or better by end of Year 3.

### What Castellví knew

The internal expansion team had modeled three Year-1 portfolios. A Conservative Portfolio (Mexico JV 50%, Colombia Distributor 25%, Chile Greenfield 25%) projected Year-1 revenue of USD 1.4M, Year-4 revenue of USD 12.4M, cumulative profit of USD 2.1M, and one self-sustaining operation by Year 4. An Aggressive Portfolio (Mexico JV 40%, Brazil JV 35%, Chile Greenfield 25%) projected Year-1 revenue of USD 1.8M, Year-4 revenue of USD 16.2M, cumulative profit of USD 1.4M (lower because of Brazil regulatory drag), and two self-sustaining operations by Year 4. A Defensive Portfolio (Chile Greenfield 50%, Mexico JV 30%, Peru Distributor 20%) projected Year-1 revenue of USD 0.9M, Year-4 revenue of USD 8.6M, cumulative profit of USD 1.9M, and two self-sustaining operations by Year 4 at smaller scale.

### What she did not know

Whether the Mexican JV partner her team had pre-qualified would accept a 50/50 ownership structure or push for 60/40 in their favor; whether the Colombian distributor's offered exclusivity terms would survive a Year-2 underperformance trigger; whether the Chilean greenfield operation, planned for Santiago, would secure the lease on the Providencia warehouse the team had identified; and whether the Argentine FX situation in Years 2–4 would invalidate any analysis of an Argentina-included portfolio.

### What others were saying

**Pere Ribalta**, CEO: *"Three markets, two of which are net positive by Year 4. I do not want to defend Brazil to my Chair. I do not want to lose Mexico to a JV partner who eats us. Find me the portfolio that survives Year 2."*

**Albert Vives**, CFO: *"Argentina is uninvestable until the FX regime changes. Brazil is a six-million-dollar Greenfield or a thirty-five-percent JV — neither fits the budget without crowding out Mexico. Pick three from the four low-CAGE markets."*

**Marta Fontanella**, Director of Brand: *"Local endorser model in all three. The Spanish parent is visible — that is part of the proposition. The local face is mandatory in Mexico, useful in Colombia, optional in Chile."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Castellví had narrowed the space to three portfolios.

- **Option A — Conservative Three (Mexico JV / Colombia Distributor / Chile Greenfield):** Allocation 50/25/25. Year-4 revenue projection USD 12.4M, cumulative profit USD 2.1M, one self-sustaining operation. Lowest portfolio risk; highest single-market concentration in Mexico.

- **Option B — Aggressive Three (Mexico JV / Brazil JV / Chile Greenfield):** Allocation 40/35/25. Year-4 revenue projection USD 16.2M, cumulative profit USD 1.4M, two self-sustaining operations. Highest revenue ambition; Brazil regulatory and language drag through Years 1–2.

- **Option C — Defensive Three (Chile Greenfield / Mexico JV / Peru Distributor):** Allocation 50/30/20. Year-4 revenue projection USD 8.6M, cumulative profit USD 1.9M, two self-sustaining operations. Lowest single-market exposure; lowest revenue ceiling.

Castellví's private view, not yet shared with the CEO, was that Option A was the right Year-1 commitment and that the Year-3 mode switch — Colombia from Distributor to JV — was where the real portfolio shift would happen. She had not yet decided which sentence she would use at the April 30 Executive Committee to anchor that view without prejudging the Year-3 decision.
