# Cultivating Communities — Making Atelier Numérique Learn Faster

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1846 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1846 — *Cultivating Communities* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 8 June 2026, Claire Vasseur read the post-mortem on a rail-signalling project that had just closed four months late and €4.2 million over budget, and found the detail at the center of it: the team had spent six weeks solving a real-time-determinism problem that engineers in Toulouse's aerospace group had already solved and written up eighteen months earlier. The knowledge had existed inside Atelier Numérique the entire time. It had simply never moved.

By the end of that week the CEO had created a new role — Head of Innovation & Knowledge — put Vasseur in it, and given her €1.2 million a year, a cap of 5% of engineers' time, and twelve months to prove the firm could learn faster without missing a single billable deadline along the way.

## The Protagonist

Vasseur, 44, had spent eight years running systems engineering for Atelier Numérique's Toulouse site before the CEO pulled her out of delivery entirely. She knew the aerospace archive that held the buried solution because she had approved the write-up herself, years earlier, and had watched it disappear into a project folder nobody outside that team would ever open. She had no staff yet, no track record in the role, and a CEO who had just committed a large budget on the strength of a short conversation.

## The Decision

Vasseur's mandate was to make expertise move sideways across the firm — out of the unit that generated it and into the hands of whichever team needed it next — in a way that survived contact with a billable-hours business. She had a year to design that architecture, fund it, get engineers to actually use it, and give the CEO a credible reason to keep funding it afterward.

## The Company and its Setting

Atelier Numérique S.A., headquartered in Toulouse, designed embedded software and industrial control systems for aerospace, energy, and mobility clients across Europe. Founded in 2003, it had grown by acquiring smaller engineering shops and now employed 1,600 engineers across four sites — Toulouse, Lyon, Nantes, and a smaller Berlin office — generating €210 million in revenue at an 11% operating margin. Its product was applied expertise, and each acquisition had left behind its own project-siloed business unit, so an engineer who solved a hard problem in one unit had no way to tell anyone in another unit it was already solved.

The firm had tried once before. An earlier initiative had built a mandatory documentation portal and a lessons-learned template every project was supposed to fill in; compliance had settled at 9%. Engineers were billable, and a template nobody read cost an hour they could otherwise charge to a client.

## The Situation

An internal sample showed that roughly 30% of engineering hours on new projects went to re-solving problems the firm had already solved elsewhere — a recurring cost finance put at about €11 million a year, close to 5% of revenue. Cross-sector proposals, which combined expertise from two or more domains and carried the firm's best margins, had fallen to two in the past year, down from seven three years earlier. And three of the firm's most senior architects, whose judgment nobody had ever managed to write down, were due to retire within eighteen months (see **Exhibit 1**).

Vasseur's dashboard showed the same numbers the CEO would track alongside her: a knowledge-sharing and reuse rate at 9% — exactly where the failed portal had left it — against a twelve-month target of 55%; a community-engagement score of 8 out of 100, measuring genuine voluntary participation; an innovation-output score of 18 out of 100, tracking the cross-sector idea flow that had dried up; and value recovered against the €11 million waste, standing at zero. If nothing changed, the firm would keep losing €11 million a year, new hires would keep ramping slowly, cross-sector proposals would keep falling, and the retiring architects would leave with nothing behind them. At the twelve-month review, a CEO who saw no measurable acceleration would conclude that knowledge management simply didn't work here, and shut the function down.

## What Vasseur Controlled

Vasseur first had to decide where expertise needed to move sideways. She could focus on knowledge that was tacit, evolving, and crossed unit boundaries — embedded-security judgment, debugging heuristics, client-specific know-how living in people's heads. She could try to cover every domain at once, including the safety-critical, tightly regulated standards aerospace and energy clients audited. She could restrict herself to material already stable enough to write down, like coding standards and compliance checklists. Or she could decide the firm wasn't ready to classify anything yet, and rebuild the documentation system first so more of what people knew got captured at all. A second, more abstract choice sat alongside it: whether to match each kind of knowledge to the structure that suited it — voluntary, self-organizing groups for what was tacit and still changing, formal procedure for what was stable and compliance-bound — or bet that one approach would work everywhere. She also had to weight, on a scale from zero to a hundred, how hard to prioritize the retiring architects' knowledge in that first map, with their departure clock already running.

Once the map existed, she had to fund it. The full €1.2 million had to be split across six destinations, and nothing stopped her from putting the entire budget behind one: cross-site voluntary groups built around a shared domain; structured rotations and shadowing meant to move tacit judgment through direct experience; a lightweight internal directory of who actually knew what; collaboration tools built into engineers' daily work; a documentation and standards portal enforced through the existing hierarchy; and a once-a-year, cross-site conference (see **Exhibit 2**).

Money alone would not make anyone participate. She had to decide how group leaders would be chosen — recognized and given real protected hours, asked to lead voluntarily on top of an already full workload, or formally appointed by management with attendance reported up the chain. She had to decide how much billable time to ring-fence for participation, anywhere from none up to 8%, and defend that number when a delivery director later asked her to cut the planned 5% down to 2% to save a slipping client deadline. She had to decide how to give the groups legitimacy — visible backing from senior leadership with no formal rules attached, mandatory membership with fixed attendance and monthly reporting, or no sponsorship at all. And she had to decide how to pull the retiring architects' judgment into circulation before they left: mentoring and shadowing inside whatever group she built, having them fill in the same lessons-learned templates, or leaving it to the new structures to pick it up naturally over time.

At year's end she had to commit to a way of proving it worked: quarterly checkpoints against all four measures, with an explicit account of how structure led to participation, participation to reuse, and reuse to euros recovered; a single reuse number at the twelve-month review; or a general promise that learning would accelerate, tracked informally. She had to decide how her structures would survive once the launch energy faded — a second-year plan with rotating leadership and career-linked recognition, a fresh relaunch campaign every year, or trusting the initial momentum to carry them. And she had to frame the pitch itself to a CEO who had funded this after a €4.2 million loss: a chain from structure to reuse to euros recovered, sized against a real share of the €11 million waste; a promise of compliance numbers achieved by mandating participation until reuse hit target; or a general promise of faster learning and cultural change. One more figure sat alongside it: a conviction score from zero to a hundred, measuring how firmly she would defend that time budget under renewed pressure.

## Closing

Vasseur closed the post-mortem, opened a blank organization chart of the four sites, and started marking which knowledge lived in whose head. The rail-signalling loss was six weeks old. The three architects' retirement clocks were already running. She had €1.2 million, a 5% time cap, and twelve months before the CEO would decide whether it had worked.
