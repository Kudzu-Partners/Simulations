# Lumen & Co.: Shipping the Aurore Relaunch

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1805 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1805 — *Human + AI Creative Studio* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 4 May 2026, Camille Aubert, creative director of Lumen & Co., carried a brief across the studio floor instead of emailing it. Florette Beauté's flagship skincare line, Aurore, worth €88 million a year in revenue, was losing ground to a direct-to-consumer challenger that had taken six share points in eighteen months, and the chief marketing officer wanted a relaunch shipped across six channels in five weeks — forty percent less time than the studio normally took for a campaign this size — on a fixed production budget of €1.8 million.

Aubert's task was to decide, creative task by creative task, how much of that work her studio's people would do by hand and how much its artificial-intelligence toolchain would do instead, and to keep making that call as the five-week clock ran down. The toolchain split into two families: generative tools that produced new copy, images, and variants outright, and non-generative tools that analysed audiences, predicted performance, and optimised delivery without creating anything a customer would see. Which family belonged on which task, and how much of each task stayed with a person, was the decision the CMO was waiting on.

## The Protagonist

Camille Aubert, 41, had led Lumen & Co.'s creative department since Florette Beauté spun the studio out of its marketing division in 2022, after seven years as an associate creative director at a Paris advertising agency. She had personally championed the generative-AI toolchain the studio adopted the year before, made the budget case for the three-person data-and-AI cell that came with it, and stood in the room when the board reviewed the launch that had leaned on it hardest — a campaign that tested eighteen percent below the brand's own distinctiveness baseline, technically polished but, the board said, forgettable. She had not been taken off AI decisions after that result. She had been handed the next one.

## The Decision

For the Aurore relaunch, Aubert had to assign each of four pieces of creative work — the core concept and brand voice, the resizing of that concept into hundreds of channel-specific variants, the adaptation of copy and assets market by market, and the modelling of which audiences and messages were likely to perform — to one of four ways of working: fully human, human and machine working the same material together, generative AI carrying the task on its own, or a non-generative AI tool assisting without creating anything itself. Every assignment had to be paid for out of the same €1.8 million, alongside a separate figure for how many of those euros went toward scaling generative output on the production side rather than the concept side (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Lumen & Co. was the in-house creative studio of Florette Beauté, a French cosmetics group headquartered in Paris's ninth arrondissement with €620 million in annual revenue and a 13% operating margin. The studio employed 62 people, billed Florette €14 million a year internally against an €11 million operating budget, and produced roughly 140 campaigns a year across paid social, retail, CRM, and out-of-home advertising. Its three-person data-and-AI cell, the newest team on the floor, had been stood up the previous year specifically to run the generative and non-generative tools the studio was now deciding how to deploy on Aurore.

## The Situation

Four numbers sat on Aubert's dashboard the morning the brief arrived. Distinctiveness — a score of how memorably Aurore stood out against a set baseline of 100 — read 70. Time to deliver, tracking the campaign's projected ship date against the fixed five-week retail calendar, stood at 8.0 weeks. Engagement, a 0-to-100 measure of the audience response expected across all six channels, stood at 45. And the studio had committed none of its €1.8 million production budget yet. A fifth indicator, tracking whether a generated asset had raised a rights, likeness, or disclosure problem serious enough for the board's brand-safety review, showed no flag — but it was not a settled question: a generated visual from the previous quarter had come close enough to a rival's hero image that it had triggered a legal review before it reached print (see Exhibit 1).

Last spring, the studio's most AI-heavy launch to date had tested eighteen percent below that same baseline — the result the board had not forgotten. If none of the four numbers on Aubert's dashboard moved from where they stood that morning, Aurore would reach the shelf three weeks after the retail calendar closed and thirty points below the distinctiveness baseline.

## What Aubert Controlled

Before she could allocate a single task, Aubert had to settle what the campaign was actually trying to win. Four candidates were on the table inside the studio: distinctiveness — being noticed and remembered as Aurore; polish — flawless execution at high volume across every channel; speed — reaching the shelf as fast as the five-week calendar allowed; or price messaging — competing with the insurgent on cost. Tied to that call was a second judgment: which single piece of work would do the most damage if the studio got the human-AI balance wrong on it — the core concept and brand voice, the variant production that resized one idea into hundreds of placements, the localisation of copy and assets market by market, or the tagging of assets in the media library. Aubert also had to set, as a percentage, how honestly her team could say where AI belonged and where it did not.

Once the deployment matrix was running, Aubert still had to sequence it: whether humans set the concept and voice first and AI scaled the variants and localisation afterward, whether AI generated everything first with humans editing it afterward, or whether both tracks ran in parallel with no defined order and were merged at the close. She had to decide where to put a review gate for rights, likeness, and disclosure risk — on every generated asset before it went to print or air, on a sample of assets if time allowed, or not at all, to keep the full five weeks free for production — and how strongly, again as a percentage, human judgement would override or refine whatever the AI produced at each gate. And she had to choose whether the human-AI split would be calibrated task by task or fixed at a single ratio applied across everything.

Once the launch telemetry came back, Aubert would have to diagnose the single biggest orchestration mistake to fix — calibrating the balance per task while keeping the creative core human, automating more of the workflow, removing AI entirely and returning to fully human craft, or concluding that the studio's approach needed no change. She would also decide whether to publish an AI-disclosure statement for the board's brand-safety review or leave the studio's AI use undisclosed, and what three-minute pitch to give the CMO on how the studio should deploy AI going forward — a complementary model, with AI handling divergence, scale, and analysis while people handled judgement and meaning; an AI-first model that automated the studio and reduced the human team; or a human-only model that banned generative tools entirely. On that pitch, too, she would state, as a percentage, how firmly she intended to argue it. She would be measured on where distinctiveness, delivery, engagement, and budget landed against the numbers on her dashboard that morning, and on whether the board's legal-risk flag stayed clear.

## Closing

Aubert pinned the six-channel media plan to the review wall beside last spring's post-mortem, distinctiveness stamped eighteen percent below where it needed to be. The CMO wanted the studio's diagnosis before the day was out; the retail calendar would not move past week five no matter what the studio decided. She uncapped a marker and wrote four words across the top of a blank sheet: distinctiveness, polish, speed, price. Five weeks started now.
