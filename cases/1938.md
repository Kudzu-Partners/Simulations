# Liffey Business School: Setting the Portfolio

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1938 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1938 — *Exec-Ed Strategy* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

Three documents lay side by side on the table in Deirdre Mulcahy's office above the river on the morning of Monday, January 19, 2026. The first was the *Financial Times* executive-education scorecard, which had moved Liffey Business School's custom-programme rank from #28 to #34 in a single year. The second was a note from the university's finance office raising the executive-development unit's annual surplus target by €1.5 million to help pay for a new campus building. The third was two lines from her custom director: the two corporate accounts that between them paid Liffey €3.1 million a year had each taken a meeting with INSEAD, and one had also met a fast-growing online provider selling short executive programmes at a fraction of Liffey's price.

The annual strategy review opened that afternoon and ran across four working sessions. At the end of the fourth, Mulcahy had to put a portfolio, a faculty-investment plan and a set of prices in front of the school's executive board and a dean who read the FT tables line by line.

## The Protagonist

Deirdre Mulcahy, 51, had been Director of Executive Development at Liffey Business School since 2019. She had joined the school in 2007 from a Dublin consultancy to build its custom-programme practice and ran that practice for nine years before taking the directorship. She held the unit's full profit-and-loss account. The surplus it returned to the university each year was the number by which the finance office knew her, and the FT table was the number by which the dean did.

## The Decision

Mulcahy had to settle three things and then defend a fourth. She had to divide the faculty's teaching capacity between the unit's product lines. She had to spend a fixed faculty-investment budget of €4.0 million. She had to set what Liffey charged. And she had to tell the board, in the fourth session, which of the school's targets her plan would miss.

The capacity question came with a hard edge: utilisation stood at 88% across a pool of 55 people. Four courses of action were on the table for the year: prune low-value open-enrolment programmes and move the freed teaching hours into custom; grow open, custom and qualifications together; hold all three lines at their current size; or spend part of the budget hiring practitioner faculty now, adding capacity that would not be billable until the following year.

The €4.0 million had four calls on it, and they competed. Billable teaching delivery converted directly into this year's surplus. Faculty research time produced no billable hours at all and fed one of the inputs the FT measured. New-programme development built the following year's catalogue. Practitioner recruitment eased the utilisation ceiling.

Pricing sat under the constraint of where Liffey stood in the market. Four postures were available: price by line, with custom carrying a premium and open priced competitively; hold the roughly 30% premium Liffey charged over unranked regional providers flat across everything; raise prices about 12% across the board and close the surplus gap arithmetically; or discount across the board and compete for volume against the online provider.

## The Company and its Setting

Liffey Business School — Executive Development (LBS-ED) was the executive arm of a leading Irish university business school, founded in 1994 and based in Dublin. It served senior managers and corporate clients in Ireland, the United Kingdom and continental Europe through three lines: open-enrolment programmes, including a flagship advanced management programme; custom programmes built for a single corporate client; and a small qualifications stream feeding the school's part-time Executive MBA.

The unit turned over €22 million a year at a 24% operating margin and employed 35 staff alongside the faculty pool of 55, a mix of tenured academics and senior practitioners. Its pricing rested on the FT position: ranked, Liffey charged roughly 30% more than unranked regional providers for comparable programmes. The board had already said what would happen if the custom rank fell below #40 — it would open a review of whether the university should be in executive education at all.

## The Situation

The position entering the review was set out in **Exhibit 1**. Custom repeat business had fallen from 78% to 69% in a year. The surplus target had risen by €1.5 million against a base the unit had met, not beaten. The €4.0 million faculty-investment budget was fixed, and in the year just closed it had gone €2.5 million to delivery and €0.5 million each to research, development and recruitment.

Four explanations of the six-place slide were current in the building. The rankings team pointed to faculty-research output and to the share of non-Irish participants, both of which had fallen over the same two years. The open-programmes director pointed to price, noting two open-programme bids lost to cheaper providers. The marketing lead pointed to visibility, noting that Liffey's submission to the FT had gone in late one year. Two long-serving professors pointed to the table itself, which had moved several schools more than five places in a year without any of them changing what they did.

The lines were not interchangeable (see **Exhibit 2**): they differed in how they were priced, in the faculty hours a euro of revenue consumed, in who bought them and who sat in the room, and in how far they could be scaled without new people. Custom carried the two accounts worth €3.1 million.

If Mulcahy changed nothing, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The same 40/40/20 split of capacity and the same €2.5 million of delivery spending had produced last year's surplus, which was €1.5 million short of the new target. The research output the rankings team blamed for the slide had been funded at €0.5 million and would be funded at €0.5 million again. And the two accounts would renew, or not, against offers Liffey had not yet answered.

## What Mulcahy Controlled

The review gave her four sessions and a decision in each.

In the first she had to state which line actually funded the unit and which line positioned it — custom, open or qualifications for each — name the single largest driver of the ranking slide from the four explanations in circulation, and decide how squarely the review would treat the tension between the surplus and the ranking as a real trade-off rather than a presentational problem.

In the second she had to set the share of faculty capacity going to custom, to open and to qualifications, the three totalling the whole pool; choose among pruning, growing everything, holding, or funding capacity; and decide how much of the mix would be built around the two anchor accounts.

In the third she had to split the €4.0 million across delivery, research, development and recruitment, and choose one of the four pricing postures.

In the fourth she had to name the play for the anchor accounts — co-designing multi-year work with them, matching the online provider's lower price, offering them the standard catalogue, or leaving them to the account team; say which horizon she would defend when the board pressed, this year's surplus, the ranking, or all three measures together over three years; and name the sacrifice, whether pruning the qualifications line, capping open volume, or telling the board the plan required none. Last, she had to decide how hard she would hold that position under questioning.

The dean wanted four numbers at every session: the FT custom rank, which stood at #34 against a floor of #40; the surplus accumulated against the raised €1.5 million target; custom repeat business, which stood at 69%; and faculty utilisation, which stood at 88% and which above 100% described a plan nobody could staff. The record of the review would show, session by session, the move made, the rank, the surplus and the retention figure.

## Closing

At 1:50 p.m. Mulcahy wrote the three lines on the whiteboard — custom, open, qualifications — and drew a column beside them for capacity and a column for price. The board met at the end of the fourth session. The FT submission closed after that, and the larger of the two anchor contracts came up for renewal in March.
