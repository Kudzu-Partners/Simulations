## Abstract

At 7:12 a.m. on a Wednesday morning in April 2026, CenturyVault Financial Group's Security Operations Center issued an automated alert that resolved, within eighteen minutes, into a confirmed active-ransomware incident: four Windows domain controllers were actively encrypting; the core-banking system was degrading; customer-facing mobile and ATM services were already intermittent. Alejandro Herrera, Chief Information Security Officer of a USD 36B-in-assets regional bank holding, stood in the bank's crisis-management room at 7:30 a.m. with four Round 1 decisions in front of him: which incident-response vendor (in-house, retainer partner, or emergency specialist), what gateway-isolation posture (monitor, partial, or full), what social-media communication (silence, acknowledge, or full disclosure), and what incident-command structure (CISO-led, CEO-led, or co-led). The Round 1 decisions would gate, twenty minutes later, the Round 2 ransom and regulatory-notification decisions. The case places the reader inside Herrera's head at the moment the 7:30 a.m. commit-window opened.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate an incident-command decision under active attack, time pressure, and incomplete information.
2. Analyze the trade-off between containment actions that preserve forensic evidence and actions that restore service.
3. Design a first-hour communication posture that preserves regulatory optionality.
4. Diagnose how Round 1 commitments gate Round 2 (ransom, disclosure, forensic) options.
5. Recommend a first-hour action set and defend it against a hostile board-level review.

At **7:30 a.m.** on **April 8, 2026**, **Alejandro Herrera** stood at the head of the long table in the CenturyVault crisis-management room on the thirty-first floor of the Torre Finance building in Buenos Aires. On the wall, three red lights pulsed: core-banking degraded, ATM network 32% offline, Active Directory compromised across four controllers. Below the lights, a countdown read *Round 1 commit: 00:29:47 and counting*. The bank's CEO, Rafael Castellanos, was in a car on Avenida del Libertador, inbound. The CISO had thirty minutes to commit a package of four decisions.

---

## The Protagonist

Alejandro Herrera, 46, had been CISO of CenturyVault since 2022, after twelve years at a global bank's regional security function and four years before that at a national-security-focused cybersecurity consultancy. He had joined CenturyVault with an explicit mandate — and a board-ratified budget — to build a modern incident-response capability; three years in, that capability existed but had never been stress-tested by a live, complex event. His compensation included an annual bonus tied to a cyber-maturity index and — unusually — a clawback clause tied to uninsured incident costs above a defined threshold. He held no equity in the bank. He had, in January 2026, signed a USD 1.8M annual retainer with a leading regional incident-response firm, Cyber Sentinel, plus a one-page emergency-engagement LOI with the global IR firm Meridian Cyber.

---

## The Decision

Herrera had to commit, by 8:00 a.m., a Round 1 package of four decisions:

1. **Incident-response vendor:** in-house (SOC + IT + engineering, no external), retainer (Cyber Sentinel, contracted capacity), emergency (Meridian Cyber, activated under LOI, 3x cost, broader capability).
2. **Gateway isolation:** monitor (passive observation), partial (selective network-segment isolation), full (all Internet-facing gateways severed).
3. **Social-media posture:** silence, acknowledge (acknowledge service issues without naming cause), full disclosure (public acknowledgment that the bank was under cyber attack).
4. **Incident command:** CISO-led, CEO-led, co-led.

Round 2 (8:00–8:30 a.m.) would present the ransom-payment decision, the regulatory-notification timing (Banco Central de la República Argentina requires notification within 24 hours of "material operational disruption"), and the law-enforcement-involvement decision. Round 1 commitments would remove certain Round 2 options — specifically, an "in-house" Round 1 IR commitment would disable the aggressive-recovery options in Round 2.

---

## Company Background

CenturyVault Financial Group was a mid-sized Argentine bank holding founded in 1962 and publicly listed on the Bolsas y Mercados Argentinos since 1987. It operated 340 branches, held USD 36B in assets, generated 2025 revenue of USD 1.4B and net profit of USD 240M (17.1% ROE). Its primary business lines were retail banking (55% of revenue), SME lending (22%), corporate banking (18%), and a small wealth-management practice (5%). The bank's technology stack had undergone a major modernization program from 2021 to 2024 — the core banking was a 2023 implementation of a regional platform, Active Directory was on Server 2019, and the bank had moved 38% of its workload to a public-cloud provider. Cybersecurity investment over 2022–2025 had been USD 14M per year; the 2026 budget was USD 18M.

The bank held a cyber-insurance policy with USD 50M of coverage, a USD 5M retention, and a 48-hour-notification clause. The policy covered ransom payment (subject to sanctions compliance), forensic costs, regulatory fines, and business-interruption above a defined threshold.

## Industry and Market Context

Ransomware attacks on Latin American banks had grown in frequency and sophistication from 2021 through 2025. Three peer banks in the region had experienced confirmed ransomware incidents during 2024; two had paid ransoms (the amounts were not public but were estimated in regional press reports at USD 4–10M equivalents), one had refused and had relied on backup restoration. In each of the three cases, reputation indices had declined by five to fifteen points in the two quarters following the incident; in the two banks that paid ransoms, regulatory scrutiny had intensified and one had been the subject of a formal regulator review.

The Banco Central de la República Argentina had, in November 2024, published updated cybersecurity guidance requiring regulated institutions to (a) maintain an incident-response plan tested annually, (b) notify the regulator within 24 hours of a material operational disruption, and (c) disclose to customers within 72 hours any confirmed exfiltration of customer personal data. The Argentine data-protection authority had separate — and partly inconsistent — notification requirements under its own framework.

Research on ransomware response converged on two findings. First, the median time from first-alert to core-banking restoration differed by a factor of four between banks with pre-activated IR retainers and banks that activated emergency IR under pressure. Second, ransom payments were correlated — causation unclear — with higher subsequent reputational damage, possibly because payments required disclosure that otherwise could have been avoided.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The SOC's first automated alert had fired at 7:12 a.m. The on-call analyst had escalated to Herrera at 7:18. By 7:24, forensic telemetry confirmed active encryption on four domain controllers and lateral movement into at least two application servers. At 7:27, the core-banking system's health monitor had flipped from "degraded" to "critical." The SOC had severed one domain-controller network interface at 7:28; the decision to isolate further was a CISO decision pending. No ransom note had yet appeared. Partial customer impact was already visible externally (three branches had opened late; ATMs were returning network errors).

### What Herrera knew

The attack pattern — encryption of domain controllers, lateral movement, no ransom note yet — was consistent with a recent family of ransomware, "LockerR2," that typically posted a ransom demand 45–90 minutes after initial encryption. LockerR2 had a documented decryption-failure rate of 18–27% even when the ransom was paid. The bank's backup system (immutable, air-gapped) had tested clean forty-six days earlier; restoration of the core-banking environment from backup was estimated at 6–12 hours.

### What Herrera did not know

Whether data exfiltration had occurred (a 72-hour customer-disclosure clock would start from confirmation); whether the CEO would back a "no-pay" posture under operating pressure; whether Cyber Sentinel had available bench capacity at 7:30 a.m. or whether an emergency Meridian activation was effectively required; whether the press already had the story.

### What others were saying

**Luisa Ferreyra**, Chief Technology Officer, pressed for full isolation. "Cut everything. Every minute live is a minute of lateral spread. Restore from backup in six hours. Don't call Meridian unless Cyber Sentinel punts."

**Rafael Castellanos**, CEO (on the car phone at 7:33), pressed for CEO-led command and early acknowledgment. "I take command. We acknowledge — we say 'operational incident, investigating' — at 8:00 or the press writes the story first. Call Meridian if that buys us speed."

**Diego Obregón**, General Counsel, pressed for CISO-led command and silence. "No social post without a forensic confirmation. Regulatory clock starts at 'material disruption' — let us not help define that. CISO-led. Cyber Sentinel."

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — CISO-Led, Retainer, Partial Isolation, Silence (the Obregón plan):** IR vendor = Cyber Sentinel; gateway isolation = partial (segment critical assets); social = silence through Round 1; command = CISO-led. Preserves regulatory optionality; slowest initial containment; preserves Meridian as a Round-2 activation option.

- **Option B — Co-Led, Emergency, Full Isolation, Acknowledge (the Castellanos plan):** IR vendor = Meridian (activate) + Cyber Sentinel (support); gateway isolation = full; social = acknowledge (non-specific); command = co-led. Fastest containment; highest Round 1 cost (estimated USD 3.5M incremental); opens full Round 2 option set.

- **Option C — CEO-Led, Retainer, Full Isolation, Silence (contrarian):** IR vendor = Cyber Sentinel; gateway isolation = full; social = silence; command = CEO-led. Consolidates authority; preserves regulatory optionality; narrows Round 2 IR options if incident exceeds Cyber Sentinel capacity.
