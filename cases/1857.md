# Designing the Cohort

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1857 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1857 — *Designing the Cohort* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 12 May 2026, Elina Saarinen read two messages that had landed seven minutes apart. Pohjola Energy's Chief HR Officer asked that the new executive program "build the leadership behaviours that hold up when a transition gets personal." Pohjola's commercial Chief Operating Officer asked that it "teach the P&L and market-design calls our senior people keep getting wrong." Both had signed the same one-page brief the week before. Neither had described the same program.

Saarinen had seven weeks. Aurora's design sign-off with Pohjola's steering group was due 1 July 2026, and everything Aurora would build for sixty senior managers — the objectives, the module mix, the way capability would be proven — had to be locked, priced inside a fixed fee, and ready to defend by that date.

## The Protagonist

Elina Saarinen, 42, had directed Aurora Executive Learning's custom-programs unit since 2022, after six years building corporate learning partnerships across the Nordics and eight years before that coordinating faculty inside Aurora's own MBA. She had joined Aurora in 2016, drawn by its reputation for modular, stackable program design — corporate participants could bank modules toward a full degree — and had spent much of her career since arguing that the same modularity that made Aurora flexible also made it undisciplined: clients, and her own faculty, always wanted one more module. Her unit's bonus pool moved with the margin held across the custom portfolio and with client renewal. Pohjola was Aurora's largest single account, and its CHRO had personally referred Aurora to two other Nordic utilities.

## The Decision

Saarinen had to convert Pohjola's aspiration into an actual program: which learning objectives it would chase, how the twelve contact days each manager could spare would be spent, how the program would prove capability had transferred rather than merely been attended, and how she would hold that design together once the steering group pushed back. Four sets of choices, made across four working sessions before the July sign-off, would either produce a program Aurora could deliver inside its fee, or a design that looked complete on paper and could not be run.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurora Executive Learning was the executive-education arm of a leading Finnish university, based in Helsinki's Otaniemi district and established in 2008. Alongside its part-time MBA and EMBA portfolio, it had built a fast-growing custom-program business: in 2025 it served 21 corporate clients and 540 participants, generating €12.4 million at a 24% operating margin, drawing on 31 program staff and a network of 95 faculty.

Pohjola Energy was a Nordic energy utility roughly midway through a multi-year clean-energy transition, reorganizing how its commercial, technical and regulatory teams worked together. On 5 May 2026 it had signed an €880,000 fixed-fee contract with Aurora for a program for sixty senior managers, delivered inside a nine-month window and completed before Pohjola's next strategy cycle began. The contract fixed the fee, the headcount and the calendar. It did not fix what the program would teach.

## The Situation

The constraints were exact, and several pulled against each other (see Exhibit 1). Aurora had to hold a 24% margin on the €880,000 fee — a floor of €211,000 — leaving €669,000 to spend on everything the program would contain: faculty time, materials, a possible simulation, coaching, assessment. Divided across sixty managers, that implied a cost-per-learner ceiling of roughly €11,150. Each manager could give no more than twelve contact days total, a cap Pohjola would not move. And the steering group had set a 90% completion floor — at least fifty-four of sixty had to finish — alongside a requirement that managers apply what they learned to a live transition project, not merely attend sessions about one.

An early diagnostic against the unedited brief was not encouraging: on Aurora's own 0–100 scales, it scored 12 for how clearly its content traced to a defined objective, 8 for evidence it would produce anything managers could apply on the job, and 15 for whether both sponsors would sign it, with projected completion at 78% — twelve points under the required floor. Unchanged, the brief would reach the 1 July sign-off satisfying neither sponsor and undeliverable as scoped.

## What Saarinen Controlled

The first session set how to answer a vague brief inside a fixed envelope. Saarinen could design straight to the brief, building leadership, commercial and change-management capability together in the same twelve days; let one sponsor set the direction, building the commercial program the COO had described since signing; contract four to six measurable objectives before designing a module, and write down what the program would not attempt; or start from topics her own faculty already taught well and shape objectives loosely around them. Tied to it was how to reconcile the CHRO and the COO: a tilt to the CHRO's framing, commercial content secondary; one ranked set both would sign, behaviour applied to commercial decisions; a tilt to the COO's framing, behaviour secondary; or a promise to both in full. A third dial set how much of an out-of-scope list to write, from almost nothing to a long, specific set of exclusions.

The second session allocated the twelve contact days and the budget across the module menu in Exhibit 2 — core leadership, commercial/energy-market, a possible custom simulation, a capstone project, and peer action-learning sets — the five required to sum to exactly twelve days. For the simulation, she could commission a €70,000 build anchored in Pohjola's own scenario; skip it for an €8,000 case study instead; or commission two, at €130,000 combined. For coaching, priced at €1,800 per participant, she could extend it to all sixty for about €108,000; target roughly twenty managers for about €36,000; or drop it, relying on peer action-learning instead.

The third session chose the assessment format and how it would prove transfer rather than attendance. One option used individual written reflections alone, submitted at the program's end. A second used the score the custom simulation already generated. A third paired a live capstone judged by Pohjola executives with 360-degree behavioural evidence from each manager's team. A fourth used a single graded written examination, administered in the program's final week. Separately, she could add the 360 across all sixty, at €240 each for €14,400, or skip it and rely on whatever the chosen format already produced. Two further dials set engineered engagement against pure rigour, and how much of a €0–80,000 line to spend coordinating the judges, sponsors and evidence a live capstone required.

The final session was the pitch — the moment the COO asked, mid-meeting, for two more market-design modules "without changing the price or the dates." Saarinen could agree to add both at the same price and window, changing nothing else; name an explicit trade-off: what would come out, or what the additions would cost in days and euros; or decline outright, telling the group the twelve-day design was locked. Opening the pitch was a separate choice: lead with the objectives, module mix, transfer evidence and economics as one case; lead by walking the group through every element the twelve days contained; or lead with the price, framing the discussion around how much program €880,000 had purchased. She also chose which KPIs to report — completion rate alone, reasoning the contractual floor mattered most; the full set of fit, completion, margin and cost per learner together; or no hard KPIs, presenting testimonials and satisfaction scores instead — and how firmly, on a 0–100 scale, to defend the design against the argument that more would only make it better.

## Closing

At 6:50 a.m., Saarinen opened a blank page and wrote the sign-off date at the top: 1 July 2026. Below it she drew four rows, one for each working session ahead, and two columns, one for each sponsor whose signature she needed on the same document. The diagnostic scores from that morning — 12, 8, 15, seventy-eight per cent completion — sat unresolved in her inbox. Seven weeks, twelve contact days per manager, and one fixed fee stood between the brief on her screen and a program the steering group would actually sign.
