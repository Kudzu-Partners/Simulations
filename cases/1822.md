# Wessex Mutual: Leading Member First

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1822 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1822 — *Lead the Member First Change* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 19 January 2027, the board of Wessex Mutual approved "Member First," a transformation that would close eight of the building society's 36 branches and reshape 180 of the 600 roles in its Operations & Member Services Division. Priya Nair, the division's director, had 120 days to launch the change with the division's trust still intact — judged not on the launch event, but on whether people changed how they worked afterward.

With the mandate came a folder Nair had not asked for. Six months earlier the board had sent its senior team through an external leadership assessment, and her own results were blunt about what years of running branch operations had reinforced: a style that led with facts, moved at pace, and disclosed little along the way.

## The Protagonist

Nair, 46, had run branch operations across southern England for six years before Wessex Mutual made her Director of Operations & Member Services two years earlier. Colleagues valued the trait some also found hard to read: she set a direction, moved fast, and expected people to keep up. No employer had ever treated her own psychology as part of the deliverable. Member First was about to.

## The Decision

Nair's task split into four decisions, taken in sequence: what to do with her own assessment before she spoke to anyone; how to bring her most senior and most doubtful manager onto the change; how to frame the announcement for the rest of the division; and, weeks later, how to prove to both the division and the board that the change was taking hold rather than merely announced.

## The Company and its Setting

Wessex Mutual was a member-owned UK building society holding £11.4 billion in assets for 740,000 members, with 2,300 employees (see **Exhibit 1**). Over 144 years it had built a conservative, relationship-led reputation on a claim its people repeated more than the board needed to: the mutual had never made a single compulsory redundancy. Member First would test that claim directly — closing eight branches and moving 180 of the division's 600 roles into digital-service work meant retraining people, not reassigning them, funded by a £2.4 million reskilling pot.

## The Situation

Three figures framed where Nair was starting from (see **Exhibit 2**). A trust score the people team tracked stood at 72 out of 100; below 55, the mutual's promise to look after its own was understood to have broken. A separate read of actual behaviour change — how much of the division was already working the new way, not merely discussing it — stood at 12. And a first read of how far her chosen behaviours would diverge from instinct put that figure at 20.

The person most likely to move those numbers did not sit on the board. Carol Bramwell, 22 years at Wessex Mutual, led 140 people and was treated as the nearest thing the division had to an institutional memory. Word had reached Nair that Bramwell had told a colleague she had sat through three restructurings, each ending with people she had hired losing their jobs. If Bramwell resisted openly, close to 60% of the division was expected to follow her rather than the org chart; if she came round, she stood to become the change's strongest advocate. Nothing in the trust data suggested Nair's own instinct — decisive, fact-first, sparing with disclosure — would soften that doubt rather than harden it.

## What Nair Controlled

Before speaking to anyone else, Nair had to decide what to do with the assessment: work through it and name where her decisive, guarded habits would read as cold here; skim the type it described and move to the change plan; or set it aside, reasoning she knew how she led. She then had to size the gap between that style and what the division needed: a real gap, meaning she had to lead against instinct with more disclosure, patience and visible empathy; a minor gap needing only a softer tone at launch; or no meaningful gap, since decisive and clear was exactly what the moment called for. She was also asked to settle on two behaviours to dial up for the change — empathy paired with holding back her own instinct to push; empathy paired with reading the room and building alliances; drive paired with decisiveness, for momentum; or optimism paired with charisma, to sell the vision and keep energy high — and to set, as a percentage, how firmly she would hold that style rather than her natural one.

Bramwell's comment left a diagnosis to make before any response: loss and fear for people she had spent two decades hiring; distrust that leadership's promises would hold this time; discomfort at losing control over her own patch; or plain obstruction from someone to be managed around. The diagnosis would shape how Nair engaged her — a longer coaching-style conversation working from Bramwell's concerns toward options she could help design; a conversation built on listening and reflecting before any ask; a negotiated arrangement built on a bonus and a bigger title; or a direct statement that the change was happening and her support was required. Nair also had to decide what she would promise Bramwell and what she would not — an offer to fight for the reskilling budget and give her a voice in its design while admitting not every role would survive unchanged; a promise that no one on Bramwell's team would be affected; or a warmer conversation committing to nothing specific — and how much of her own guardedness, as a percentage, to set aside in the room.

With the launch approaching, Nair had to choose a frame for a message to all 600 people, roughly half believing the mutual would look after them, half fearing it would not: naming what was ending before turning to the purpose it served; leading with why the change protected the mutual and its members; presenting the new digital roles as a career opportunity; or opening with the hard facts and letting them sit, with little reassurance alongside. Inside whichever frame she chose, she had to set, as a percentage, how much of the message would be hard facts — eight branches, 180 reshaped roles — against how much would be reassurance. She had to decide how her own voice would carry it: disciplined but recognisably herself, direct yet consciously empathetic; handed to the communications team as a polished script; or delivered without filter, on the view that bluntness was honesty. And she had to choose the channel — in person, through a division-wide gathering with small listening sessions; a cascade briefing pack down the management line; or a written email and intranet notice.

Weeks after launch, with reception mixed and an unconfirmed version of the closure list circulating, Nair had to decide how to respond: confirm the facts quickly, hold her frame, and explain the reskilling commitment openly; say nothing had been decided and call the list speculation; or focus on finding who had leaked it and tell managers to control the message. She had to decide how much of the £2.4 million fund to commit right then, up to the full amount, as a visible sign the promise still held. She had to decide what came next with Bramwell — make her a co-owner of the reskilling design and ask her to lead a listening session with fellow managers; hold a check-in to see where she now stood; or route the change around her to avoid further delay. And she had to prepare a short board update: an account of adoption, trust, her shift from instinct, and the resistance still to be worked through; a declaration of success based on the launch event; or a version that inflated the trust figure and left out the resistance the board had not yet seen.

## Closing

At her desk that morning, the assessment folder sat next to the one-page Member First timeline. Nair had a hundred and twenty days, a £2.4 million fund she had not yet allocated, and one decision in front of her before she led anyone else: open the folder, or push it aside and start with the plan.
