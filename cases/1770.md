# Helvetia Instruments AG: Jump the Curve

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1770 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1770 — *Jump the Curve* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 20 January 2026, Sabine Achermann's phone was already ringing when she reached her desk in Zürich. A venture-backed challenger called Lumea had gone live overnight with a cloud-native diagnostic platform, and by the time European markets opened it had taken one of Helvetia's marquee pharmaceutical accounts — a decade-old relationship — on a pitch built entirely on subscription data services rather than instruments. Within the week the board had commissioned a future-readiness review and handed her the file: a fixed capital pool of CHF 300 million to spend over three years, and one blunt question. Was Helvetia riding a dying curve, and what was she going to do about it?

She had ninety days to bring the board a three-year capital-allocation plan and a trajectory of readiness for what came next that it could believe — a single score the board wanted to track through each stage of the plan as a read on how prepared Helvetia actually was for the shift. Eighteen months after that, the board wanted to see the new-business revenue mix actually moving; if it had not, it would consider replacing management or selling the franchise while it still commanded a premium.

## The Protagonist

Achermann, 51, had been chief executive of Helvetia Instruments AG since 2023, after eighteen years inside its hardware engineering and commercial organisation. She had helped build the precision-instruments franchise into the category's global leader, and her reputation — and the incentive plan the board had set for her — rested on the operating discipline that made it the most profitable name in the sector. She had watched Lumea's rise for over a year without treating it as more than a curiosity. The board's mandate now asked her to commit a large share of the margin she had spent a career defending, against a market that barely registered yet in Helvetia's own revenue.

## The Decision

Achermann's task was to place Helvetia correctly on two curves and then act on the placement. She had to judge where the hardware core actually sat in its growth arc — still climbing with room to grow, settled into a comfortable middle stretch, or already at a structural peak — and how close the new software-and-analytics curve sat to overtaking it: a distant possibility, a window opening now, or a crossover already missed. Then she had to decide what the core's evident health actually meant: that its calm was exactly what it appeared to be, or that its calm concealed something worth a second look. Whatever she concluded would set the terms for every capital decision that followed, because the board would hold her account to the diagnosis she filed in the first ninety days.

## The Company and its Setting

Helvetia Instruments AG, founded in 1978 and headquartered in Zürich, made high-precision laboratory and diagnostic instruments trusted as the standard across pharmaceutical and clinical labs worldwide. It employed 4,600 people across Switzerland, Germany and Singapore and generated CHF 1.9 billion in annual revenue at an 18% operating margin — CHF 342 million a year of core cash flow that would also have to fund whatever came next — built on a large installed base and the service contracts that came with it (see **Exhibit 1**). That hardware franchise was growing at only 2% a year, a rate that had held for several years. The market Lumea had just entered — cloud-connected instruments and AI-driven diagnostic analytics, where recurring data revenue rather than mechanical precision decided the winner — was growing at more than 30% a year, though it still represented under 4% of Helvetia's own revenue. Helvetia had the balance sheet, the brand and the customer relationships to compete there. It also had a core organisation built on precision hardware, not eager to be told the model was changing.

## The Situation

The CHF 300 million pool the board had allocated could not fund everything at once. Every franc committed to building the new capability was a franc the core business would argue it needed to defend its own share against slower, but real, competitors. Waiting for proof that the new curve would win meant waiting until Lumea, or another entrant, had already set its standards and its customers' switching costs; moving before the market matured meant spending capital and management attention on a business that might still take years to reach scale; staging the commitment to the pace of the window carried its own risk, misjudging that pace and ending up either short of what a fast-moving market demanded or ahead of what a slow one yet justified. There was a window in which to act, but no calendar told her when it opened or when it closed.

If Achermann committed nothing new, Lumea would keep the account it had already won and pursue others built on the same pitch, the new-business share of Helvetia's revenue would stay near its current 3.8%, and the board's ninety-day deadline would arrive with a diagnosis on paper but no capital actually committed.

## What Achermann Controlled

Once the board had her diagnosis, Achermann set the pace and size of the leap. On timing, she could hold back, waiting until the new software curve had clearly proven itself before committing serious capital to it; she could stage the commitment now, sizing each year's spend to how fast the window actually seemed to be opening; or she could commit the bulk of the pool at once, betting that whoever set the standard first would keep its customers. Whatever the timing, she also set a specific figure for how much of the CHF 300 million to commit in the first year, and she named one condition that would cause her to accelerate the plan: a second marquee account lost to Lumea, conclusive proof that the market had matured, or no named trigger, reviewed annually instead.

Funding the leap meant reallocating from the core, and here she had three routes to the new capability. She could acquire a software-native team outright, paying a premium up front for capability that already worked. She could partner with an existing software platform, moving faster and sharing both the cost and the control. Or she could build the capability in-house from nothing, trading speed for full ownership of whatever the team eventually produced. Each route accrued a different pace of capability build — the working software-and-analytics product Helvetia could actually point to by the time the plan closed. Alongside that choice she set three further numbers: how much to cut from the core's hardware R&D budget to help fund the build, how much service-contract cash to harvest for the same purpose, and how much additional capital to stage into the new curve across the second and third years, on top of whatever she had already committed in year one — with the total across all three years still bound by the fixed CHF 300 million (see **Exhibit 2**).

For the final act, Achermann chose how the organisation itself would take up the new curve. She could house the new capability inside the existing hardware division, reporting to the leaders who already ran the core. She could hold an all-hands meeting and publish a new vision statement setting the direction. She could let an acquired team operate with full autonomy, resourced and evaluated on its own terms. Or she could stand up a separate venture unit with its own leadership and its own mandate. Alongside that bet she decided whether to rewire compensation toward recurring revenue and platform adoption across the whole company, pilot the new approach inside the new unit only, or leave everyone's pay tied to hardware units sold as before. Finally, she set the posture she would take in front of the board: present the plan as fully timed and resourced, recommend waiting another year for more evidence, or propose harvesting the core and returning cash to shareholders instead.

## Closing

At her desk that morning, Achermann set the ninety-day deadline in her calendar and turned to the first blank page of the diagnosis the board was waiting for. The capital pool was fixed at CHF 300 million, and the window, whatever its size, was already open.
