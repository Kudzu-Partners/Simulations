# Atlântico Foods: The Kenya Bet

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1951 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1951 — *Manage Across Markets* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 4 February 2026, Inês Vilar read the January close for Atlântico Foods' East Africa business twice before she accepted it. Six months after the Nairobi office opened, the annualised run-rate stood at US$3.4 million against a Year-1 target of US$9.0 million — 38% of plan, and a gap of US$5.6 million. Beneath the accounts sat a delivery report she had commissioned the week before. Of the outlets the plan had named as targets, Atlântico's own trucks were serving 11%.

The board had given her 90 days. At the review she had to present a corrected operating model for Kenya and a decision on which partner Atlântico would enter the market with. If she did not, the US$18 million three-year programme would be paused and the Nairobi office closed.

## The Protagonist

Inês Vilar, 44, had been Regional General Manager for East Africa since the mandate was created, having spent the previous six years running Atlântico's commercial operation across southern Iberia and, before that, the integration of its Moroccan plant. Her reputation inside the company rested on distribution: she had rebuilt the Iberian chilled network into the asset the board most often cited when it described what Atlântico was good at. She had written the Kenya business plan, and it was her recommendation that the company enter with the European go-to-market playbook rather than design a new one. The expatriate secondments now filling the senior roles in Nairobi carried her signature. Her variable compensation moved with the programme's contribution and its milestones.

## The Decision

Vilar had to decide which parts of a model that worked in Iberia should survive in Kenya, what should be rebuilt, who would run the business, and on whose balance sheet the market would be entered. She had to decide all of it inside one quarter: the board's next review was the last one before the funding gate.

## The Company and its Setting

Atlântico Foods, S.A., headquartered in Lisbon, produced and distributed packaged dairy, juices and shelf-stable nutrition products. It generated EUR 410 million in annual revenue at a 12% margin on earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, employed 1,900 people, and operated four plants across Iberia and one in Morocco (see **Exhibit 1**). It had built a profitable franchise in Portuguese-speaking and Maghreb markets on one distinguishing capability: an ambient-and-chilled distribution network that reached modern-trade supermarkets efficiently and reliably.

Kenya had 55 million people and was growing at about 5% a year. Its grocery trade was shaped unlike Iberia's: 68% of grocery volume moved through informal *dukas* — small independent kiosks — and open-air markets, rather than through the supermarket chains Atlântico's plan had counted on. An unbroken cold chain existed in two cities and not beyond them. Shelf prices and basket sizes were set by shoppers buying for the day rather than for the week.

## The Situation

The Kenya entry had been executed as planned, and the plan was not working (see **Exhibit 1**). The direct fleet reached 11% of target outlets. Chilled stock-keeping units (SKUs) — 40% of the portfolio — were spoiling at 22% between plant and shelf, against 3% in Iberia. Of 24 local hires, 3 had category or modern-trade experience, and Vilar had been covering the shortfall with expatriates at a fully loaded cost of US$220,000 each per year; five of them ran at US$1.1 million a year. The group's people function scored the depth of local management and the existence of named successors on a 0-to-100 index, and East Africa scored 18.

The board would read five numbers at the review. Outlet reach was the share of target outlets Atlântico actually served in a month, then 11%. Chilled spoilage was the share of chilled product lost before sale, where a lower number was better, then 22%. Local capacity was the 0-to-100 management-depth index, then 18. Contribution was the annualised run-rate the business generated, then US$3.4 million. And the board expected a three-year payback case for whichever partner Vilar recommended. Nothing in the accounts moved on its own: on the current model the run-rate held near 38% of plan, chilled product kept spoiling at 22%, the expatriate line kept running at US$220,000 a head, and the pause trigger the board had written into the programme applied at the 90-day review.

Two partners were pressing for an answer, and a South-African competitor was in conversation with the same joint-venture candidate (see **Exhibit 2**). Against all of it stood the programme envelope the board had approved: US$18 million over three years, of which nothing had yet been committed to a corrective plan.

## What Vilar Controlled

The quarter gave her four decision windows.

First, the diagnosis she would put in front of the board. Four readings were available. **Execution** meant the model was right and under-delivered: add trucks and salespeople, run it harder. **Market** meant Kenya was not yet ready for the category: scale the ambition back and wait. **People** meant the local team was too inexperienced to run the plan: bring in more expatriates. **Transfer** meant the brand and the recipes travelled and the rest did not: rebuild the model for Kenya. She also had to name the single largest source of the US$5.6 million gap — the route-to-market mismatch, the cold-chain losses, the capability gap, or European pack sizes and price points — and split her team's remaining effort between redesigning the model and improving execution of the one in place.

Second, the operating model. She would set the share of volume routed through informal-trade aggregators and wholesalers, the remainder served direct to modern trade. Four cold-chain answers were on the table. She could leave the chilled range as planned and commit no cold-chain spending, keeping the full chilled margin and the capital for other uses. She could build distributed chilled depots and last-mile capacity, at capital cost and more slowly, retaining that margin. She could reformulate the range toward ambient, shelf-stable products, faster and cheaper at a lower margin. Or she could place chilled depots in the two cities where the cold chain existed and sell ambient products everywhere else, at part of the capital cost and part of the chilled margin. Pack architecture offered three answers: leave European pack sizes and price points unchanged, holding the price architecture the brand carried elsewhere; add sachet formats on the fastest lines; or rebuild the range around Kenyan shilling (KSh) sachet price points for the duka shelf. Funding the redesign was itself a choice: US$1 million, US$3 million or US$5 million.

Third, the people model. She would set the share of leadership roles held by local managers by Month 24. Four ways of filling them were open. Expatriates at US$220,000 each per year were the fastest route to execution. Experienced local managers, recruited from other companies at a premium to market pay, bought capability at once. A graduate academy with structured development grew general managers internally, over a longer horizon. A lean expatriate core above an academy pipeline combined the two. She would also decide whether expatriates worked with no handover mechanism, with informal shadowing, or each paired with a named local successor on a timeline — and how much budget, US$0, US$1.5 million or US$3 million, moved from expatriate salaries to capability building.

Fourth, the entry decision. She would choose the exclusive distributor, the 50/50 joint venture, or a hybrid (see **Exhibit 2**). She would commit within the quarter, ahead of the conversations the competitor was having, or keep negotiating into the next season for better terms. She would govern the relationship through a supply contract alone, leaving disputes to commercial practice; through agreed volumes and a quarterly review; or through board seats with defined decision rights, brand-discipline clauses and an exit clause. Last, she had to decide how much of the three-year financial case to rebuild on the model she had chosen rather than on the plan the board had approved.

## Closing

At the end of the afternoon Vilar wrote the five numbers on a single sheet and set the January close beside the original plan. The board would sit in 90 days. She had one quarter to say what travelled, what she would rebuild, who would run the business in 24 months, and whose name would be on the entry.
