# GharSe: Ninety Days to Fix Two Sides of a Marketplace

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1852 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1852 — *Experience by Design* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the second Monday of April 2026, Naina Reddy sat at her desk in GharSe's Bengaluru headquarters with three documents open at once: a consumer-satisfaction report showing sentiment down fifteen points in a single quarter, a professional-attrition dashboard showing GharSe's top-rated home-service providers leaving twice as fast as six months earlier, and an email from the board setting the terms of the next ninety days. GharSe was a four-year-old marketplace that connected households across six Indian cities with the plumbers, electricians, cleaners, beauticians and appliance technicians who served them, and its founding promise — a fixed price, quoted upfront, with no haggling — now sat behind a program that had to repair both sides of that marketplace inside one shared budget.

The board's terms were specific. Reddy, GharSe's CX Manager, had one quarter, a ₹9 crore improvement budget and six engineering sprints to get consumer satisfaction, the platform's ability to fill a booking with a qualified professional, and the rate at which customers returned within ninety days, all rising together. If any of the three kept falling, the board would cut the improvement budget by 40 percent and pause the launch of two more cities already on the roadmap.

## The Protagonist

Reddy, 34, had joined GharSe in 2023 as its first dedicated customer-experience hire, after six years in customer-operations roles at a food-delivery company and a ride-hailing platform. She reported to the co-founder who ran product, and her mandate since the day she joined had been to own the experience of a booking from the moment a household opened the app to the moment the professional who did the job was paid. She had never before had to defend a plan touching both sides of the business to a board this openly nervous.

## The Decision

Across the quarter, Reddy would have to commit, in sequence, to a diagnosis of where the platform's experience was actually breaking down, a costed set of fixes built against a hard budget and engineering-capacity limit, a redesign of how bookings were matched and how trust was rebuilt after two incidents had gone public, and — once the quarter's results came in mixed — a single move to correct whichever side of the marketplace had fallen behind.

## The Company and its Setting

GharSe connected 1.9 million households with 48,000 vetted home-service professionals across six cities, completing roughly 540,000 bookings a month at an average order value of ₹850, an 18 percent take rate, and an annualized run-rate of about ₹5,500 crore in bookings value. Its most-cited selling point among consumers was the fixed, upfront price quoted before a booking was confirmed. A rival, UrbanReady, had entered three of GharSe's six cities in the past year promoting a guaranteed ₹45,000 a month for participating professionals and a booking flow it advertised as faster than GharSe's own.

## The Situation

Consumer NPS had fallen from 46 to 31 over the quarter. Match-success — the share of bookings filled by a qualified professional inside the promised window — had slipped from 88 percent to 79 percent. Repeat usage, the share of customers who booked again within ninety days, had dropped from 62 percent to 54 percent. Separately, monthly churn among GharSe's top-rated professionals had doubled, from 4 percent to 8 percent (Exhibit 1). In the same month, a no-show and a safety complaint had each gone viral on social media, and the post-booking trust score the customer-service team tracked had fallen nine points as a result.

If Reddy changed nothing, the direction was already set: professionals would keep leaving for UrbanReady's guarantee, match-success would keep slipping as fewer of them were left to fill bookings, repeat usage would keep falling as more bookings went unfilled or badly filled, and the board's ninety-day deadline would arrive with all three metrics still headed the wrong way.

## What Reddy Controlled

In the first round, Reddy set the frame for the diagnosis itself. She could treat it as a problem inside the consumer booking flow, trusting that fixing what customers saw would carry the business; treat the dip as a matter of individual conduct, tightening the rules on specific professionals and specific customers; score every touchpoint on the consumer side and the professional side by frequency and impact, and look for where the two overlapped; or line GharSe's touchpoints up against UrbanReady's, point by point, and close whatever gaps that comparison revealed. Alongside the frame, she had to name the single highest-leverage problem: the earnings gap itself, closed by matching UrbanReady's guarantee for every professional on the platform; the seven-day wait professionals faced to be paid, and the slow pace of resolving their disputes; friction inside the consumer booking flow, made more visible by comparison with UrbanReady's app; or no structural cause at all, a difficult quarter that would correct itself without intervention. A separate dial, running from zero to one hundred, set how much of that diagnosis rested on funnel data, customer and professional feedback and the matching dashboard, versus her own read of the situation.

In the second round, Reddy built a portfolio of touchpoint investments against the ₹9 crore budget and the six sprints, choosing among six proposed fixes spanning both sides of the marketplace, from a redesigned booking flow to faster professional payouts (Exhibit 2).

In the third round, she set two platform decisions at once. The matching objective could favor speed, assigning whichever professional was free soonest to raise the share of bookings filled on time; quality, always assigning the highest-rated available professional regardless of how long the wait grew; fairness, distributing bookings to retain mid-tier professionals while also protecting top-rated professionals' earnings; or price, assigning whichever professional was cheapest among those who cleared the customer's upfront quote. The trust mechanism could be an escrow held until the job was confirmed complete, paired with a redo guarantee; all four of the other mechanisms combined together into a single package; a strengthened two-way ratings system, built on reviews that both sides had verified; an insurance-backed safety cover extended to every booking on the platform; or an identity and background verification badge shown on a professional's profile. Two further dials came with the choice: one, from zero to one hundred percent, set how much she lifted effective take-home pay for top-rated professionals against UrbanReady's guarantee; the other, from zero to four, set how many additional verification steps she was willing to add to the booking flow.

In the fourth round, with the quarter's results in and mixed, Reddy picked one rebalancing move within whatever budget remained: a floor placed under top professionals' effective earnings, funded from what remained of the budget; a booster added to the consumer-facing trust features the plan had already funded; spending nothing further and carrying the rest of the quarter on the budget already committed; or matching UrbanReady's guarantee outright for every professional on the platform. She also chose how to handle the two incidents that had gone public — no response, on the expectation that the two posts would fade without one; a redo-or-refund resolved quickly and made visible to other customers; or the same redo-or-refund resolved quietly, case by case, without a public statement — and how to frame the quarter for the board: presenting the consumer-side numbers as the win, with an argument that the professional side would follow; presenting the quarter as a recovery on both sides, tied together in a single account; or presenting the single match-rate figure to the board as the quarter's one headline result. A final dial, from zero to one hundred, set how firmly she held that framing once the board pushed back.

## Closing

At 9:05 a.m., Reddy closed the three documents, opened a blank slide for the board, and began drafting the diagnosis she would have to defend for the rest of the quarter. She had ninety days, ₹9 crore, six sprints, and two sides of a marketplace to answer for.
