# Anatolia Sensörik: Choosing a Position Before the Blocs Close

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1842 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1842 — *Network Advantage* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 5 May 2026, Zeynep Arslan, Chief Strategy Officer of Anatolia Sensörik A.Ş., read the wire alert twice before she trusted it: a global Tier-1 automotive supplier had announced an exclusive autonomous-driving alliance bloc. To the rest of the mobility industry, that meant the open seats in the alliance network were about to close, and whoever had not claimed one would be shut out. Within the hour, Anatolia's chief executive gave Arslan one quarter and roughly USD 28 million of alliance-investment budget to decide who Anatolia would ally with, on what terms, and how it would keep any value those alliances created.

The board had reached its own conclusion two weeks earlier, after a formal review of performance: Anatolia's design-win rate — the share of sensor competitions it actually won against rival suppliers — had fallen from 31% to 22% over the previous year, well below the 28% aspiration the board had set. Its instruction to Arslan was direct: a firm that far below its own target needed to search for new positions rather than protect the ones it already held, and it needed a decision inside the quarter, before the industry's emerging blocs closed around it.

## The Protagonist

Arslan, 44, had spent nine years in corporate development at one of the Tier-1 integrators now circling Anatolia's own partners, before joining Anatolia four years earlier to build its alliance function from scratch. She had been promoted to Chief Strategy Officer eleven months earlier, after her predecessor left following two consecutive quarters of design-win losses. Her annual bonus was tied partly to the recovery of the design-win rate and partly to measures the board had only just introduced: the strength of Anatolia's network position, and how much of the value it created actually flowed back to the company. This was the first quarter in which both would be scored.

## The Decision

Over the coming weeks Arslan would work through four linked calls under the same one-quarter clock and budget ceiling: diagnose where Anatolia's real advantage came from and which unfilled seats to pursue; assemble a portfolio inside the USD 28 million budget and Anatolia's engineering capacity, including a call on two rival partners who would not both accept Anatolia at once; once relationships were live, decide how visibly to broker between partners and how to answer one that discovered Anatolia's tie to its rival; and, once a competitor built a rival portfolio, decide how to keep the value created from dispersing to the partners themselves, setting the rule for whether to keep searching or hold what she had built next quarter.

## The Company and Its Setting

Anatolia Sensörik A.Ş. was an Istanbul-based maker of automotive sensors and embedded-perception modules, generating USD 240 million in annual revenue at a 14% EBIT margin, selling radar and camera-adjacent sensor modules to mid-tier vehicle manufacturers across Europe, Türkiye and the Gulf. It owned no dominant patent and no platform of its own — the technologies that actually differentiated a mobility offering, high-definition mapping, edge-AI chips, vehicle-to-everything communication and sensor-fusion software, belonged to other companies entirely. What it did have was a seat at almost every table: two rival mapping companies, known inside the firm as GeoNorth and AtlasMap, and three Tier-1 integrators all ran deals through Anatolia despite barely dealing with one another directly. What that seat was worth depended on what Arslan did with it this quarter.

## The Situation

As Exhibit 1 sets out, Anatolia's network map carried roughly 18 candidate partners, of which about eight occupied genuinely attractive bridging positions — seats between camps no other firm currently held. Engineering could properly resource only three to four in a single quarter; beyond that, delivery on existing programs would suffer. The GeoNorth–AtlasMap pair would not both accept Anatolia if the other was also in the portfolio. Brokering too openly between introduced partners risked letting them deal directly and cut Anatolia out; an undefended broker position had, by industry experience, a shelf life of two to three quarters. The portfolio was estimated to create roughly USD 60 million of joint value across the network — nothing in that estimate said how much Anatolia itself would keep. Left unchanged, the eight open positions would be filled by rivals before quarter's end, and the firm's trajectory — design-win rate already down nine points — pointed toward the fallback of a commodity supplier, margins near 6–7%.

Exhibit 2 summarizes the board's scorecard: Network Centrality at 18 of 100 (value of position, not partner count), Rent Appropriation at 12 of 100 (share of network value returning to Anatolia), and Partner Trust at 50 of 100 (relationship health across a portfolio already carrying one open conflict) — plus positions resourced against the three-to-four ceiling, and how much of the USD 28 million committed.

## What Arslan Controlled

The first stage asked where Anatolia's advantage actually came from: its position, bridging partners with no direct link to each other; the sensor module, out-engineering rivals on the technology itself; the total number of partners it could sign; or its existing relationships, protected while the market settled. Tied to that was which target class to pursue first — the three highest-value bridging seats no one else held, the single most central hub partner regardless of conflict risk, or the cheapest peripheral partners, spread across the largest number of ties — plus a percentage dial for how strongly to act on the board's finding that Anatolia should search for new positions rather than defend its current ones.

The second stage shaped the portfolio: three to four positions in real depth with exclusivity attached; seven or more partners at a lighter level of commitment; or one partner in full depth, the rest set aside. Bound up in that was the GeoNorth–AtlasMap conflict — resolved by committing to one firm and disclosing the choice openly, by engaging both without disclosing either relationship to the other, or by engaging neither and leaving that pair of seats unfilled. She then split the USD 28 million three ways, among the seats between camps, the engineering depth inside alliances already committed, and light, low-cost ties across the periphery, the three figures required to total no more than the budget available.

The third stage arrived once relationships were live. A partner that discovered Anatolia's tie to its rival could be met by reaffirming the choice already made and renegotiating terms, by granting the exclusivity demanded, or by declining to respond and continuing to engage both sides as before. Once two partners Anatolia had introduced began meeting on their own, she could embed Anatolia into the relationship technically and contractually, publicize the connection she had brokered, or step back from introducing partners altogether. A smaller partner — itself a part-supplier to Anatolia's largest rival — offered a unique innovation tie: it could be accepted on bounded, time-limited terms under restrictive intellectual-property conditions; accepted in full by sharing Anatolia's complete sensor-fusion roadmap; or declined outright. Whatever remained of the USD 28 million could go to durable lock-in: contracts, embedded interfaces, shared standards.

The fourth stage asked how Anatolia would keep the value once a rival assembled a competing portfolio: writing exclusivity clauses, embedded standards and switching costs into every agreement; leaving agreements loosely governed and relying on the ecosystem to divide value fairly; or competing on price to win volume, independent of the position built so far. Next came whether to concentrate on the highest-value seats and end the weaker ties, add more partners to exceed the rival's count, or leave the portfolio unchanged — and a rule for the next cycle: a formal target combining design-win rate and network position, searching below it and consolidating above; total partner count, added regardless of the network's condition; or no formal rule at all. Last, a percentage for how firmly to argue the position-based case to the board, against a product- or partner-count-based one.

## Closing

At 8:55 that morning, Arslan opened the network map on her screen — eighteen partners, a scattering of ties, Anatolia sitting somewhere in the middle of lines it had not drawn. The quarter's clock had already started, and beside the map sat one blank sheet for the portfolio.
