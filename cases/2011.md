# Helveti Labs: The Runway Clock

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-2011 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2011 — *Opportunity Scout — Helveti Labs and the Runway Clock* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Tuesday, 17 March 2026, Livia Steiner took a call from the cantonal innovation agency that had funded her company and was told that the second tranche of the grant — CHF 60,000 she had already written into the cash-flow sheet — was frozen. The agency would release it when Helveti Labs GmbH could put evidence of validated customer demand in front of it, and not before. The officer named the end of the second quarter.

That gave Steiner four months. In that time she had to choose one of the three opportunities the company had been holding open since January, put customer evidence behind it, and carry it into a meeting with the angel investor who had offered Helveti Labs CHF 250,000 on a SAFE. The bank balance that afternoon was CHF 118,000 against a burn of CHF 22,000 a month.

## The Protagonist

Livia Steiner, 28, was co-founder and chief executive of Helveti Labs. She had finished a mechanical engineering degree at the university of applied sciences in Winterthur in 2021, spent three years as a product engineer at an automation supplier in Schaffhausen, and left in November 2025 to start a company with Jonas Frei, from her cohort. In January 2026 the two of them won a CHF 130,000 idea-stage grant from the cantonal innovation agency, added CHF 30,000 from family, and incorporated inside a fortnight. Steiner had never run a company before. She had told the agency, and the two colleagues who joined part-time in February, that Helveti Labs would not commit to a market until it had a reason to. The company had a thesis, a workspace and three written hypotheses; it had no product and no customer.

## The Decision

Steiner had to decide, month by month, which of the three opportunities Helveti Labs would commit to, what evidence she would buy to test it, and whether to hold that commitment or change it once the evidence came back — with the cash that paid for each month of deciding coming out of the account that paid the salaries.

## The Company and its Setting

Helveti Labs GmbH was incorporated in Winterthur, canton of Zurich, in January 2026 and worked from four hot desks in a Technopark co-working space. The team was four: Steiner and Frei full-time, Milena Zogg on customer research and Ruben Achermann on finance two days a week each. Monthly burn was CHF 22,000 — two salaries, cloud credits and the desks — or CHF 5,500 a week (see **Exhibit 1**).

Three opportunity hypotheses sat on the whiteboard, each written up as a one-page brief, none tested with a paying customer (see **Exhibit 2**). SwissCare Scheduling was an appointment no-show predictor for private medical practices: roughly 6,500 practices, a sales cycle practice managers put at nine to twelve months, patient data governed by the revised federal data-protection act, and incumbent vendors already embedded in the clinic software. FairShip Returns was a returns-orchestration widget for small Swiss e-commerce merchants: quick to build and quick to put in front of buyers, in a category already served by several products, where published pricing sat at CHF 29 per month and tests Steiner had seen landed nearer CHF 19. AlpTrace Provenance was a blockchain-verified provenance record for premium alpine food producers: roughly 300 producers in the country, high gross margin per contract, slow procurement, and nothing to show that any producer treated provenance as urgent. It was the one Steiner and Frei talked about after work.

## The Situation

Divided by the CHF 22,000 monthly burn, the CHF 118,000 in the bank on 17 March was 5.4 months of company. The frozen tranche was not in that number.

Two fixed resources stood between Steiner and any evidence. The first was customer access: forty structured conversations, booked through a research panel Zogg had contracted in February, one credit buying one conversation, with further credits available from the panel at CHF 250 each. The second was an experiment budget of CHF 12,000, for landing pages, ad spend, prototypes and anything that had to be built before it could be tested. Neither replenished. Spread across three opportunities, forty conversations came to about thirteen each.

The arithmetic of changing nothing was not in dispute. Four months at CHF 22,000 took CHF 118,000 down to CHF 30,000 — about six weeks of company — with the tranche still frozen, no opportunity committed and no customer evidence in hand. Each week spent deciding rather than testing cost CHF 5,500 whether or not anything came of it.

The angel had been specific. She would fund CHF 250,000 on a SAFE for a company that had committed to one opportunity, could show a real demand signal behind it, and scored above 65 out of 100 on the opportunity-fit rubric the two of them had agreed. She had said she did not fund companies that were still weighing options.

Inside Helveti Labs the positions were already taken. Frei wanted AlpTrace. Achermann had built a sheet arguing that only FairShip could be built, priced and sold inside the runway, and would not discuss the others until Steiner ruled on the ranking. Zogg had said that thirteen conversations would settle nothing, whichever way the ranking went.

## What Steiner Controlled

Steiner's decisions came in four monthly blocks, each committed before the next began.

The first block was the screen. She scored the three on the five headings the rubric used — problem severity, market size, timing, team fit, and feasibility inside the runway — and set which ranked first. She decided how many to carry into validation: all three, the top two, or the leader alone. She named the opportunity she was killing, if any. And she fixed, from nothing to everything, how much weight the rubric carried against her own judgement when the two disagreed.

The second block was validation. She decided where the forty credits went — spread across all three at about thirteen apiece, split between two at about twenty each, or concentrated with about thirty-two on the leader and eight on the runner-up. She chose the tactic the month was built around: a smoke-test landing page, structured problem interviews, or a concierge MVP carrying a paid pre-order or letter-of-intent ask. She fixed in advance what she would accept as proof — a headline sign-up count, a set of transcripts, or money and signatures. And she committed experiment spend of nothing, CHF 4,000, or CHF 8,000 against the CHF 12,000 budget.

The third block was the call on the committed opportunity: persevere, change one hypothesis and continue, or kill it and move. She recorded whether she had written down the condition that would reverse the decision before the evidence arrived, had left it as an intention to watch the numbers, or had set none. She chose how long to take — deciding inside the week, buying one further week of confirmation at CHF 5,500, or taking the full month at CHF 22,000. And she decided whether to buy ten further interview credits for CHF 2,500, twenty for CHF 5,000, or none.

The fourth block was the pitch. She chose what to lead with: the size of the opportunity, an open position between two candidates, or one committed opportunity tied to the evidence, the unit economics and the next milestone. She set the ask and the use of proceeds. She decided how to answer when the angel went at the plan's weakest assumption. And she stated, as a number from 0 to 100, how far her commitment rested on evidence rather than on expectation.

Four numbers followed her across the four months. **Runway** was cash divided by the CHF 22,000 monthly burn, in months. **Cash in bank** was the balance in Swiss francs. **Validation evidence**, scored 0 to 100, was the falsifiable demand signal behind the committed opportunity, on a definition agreed with the agency that excluded vanity metrics. **Opportunity fit**, also 0 to 100, scored that opportunity against the rubric, and carried the angel's threshold. Beside them sat the credits and the experiment budget still unspent, and which opportunity was on record as committed. Each month the move Steiner had made, the opportunity committed, and the three scores went into one row of a single sheet.

## Closing

At 19:20 on 17 March, Steiner wrote the three names on the whiteboard and drew the five rubric headings beside them. Frei was still at his desk. The agency wanted its evidence by the end of the second quarter, the balance was CHF 118,000, and there were forty conversations in the account.
