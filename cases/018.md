# Casa Palma Boutique Hotel: Twelve Months in the Revenue Chair

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-18 · **Companion simulation:** Express 018 — *Hotel Revenue Maximizer* · **v2.0 · September 2026**

---

On the last Sunday of December 2025, Marisol Villanueva let herself into the second-floor office at Casa Palma with a key she had been given eleven weeks earlier and had not yet used on a weekend. The hotel was full that night — the week between Christmas and New Year always filled itself — and from the window she could hear the rooftop bar running two floors above her. On the desk were the twelve months of 2026, printed one to a page, and a single sheet from Vela Hospitality's Miami office headed *Casa Palma — Composite Revenue Management Index*. The sheet listed five measures and what each was worth out of a hundred. It did not say what to do about any of them.

She had been hired in October to hold a chair that had been empty since August, when the previous director of revenue had left three weeks after the owner's investment committee approved a three-year transformation plan. From January, and for twelve months, Villanueva would set the hotel's rate, decide how much of the house to give the online travel agencies, spend or not spend two budget lines, and answer one group enquiry a month. Nobody above her would approve those five things individually. At the end of December 2026 the Miami sheet would produce one number, and that number would be the year.

## The Protagonist

Villanueva, 39, had trained at the Universidad Externado and at Cornell's hotel school, and had spent ten years in revenue roles — six at two international chains, four at an independent resort group in the Riviera Maya, where she had taken a 240-room property through a channel restructuring that moved its direct share from nine per cent to thirty-two over twenty-eight months. Vela had recruited her on that record and on a second, less quantifiable one: a reputation among the general managers she had worked for of not moving every lever in her first week.

Her contract carried a three-year gate tied to the transformation plan and a monthly scorecard that Rosa Villarreal, Vela's managing director, was known to read on the day it arrived.

## The Decision

Villanueva had to commit, month by month across 2026, a revenue plan built from five decisions, and to declare to Villarreal in January which of three plans on the table she was running for the year.

The five decisions repeated every month. She set one blended room rate for the property, between sixty and two hundred and fifty dollars. She set the share of transient inventory released to the online travel agencies, from none of it to all of it. She set a marketing and loyalty budget and a service-quality budget, each between zero and twenty thousand dollars a month. And she accepted or declined the one group booking that came in that month, each with its own room block, its own nightly rate and its own food-and-beverage spend attached (see **Exhibit 4**).

What she did not have was a second rate. Casa Palma sold four room categories, and the system Vela had installed carried one blended number for all of them; length-of-stay and advance-purchase fencing, which Villanueva had used in every previous job, were not available to her. Nor was the commission. The hotel's agreement with its online travel agencies was signed at eighteen per cent and ran to the end of 2027 without a renegotiation clause.

## The Hotel and its Market

Casa Palma opened in 2002 as a 140-room boutique hotel inside a converted eighteenth-century colonial mansion in Cartagena de Indias, and was expanded in 2016 by a sixty-room rear building with a rooftop pool. Two hundred rooms across thirty-day months gave Villanueva six thousand room nights a month to sell, and seventy-two thousand across the year. The property had been rated five-star boutique by the Colombian tourism authorities and had been a member of Small Luxury Hotels since 2020.

Vela Hospitality, a Miami mid-market private-equity firm holding six assets across the Caribbean and Central America, had acquired Casa Palma in the fourth quarter of 2024 for an enterprise value of USD 36M, and in November 2025 its investment committee had approved a three-year plan ahead of a 2028 exit. The plan's operating targets had been translated, for Casa Palma's revenue chair, into the composite index on the Miami sheet (see **Exhibit 6**).

Cartagena's demand was seasonally concentrated and the concentration was legible a year out: a soft January and February, a shoulder spring, a strong May carried by a twelve-hundred-delegate business conference, a peak July and August, a second conference in October, and a December split between holiday leisure and corporate shutdown. Casa Palma's competitive set — seven comparable boutique properties — published rate and occupancy monthly, and Villanueva's index against it was one of the five scored measures (see **Exhibit 2**).

One further fact about the market sat in the file and was dated. A hundred-and-fifty-room international-brand property, under construction on the far side of the old city, was due to open in September 2026. Its rooms would add roughly fifteen per cent to the capacity of Casa Palma's competitive set, in a shoulder month, three-quarters of the way through Villanueva's twelve.

## The Situation

The chair had been empty for five months and the hotel had run on last year's settings. At the end of December the property's rate sat at a hundred and twelve dollars, its guest score at 4.1 out of 5, and twenty-four per cent of its transient business came through the hotel's own channels, the remaining seventy-six through the agencies at eighteen per cent commission. The operating account held five hundred thousand dollars.

The previous director had left three arguments unresolved, and Villanueva inherited all three in the form of three colleagues who had each written her a memo in her first month.

**Camila Restrepo**, head of sales, six years at Casa Palma, argued for volume. The agencies filled the house, the composite index rewarded revenue, and a boutique property in a market that was about to absorb a hundred and fifty new rooms could not afford to be invisible in the places where people looked. Hold the allotment high, hold the rate near where it was, take every group that came, and spend the service line only as far as the score needed.

**Andrés Mejía**, general manager, who had come from a Miami property that cut its agency share to twenty-eight per cent in two years, argued the opposite. Every room sold through an agency paid eighteen per cent to someone else. Cut the allotment hard, fund the marketing and loyalty line properly to build the direct base that replaces it, put real money into the guest experience, push the rate up where the season allows, and decline the groups that arrive below what the rooms are worth.

**Fernando Escobar**, chief financial officer, argued for the margin and against both of them. The rate was where the market had put it and moving it was a bet. The marketing line was a cost with a slow and unproven return. Hold the rate, hold the allotment near the middle, spend as little as the score tolerates, and take groups only when the arithmetic on the individual block works.

The three plans are set out in **Exhibit 5**. Each was internally coherent, each was argued from the same trailing data, and none of the three advocates had tested his or her plan against the index Villarreal would read.

## What Villanueva Controlled

Five inputs a month, and nothing else.

The **rate** was a single blended figure for the whole property, adjustable in one-dollar steps between sixty and two hundred and fifty. It moved demand: cheaper filled more rooms, dearer filled fewer, and the relationship was steep enough at the extremes that both ends of the range stopped behaving the way the middle did.

The **agency allotment** was the percentage of transient inventory released to the online travel agencies, in five-point steps from zero to a hundred. Rooms sold through it paid eighteen per cent commission; rooms sold direct cost the hotel four dollars each to deliver. The allotment also bought visibility: a property listed widely was found more often, including by guests who then booked direct.

The **marketing and loyalty** line, zero to twenty thousand dollars a month in five-hundred-dollar steps, built the direct-booking base over time. Spend below two thousand a month and the base decayed rather than held.

The **service-quality** line, on the same range and steps, moved the guest score in the month the money was spent. Five thousand a month held the score where it was; more raised it, less let it fall. The score was one of the five measures, and it also moved demand and food-and-beverage spend per occupied room.

The **group decision** was accept or decline, once a month, on the block described in that month's enquiry. An accepted block was carried at its stated rate and brought its stated food-and-beverage revenue; it also consumed rooms that transient guests might otherwise have taken at Villanueva's rate. The enquiries for the whole year were known in advance (**Exhibit 4**) — this was the only decision on which she could plan the full twelve months from day one.

The console opened each January with the five inputs already populated at last year's settings: rate a hundred and twelve, allotment fifty-eight per cent, marketing three thousand, service five thousand, group accepted. A month committed without touching anything committed those.

## Closing

Villanueva squared the twelve printed pages, put the Miami sheet on top of them, and wrote three figures across the header of January: *112 · 58 · accept.* Under them, she wrote a fourth line — *what does this sheet actually pay me for?* — and then went up to the rooftop bar, where the year's last full house was still running, to look at the property she would spend twelve months pricing.
