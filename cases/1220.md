# The Container Line: Costaria, Boravia and the First Cargo

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1220 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1220 — *Comparative Advantage Live* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On the morning of Monday, April 6, 2026, Julia Arredondo-Vial opened the commission's shared folder in the harbour office at Puerto Costaria and found the term sheet for the first cargo exactly as she had left it on Friday: unsigned, with the price line blank. The new container line to Boravia-Norte would cut the crossing from fourteen days to three and make a twenty-foot container cheap enough to fill. For as long as anyone at the commission could remember, the Republic of Costaria and Boravia had each lived on what it produced at home.

Arredondo-Vial, executive secretary of the Costaria–Boravia Joint Economic Commission, had to put three papers before both cabinets in turn: a production plan for the last season before the line opened, a plan for the first season of trade, and a package for the workers that plan would move. The first paper was due at 18:00 on Friday, April 17. Both cabinets had agreed to adopt whatever the commission sent and to judge it on the full record once the third paper was in.

## The Protagonist

Arredondo-Vial was forty-six. She had grown up in Costaria's orchard country, trained as a statistician, and spent eleven years in the national accounts division of Boravia's statistics office. The two governments appointed her jointly in January 2026; each cabinet had accepted her because the other had. She had built input-output tables for both economies. She had never had to choose between them.

## The Decision

For each country the commission set one number: the share of its workers who would grow mangoes. Everyone else would produce software. Once the line opened, the commission would also write the first contract: how many million mangoes Costaria would ship north, and the price, in mangoes per software unit, at which Boravian software would come back. After that first trading season it would recommend one mechanism for the workers who had changed sectors (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Company and its Setting

Costaria was a small coastal republic of 2 million people, 1 million of them in productive work. Its northern orchards were known for mangoes, and its capital had a young software-services industry selling to clients abroad. Boravia, two flight-hours away, had 18 million people and 10 million workers, mature technology firms, and greenhouse farms run by trained agronomists. Per worker, Boravia produced more of both goods: 200 mangoes or 200 software units a year, against Costaria's 100 mangoes or 50 software units (see **Exhibit 1**).

Newspapers in both capitals had taken positions. *"They produce less than us in everything,"* the leading Boravian business daily wrote. *"What could they possibly sell us that we don't already make better at home?"* Costaria's main opposition paper argued from the other side: Boravia was bigger and richer in every industry, and open trade would wipe out Costarian farmers and coders alike.

## The Situation

The commission worked in physical units, not money. The two countries had no agreed exchange rate, and the first contract would be a straight exchange of fruit for software. Every figure on the commission's dashboard was a count: millions of mangoes, millions of software units, or units per citizen per year.

In the season just ended, Costaria had kept 60% of its workers in mangoes and 40% in software. It produced 60 million mangoes and 20 million software units, which came to 30 mangoes and 10 software units per Costarian. Boravia had split its workers evenly and produced 1,000 million of each good, about 56 mangoes and 56 software units per Boravian. Together the two countries consumed 1,060 million mangoes and 1,020 million software units. Moving workers between the two goods changed output at a fixed rate in each country. In Costaria, one more software unit meant two fewer mangoes. In Boravia the exchange was one for one.

If nothing changed, the coming seasons would repeat the last one. Each country would consume exactly what it produced, at the mix it had inherited, and the line would sail empty. The Trade Ministry's draft term sheet carried a placeholder of 60 million mangoes for Costaria's shipment, the figure first floated in the talks. Neither delegation had been willing to write the first price.

## What Arredondo-Vial Controlled

In the first paper she set each country's split of workers between mangoes and software, with no trade. Whatever each country grew and wrote, its own citizens consumed.

In the second paper she set the two splits again, this time knowing the line would sail, and wrote the contract. It had two lines: the volume of mangoes Costaria would ship, in millions, and the price in mangoes per software unit. The commission's terms of reference allowed any price from half a mango to three mangoes per software unit. Boravia's software shipment followed from those two lines, and neither country could ship more than it had produced. Marek Ostrovan, Boravia's Trade Minister, told her the price was the only line he would read. Tomás Echeverri-Luna, Costaria's Minister of Production, read the volume first: the orchards, he said, fed Costarians before they fed anyone else.

In the third paper she recommended one adjustment mechanism, and could revisit the splits and the contract. The ministries had priced five options. Cash transfers to displaced workers would cost about 8% of the gains from trade. Retraining programmes would cost about 12%, and investment in the affected regions about 15%. A tariff or quota on the partner's exports would, by the Trade Ministry's estimate, destroy about 9% of the gains and bring back about 30,000 jobs. Doing nothing cost nothing in the budget; the Interior Ministry warned that its political cost would rise every year. The commission counted as displaced every worker whose sector differed from the season before the line. In Costaria that meant software developers reabsorbed into the orchards at lower wages. In Boravia it meant growers in the south whose farms had closed.

The dashboard showed, for each country, mango output, software output, mangoes per citizen and software per citizen. It also showed combined world consumption of each good, Costaria's exports and imports under the contract and, in the third season, the displaced-worker count in each country. A round history kept each season's two splits, the four per-citizen figures and the contract price. The joint review would weigh three lines: combined consumption against the season before the line, the price at which the first contract cleared, and the mechanism chosen. It would reduce them to one overall score out of 100 and a performance band.

## Closing

At 17:20 on Friday, April 17, Arredondo-Vial had two cells left to fill in the first paper, one for each country. Ostrovan's office had called twice that afternoon. Echeverri-Luna had sent a one-line note asking what the orchards should plant for the season. The term sheet sat open in the next window, the volume line reading 60 and the price line still blank.
