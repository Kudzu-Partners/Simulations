# Zuriel Foods Plc.: Five Days to Answer

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1869 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1869 — *Brand Under Pressure* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Thursday, 14 May 2026, Folasade Adeyemi's phone would not stop lighting up. A consumer influencer with a large following had posted a video the night before alleging that a batch of Zuriel Foods' flagship seasoning cube carried an undeclared additive, holding a blurry lab printout up to the camera as proof. By the time Adeyemi reached her office in Lagos, the video had been watched 2.4 million times in eighteen hours, the hashtag #ZurielExposed was the top trending topic on Nigerian social media, and two of the country's largest retail chains had already pulled the product from their shelves pending clarification.

Adeyemi, Zuriel's Brand Director, had five days. The company's own laboratory could confirm or clear the allegation, but the result would not return before the following Tuesday, and public belief was hardening by the hour. Whatever the company said about itself before the lab spoke would have to survive whatever the lab then said.

## The Protagonist

Adeyemi, 44, had run Zuriel's brand function since 2021, after five years as Head of Consumer Marketing. She had joined from a multinational consumer-goods company in 2016, drawn by the chance to build a corporate brand from a genuinely Nigerian base rather than manage a regional outpost of someone else's. In 2019 she had led the refresh that put "Nourishing Nigerian families since 1991" on every pack and briefed every retailer on it personally; the language now scrolling under the influencer's video was, in large part, her own. She reported to the chief executive and held a standing contingency communications budget of ₦600 million, set aside for exactly this kind of event and never yet drawn on.

## The Decision

Over the coming days Adeyemi would have to work through a linked set of calls: what kind of problem this actually was, what the Zuriel brand now stood for, where the ₦600 million would go and in what tone, and how the company would carry itself once the lab result was known — to the board, to a press corps that had already started asking questions, and to five different audiences who were, for now, hearing five different things.

## The Company and its Setting

Zuriel Foods Plc. was a publicly listed Nigerian consumer-goods company, founded in 1991 and headquartered in Lagos, with manufacturing in Ogun State and distribution nationwide. It employed 2,300 people and reported ₦84 billion in annual revenue. Its flagship seasoning cube was the second-largest brand in its category, with 24% market share, sold on the strength of a single promise held consistently for thirty-five years. The corporate reputation behind that promise had, until this week, been a genuine commercial asset: shelf priority with retailers, pricing power against private-label competitors, and a public trust score that Zuriel's own tracking placed at 72 out of 100.

## The Situation

Within seventy-two hours of the video going up, that trust score had fallen to 51 (see **Exhibit 1**). A separate internal measure of how consistently Zuriel's story was landing across its different audiences — consumers, retailers, regulators, staff, investors — stood at just 30 out of 100. Week-one sales of the flagship seasoning were down 22% against the prior month, and 89% of everything being said about Zuriel online was now negative, with a rival brand being recommended in the replies as the safer choice. The two retail chains that had delisted the product represented 31% of Zuriel's modern-trade volume between them. Nigeria's food regulator, NAFDAC, had requested the batch's manufacturing documentation. And beneath the allegation itself sat a fact Adeyemi already knew: the additive named in the video was permitted under Nigerian food regulation, present in the recipe, and had never been clearly disclosed on the pack. Whether it was present at a level the video's blurry printout suggested — above safe limits — was exactly what the five-day test would settle. Nothing in the week so far suggested the two delisted chains were in a hurry to reconsider on their own, and every day without a company position let the number of people who had only seen the accusation, and not any response to it, keep growing.

## What Adeyemi Controlled

The first call was diagnostic. The threat could be read as a legal problem, since the underlying claim could be attacked in the courts as false and defamatory; as a shelf problem, since the fastest fix was winning the two delisted chains back; as a deeper misalignment, in which what the company said, what it did, and what the public now believed had drifted apart; or as a wave of viral noise, certain to recede once the lab result cleared the company. Tied to that was a judgment on where the gap sat: mainly in what people had come to believe rather than what was in the product; between promise and product, since the pack said "all-natural" while the recipe carried an undisclosed additive; or nowhere at all, since the allegation itself was simply false and Zuriel's claims stood as written. Adeyemi also had to put a number on it — what share of the problem, as a percentage, was a genuine product issue rather than a matter of perception.

The second call was about identity. She could keep the existing claim as written and contest the allegation on the facts; retire the "all-natural" promise for a claim built on safety and transparency, disclosing the additive in full; set the natural-ingredient claim aside and relaunch around a different idea — a modern, science-led food company; or keep "all-natural" as the promise and reformulate the recipe to remove the additive. Attached to that was a decision on the product itself: keep the additive in the recipe and disclose its presence plainly in new on-pack labeling; change nothing about the product itself and treat the whole episode as a matter of communication only; or remove the additive from the recipe entirely and resupply retailers once the new batch was ready. She also had to decide how tightly any new position held to the thirty-five-year promise, from a clean break to strict continuity.

The third call was where the ₦600 million went, allocated in full across six lines (see **Exhibit 2**): an independent lab certifying the batch; a public microsite showing batch-level traceability; a filmed statement from the chief executive; direct outreach to the two retailers and the original influencer; a paid advertising campaign at national scale; and a briefing for the frontline and retail staff who spoke to customers daily. Layered on the spending was a choice of tone — acknowledge the concern, commit to sharing whatever the lab found, and say plainly what was and was not yet known; call the allegation malicious and false, demand a retraction, and put the lawyers on record; or tell consumers there was nothing to worry about and keep every message calm and confident — and a judgment on how tightly the same story would hold across consumers, retailers, the regulator, employees and investors.

The fourth call would come once the lab spoke. Adeyemi would choose a medium-term direction: re-commit to the founding promise of nourishing Nigerian families; declare the matter closed the moment the result was known; leave the founding promise behind and relaunch around a premium, science-forward identity; or make certification, disclosure and traceability the new standard the company followed going forward. Whatever the result said, she also had to decide how much further effort went into rebuilding trust rather than simply announcing the outcome — and, in front of the board and a skeptical journalist, whether to restate the company's mission, describe the outcome as full vindication, and move past further questions; to walk them through the certified result, the relisted shelves and what was still being fixed; or to let the certified result speak for itself and decline further questions.

## Closing

Adeyemi set her phone face-down on the desk, opened a blank page, and looked at the clock. Five days. Six budget lines and not yet a Naira committed. A thirty-five-year-old promise that two million people had just heard questioned for the first time. She picked up her pen.
