# Smoky Mountain Foods: The Empty Columns

In her first week at Smoky Mountain Foods, Inc., in June 2026, Renata Vaughn read two documents that had arrived four days apart and had not been written to be read together. The first was a service-watch letter from the grocery chain that took about 22% of the company's revenue, roughly US$275 million a year. Deliveries to that customer were arriving on time and complete 89% of the time, against a contractual requirement of 96%. A second strike inside two quarters would delist the slower-moving items and send the remainder of the range to a line review. The second document was Vaughn's own charter. The board had created the job she now held, the first Vice President of Supply Chain in the company's history, and asked her for an integrated supply-chain strategy funded from US$40 million over three years, released one board gate at a time.

The board wanted the strategy and a Phase-1 plan in six weeks. The customer's second service review was about two quarters out. Between those two dates Vaughn had to settle what she was actually looking at, which few things Smoky Mountain Foods would do about it, how to divide the US$40 million and in what order, and what she would promise the board she could prove before asking for the next tranche.

## The Protagonist

Vaughn, 46, had spent eleven years at a larger packaged-food manufacturer, the last four of them running demand and supply planning across a network three times the size of this one. She had been hired in April after a search the board ran itself. She had never worked at a company where nobody had held her job before: there was no predecessor's plan to inherit, no standing forum to join, and no agreed set of numbers that the plants, logistics, sales and finance all recognised. Her incentive letter tied part of her bonus to the service level at the at-risk account and part to cash released from the balance sheet.

## The Decision

Vaughn had to name the problem, choose the priorities, allocate and sequence US$40 million, and commit to a test the board could hold her to.

## The Company and its Setting

Smoky Mountain Foods, founded in 1986 and headquartered in Chattanooga, Tennessee, made branded shelf-stable and refrigerated food — sauces, dressings, snacks and ready meals — for national grocery, club and food-service customers. Revenue was US$1.25 billion at an 11% operating margin. It ran four plants and six distribution centres and employed about 3,800 people.

The company had grown by acquisition, and the supply chain still showed the seams. Three separate ERP systems held the transaction records, and none agreed with the others without manual reconciliation. There was no company-wide cycle in which sales, the plants, logistics and finance settled a single plan for demand, supply, inventory and cash, so each function planned against its own numbers. The four plants were measured on their own unit costs and behaved accordingly, running long batches that suited the plant and complicated the network. Planning itself was five people working in spreadsheets.

The arithmetic of that patchwork showed up in three places. Total supply-chain cost ran at about 26% of revenue against a peer benchmark of roughly 21–22%. The cash-to-cash cycle — the days between paying for materials and collecting from customers — stood at 94 days, and inventory sat about US$60 million above what a well-run network of this size would carry. And service to the largest customer had drifted to 89%.

## The Situation

Nothing in the operating numbers was moving on its own. If the pattern of the previous four quarters continued, on-time-in-full at the at-risk account would stay near 89% into the second service review, the delisting clause would be available to the customer, and the roughly US$275 million of revenue that account represented would be renegotiated from a weak position. The 94-day cash cycle would keep about US$60 million of cash inside inventory, and supply-chain cost would stay four to five points of revenue above the benchmark (see **Exhibit 1**).

Inside the company, four readings of the situation were in circulation, each with a senior sponsor. One held that the company had no integrated supply-chain strategy at all, only functional plans that did not add up, and that building one was the work. One held that it was an operations problem: tighten plant costs, tune the distribution centres, and the numbers would recover. One held that it was a systems problem, that three ERP instances were the root cause, and that unifying the technology would settle everything downstream. One held that it was a people problem, that the planning group was too small and too junior, and that better planners would make the spreadsheets unnecessary.

Vaughn also had to say which single threat the company would answer first. Service to the largest customer was one candidate, at 89% on about US$275 million of revenue and one strike from delisting. The supply-chain cost gap was another, at about 26% of revenue against a 21–22% benchmark. The 94-day cash cycle and the trapped inventory was a third, and the one the CFO named first. The fragmentation itself — three ERP systems and no joint planning cycle — was the fourth.

## What Vaughn Controlled

Her first deliverable was the diagnosis: which reading of the problem she put her name to, which threat she named as the one that defined value, and how tightly she wrote the diagnosis around the way Smoky Mountain Foods competed for that particular customer, anywhere from a general operational review to an argument built entirely on that account's requirement.

Her second was the priority list. She could lead with restoring service through a joint planning cycle and network fixes; with plant-level cost reduction across the four sites; with the single-ERP unification ahead of everything else; or with service, cost, cash and systems pursued together. She had to decide how many priorities to carry — two or three, four, or six and above — and whether every priority would carry a written line connecting it to how the company competed, whether only the largest would, or whether the list would stand on its own merits. She also had to decide how hard she would hold that list against the pressure to fund every function.

Her third was the money. Six investment lines were on the slate (see **Exhibit 2**), and the six amounts had to sum to exactly US$40 million. She then had to name the deliberate first move: standing up the joint planning cycle together with a quick network fix, launching the ERP unification, or beginning plant automation.

Her fourth was the board brief. She had to define the test the board would use to decide whether Phase 1 had worked: a quantified one, taking on-time-in-full to 94% or better and releasing US$25 million or more of cash within two quarters, with Phase 2 unfunded if she missed; a directional one, promising meaningful service improvement and material cash release by year-end; or no hard test, asking the board to back the roadmap and review progress as it went. She had to say how Phase 2 would be paid for — out of the working capital Phase 1 released, out of the next budget tranche requested up front, or a mixture without showing the bridge. And she had to answer, in advance, what would happen if Phase 1 underdelivered: a pre-agreed fallback and a hold on Phase 2 spending, a statement that it would not underdeliver, or a request for more time and more budget.

## Closing

On the Friday of that first week Vaughn wrote the six investment lines down one side of a single sheet, drew a column beside them for dollars and a column for the phase, and left both columns empty. The board wanted the strategy in six weeks. The customer's review was two quarters out, and the letter did not say what an acceptable trajectory looked like — only what the number had to be.
