# Costaria Mutual: The Individual Line

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1305 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1305 — *Insurance Markets Lab — Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 07:40 on Wednesday, April 15, 2026, Renata Obando-Leclerc, director of individual and family business at Costaria Mutual, opened a two-page memo from the Office of the Chief Actuary. Its first line read: *our individual-market enrollment is collapsing.* Membership of the line had fallen 18% in a year while average claims per member had risen 22%. The 14% premium increase filed for 2025 had not closed the gap.

Obando-Leclerc chaired the insurer's Pricing Committee, which met on Friday, April 17. The board wanted the committee's recommendation for the line by the end of that day, and the Superintendency of Insurance had asked to see the brief that went with it. By Friday evening she had to settle three things, in order: a posted premium, the share of each medical bill that members would pay themselves, and a package of rule changes to put to the regulator.

## The Protagonist

Obando-Leclerc was forty-four. She had spent six years running Costaria Mutual's employer-sponsored plans, a book that made money every year because employees joined with their jobs, not because they expected to be ill. She took over individual and family business in January 2025, three weeks after the 14% increase had been filed, and inherited the chair of the Pricing Committee with it. She knew how to sell a health plan. She had never had to defend one to a regulator.

## The Decision

The memo set out three courses: raise the individual premium a further 18% to restore the loss ratio; hold the premium and absorb the losses; or propose a structural redesign of the line to the Superintendency. The Chief Actuary recommended the third. Each course had a sponsor on the committee, and each sponsor had numbers.

To test them, the actuarial office had built a pricing lab: a model of the line that the committee could run forward one contract year at a time for nine years, in three phases of three years each (see **Exhibit 2**). The Superintendency would judge the package the committee left standing at the close of the third phase. The first two phases were the committee's evidence.

## The Company and its Setting

Costaria Mutual, S.A., founded in 1962 as a workers' mutual society, wrote about €680 million of health premium a year and employed 1,200 people. It covered roughly 420,000 lives: 280,000 in employer-sponsored plans, 95,000 in individual and family plans, and 45,000 in complementary plans that topped up the public system (see **Exhibit 1**). In voluntary health insurance it held about 22% of the market, second to a multinational rival with about 31%.

Costaria's tax-funded public health service provided basic care to everyone. Private plans bought faster access, wider provider networks and elective procedures. The individual line was voluntary and renewed every year. Under the Superintendency's rules it charged every applicant in the capital region the same posted premium. Each applicant knew far more about their own likely medical bills than the underwriter did, and the underwriter was not allowed to price on it.

## The Situation

The memo's lapse analysis was the page the committee read twice. Members who had left the line during the year had averaged €1,420 of claims in their final year; the members who remained now averaged €4,180. The line's loss ratio, claims paid as a share of premiums collected, had run above 100% since January against the committee's target of 86%.

The lab reproduced the line in miniature. It held 1,000 prospective customers in the capital region, each with their own expected annual health-care cost. The actuarial office had calibrated them to the line's claims history: a median of about €1,800 a year, an average of about €3,200, and a long upper tail in which the costliest few expected more than €40,000. Each customer knew their own figure; Costaria Mutual saw only the spread. The posted premium stood at €3,200, the average. All figures were in euros, the currency in which the line reported.

If the committee changed nothing, the line would keep selling one policy at €3,200 to whoever chose to buy it. The lapse figures showed who had been choosing. Customers whose own costs sat well below the premium had been leaving, and those who stayed were costing more than they paid. The losses would continue at their present rate, and the Superintendency's file would hold no proposal.

## What Obando-Leclerc Controlled

In the first phase the committee set one thing each year: the **posted annual premium**, a single price for every applicant. Gloria Mairena-Stout, the chief financial officer, wanted the further 18%, and the same again each year until premiums caught up with claims. "The 14% was priced for last year's medical trend," she said. "We are a year behind, not wrong." Esteban Lizano-Marsh, head of member services and public affairs, wanted the premium held at €3,200 and the losses absorbed for now. Tomás Ibarra-Fennell, the Chief Actuary, had written that a further increase would speed the losses up rather than stop them.

In the second phase the premium stayed where the first phase had left it, and the committee set the **coinsurance rate**: the share of each medical bill that members paid at the point of care, anywhere from none of it to all of it. Mairena-Stout favored half. Lizano-Marsh favored none. "Our members bought cover," he said, "not a bill." Ibarra-Fennell proposed a fifth.

In the third phase the premium followed the rules of the package rather than the committee's choice, and the committee built the package for the Superintendency from four parts. **Mandatory enrollment**, with subsidies for low-income households, was Ibarra-Fennell's structural redesign, with members paying a fifth of each bill. Lizano-Marsh would back a mandate only with no member share at the point of care, so that low-income members were not priced out of care they needed. Mairena-Stout called the mandate a political project that would take years. **Risk-rated premiums**, priced by age, district and smoking status, were her answer, with members paying half of each bill; Lizano-Marsh said the regulator would not approve them. The **cost-sharing rate** reopened the second phase's argument. **Managed care** meant provider networks, prior authorization and capitation. Paula Sanabria-Holt, director of the provider network, expected it to cut the use of care, and expected complaints and overhead to rise with it.

For every contract year the lab reported the same figures (see **Exhibit 2**): customers enrolled out of the 1,000, and the coverage rate that made; the posted premium; the loss ratio, against the 86% target; the **conditional mean cost**, the average expected cost of the customers who actually bought; per-capita spending, the average medical bill per enrollee including the part members paid themselves; and the net result for the year, premiums less claims and administration, with its running total. At the close, the Superintendency's review would add an indicator of population health and access, scored from 0 to 100, and rate the package out of 100.

## Closing

At 08:30 on Friday, Obando-Leclerc opened the lab on the boardroom screen. The premium field read €3,200. Mairena-Stout's 18% slide was already in the meeting pack. Lizano-Marsh had brought the quarter's complaint log, and Ibarra-Fennell his memo. The Superintendency expected the brief by 18:00.
