# Riley Carter: Forty-Three Years on One Form

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-884 · **Companion simulation:** Express 884 — *The Retirement Game* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, April 20, 2026, Marvin Keller, the human-resources representative at Lindell Regional Logistics in Columbus, Ohio, dropped a fourteen-page benefits packet on Riley Carter's desk. "We'd love to get you enrolled in the 401(k) by Friday," he said. "Your first paycheck hits the same day." Riley was twenty-two, three weeks out of school, and earning US$40,000 a year.

The enrollment form asked three things: how much of each paycheck to put into the company's retirement plan, how to split it between the plan's traditional and Roth accounts, and which fund to invest it in. Riley had until Friday. The choices made that week, and revisited at the start of each decade, would decide what sat in the account at sixty-five.

## The Protagonist

Riley Carter had finished a two-year associate's degree in business and taken a job as an operations associate at Lindell, a regional logistics company. Riley was single, rented a one-bedroom apartment for US$1,150 a month and took home about US$2,560 a month after tax and before any retirement contribution. Riley had no student debt and no savings to speak of.

Riley's mother was sixty-four and still working. She had not started saving for retirement until she was thirty-eight. Riley had heard her talk about it, and had also just paid a security deposit and first month's rent.

## The Decision

Riley had to fill in the form by Friday: a contribution rate, a split between traditional and Roth, and a fund. Those three settings could be changed at the start of every decade that followed, at ages thirty-two, forty-two, fifty-two and sixty-two. Each decade would also bring its own question, and the answer to it would stay in the account for good.

## The Company and its Setting

Lindell offered a 401(k), the most common workplace retirement plan in the United States. Money put into the traditional side came out of pay before income tax and was taxed when withdrawn in retirement. Money put into the Roth side was taxed now and came out tax-free later. At US$40,000, Riley sat in the 12% federal income-tax bracket.

The company matched 50% of whatever an employee contributed, up to 6% of salary. The match vested on a three-year cliff: an employee who left before three years of service forfeited every matching dollar the company had put in (see Exhibit 1).

The plan offered three funds. An S&P 500 index fund charged 0.04% a year in expenses. A target-date fund built for people retiring around 2065 charged 0.12%. An actively managed growth fund, whose brochure described a team that aimed to beat the market, charged 1.10%.

## The Situation

The form came pre-filled with the plan's defaults: 6% of pay, with half of it going to the Roth side. The fund line was blank. At 6%, Riley's contribution would be US$2,400 in the first year and the company would add US$1,200; Riley's monthly take-home would fall by roughly US$200, less on the traditional side because of the tax saving. Riley's accounts stood at zero.

A colleague two desks over, Tyler Brandt, had set his contribution to zero until his car was paid off. Riley's mother had told Riley to "put in as much as they'll let you." The rent was due on the first.

If Riley left the form in a drawer, nothing would go in: no contribution, no match, and a balance that stayed at zero while the salary grew. If Riley signed it as it came and never touched it again, 6% of a rising salary would go in every year, the company would add half as much again, and the money would compound in whatever fund Riley picked.

A handout at the back of the packet, prepared by the plan's administrator, listed five forks at which most workers' retirement savings had been made or lost (see Exhibit 2). The first was a better-paid job offer arriving before the match vested. The second was what to do with raises. The third was a layoff, and what to do with the old 401(k). The fourth was a market crash in the decade before retirement. The fifth was when to start claiming Social Security.

## What Riley Controlled

At the start of each decade Riley set three things. The contribution rate, anywhere from 0% to 25% of salary; the form's default was 6%. The Roth split, from none of the contribution to all of it, in steps of ten percentage points; the default was half. And the fund: the index fund, the target-date fund or the active growth fund.

Each decade also carried one question of its own. In the first, when a US$46,000 job came up in year two, Riley could stay and let the match vest, or switch and forfeit the unvested match. In the second, as raises arrived, Riley could raise contributions with them or let them go to rent and a car. In the third, after a layoff, Riley could roll the 401(k) into an individual retirement account (IRA), cash it out and pay income tax plus a 10% penalty, or leave it in the old employer's plan. In the fourth, if the market crashed around fifty-five, Riley could stay the course, sell to cash and wait for the bottom, or rebalance and buy more equities. In the fifth, Riley could claim Social Security at sixty-two, reduced by about 30% for life; at sixty-seven, the full retirement age; or at seventy, about 24% higher for life.

After each decade the plan's portal showed Riley's age, salary, traditional 401(k) balance, Roth balance and the total employer match captured so far. A decade-by-decade log recorded the salary, the contribution rate, the dollars contributed, the match received and the combined balance. The portal also drew Riley's balance against three reference savers: one who started at thirty-two, one who skipped the match for the first ten years, and one who held the active fund. At sixty-five the question would be simple: how much was in the accounts, and what the monthly Social Security check would be.

## Closing

On Thursday night Riley sat at the kitchen table with the form, the rent receipt and a pen. The contribution box said 6%. The fund line was still blank, and Friday's payroll cut-off was at noon.
