# Instituto Cumbre de Liderazgo: The Mission–Margin Test

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1957 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1957 — *The Humanist Dean* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Friday, April 10, 2026, Ximena Alcántara Bravo read the two-page brief the dean had left on her desk the evening before and wrote a single date at the top of it: May 15. Alfonso Berlanga, dean of Instituto Cumbre de Liderazgo (ICL), had given her the school's next flagship open-enrollment program — *Líderes con Propósito*, first intake September 2026 — and the university board had attached two numbers to it. The program had to cover a fixed design-and-delivery budget of MXN 18,000,000, and it had to contribute MXN 6,000,000 of margin from the first cohort. It also had to be recognisably the school's own: ICL had been founded in 2009 on the commitment to develop leaders as whole persons, grounded in dignity and the common good, and the board's letter used that phrase twice.

The brochure, the tuition and the faculty contracts had to be locked by May 15 for a September room to fill. Five weeks. If the lock slipped, the launch moved to 2027 and the MXN 6,000,000 of first-cohort margin went with it. On the Tuesday of that week, three of ICL's senior professors had told Berlanga they would not teach a leadership bootcamp sold on return on investment.

## The Protagonist

Alcántara, 44, had directed executive education at ICL since 2022. She had joined the school in 2014 from a corporate learning-and-development post at a Monterrey industrial group, and had spent her first six years running custom corporate programs, the part of the portfolio that carried the margin. The open-enrollment portfolio came to her with the directorship, along with responsibility for its contribution line. In 2024 she had argued to hold a mid-market program at its list price; the school repositioned and discounted it instead, and it closed after two cohorts. She had never established which of the two had ended it. Her own contract came up for renewal in March 2027, after the first *Líderes con Propósito* cohort had run.

## The Decision

Alcántara had to decide what the program was, what it cost to buy, and how many people would be in the room — and then defend all three to the board and to the faculty on the same afternoon.

Three framings were on the table, each with an advocate inside the school. The first addressed managers who wanted to lead differently: a self-selecting audience that ICL's market research put at a smaller size and a higher price point than the school's other open programs. The second addressed managers buying advancement, a larger pool that placed *Líderes con Propósito* in the same comparison set as an online leadership certificate then selling at MX$70,000. The third led with purpose and carried a career outcome alongside it, at a price above both the online certificate and the school's other open programs.

Tuition was hers to set anywhere between MX$50,000 and MX$140,000 per participant; the planning file carried MX$95,000 as the target. The cohort target was hers to set anywhere up to the 320 that rooms and faculty allowed.

## The Company and its Setting

ICL was the executive-education unit of a private university in Monterrey, Mexico. It ran open-enrollment programs, custom corporate programs and an executive coaching practice, on annual revenue of MXN 220 million at a 28% contribution margin. It employed 34 full-time staff and drew on 80 adjunct faculty and 22 certified executive coaches, serving roughly 2,400 participants a year across northern Mexico (see **Exhibit 1**).

What ICL sold against larger rivals and cheaper online providers was the founding commitment. Corporate clients in Monterrey named it when they explained why they had chosen ICL, and the senior professors who had written on it were the reason the claim carried outside the campus.

## The Situation

The arithmetic on Alcántara's desk was short. At the MX$95,000 target, the MXN 18,000,000 envelope was covered at roughly 190 participants and the board's MXN 6,000,000 came at roughly 253. Rooms and faculty capped a single cohort at 320. The envelope itself had to be split across five lines — modules and content faculty, executive coaching, experiential and retreat components, marketing and enrollment, and participant services — and the planning office had circulated a draft split that totalled MXN 18.0 million (see **Exhibit 2**).

Inside that split sat the costliest choice in the design. A certified executive coach billed MX$2,200 an hour; an adjunct content faculty member billed MX$1,400. The planning file recorded that participants who described being changed by a program named their one-to-one coaching hours; it was also the line that moved the total fastest.

Berlanga would judge the launch on four things, and had said so. The first was the first-cohort margin: what the program contributed once the MXN 18,000,000 was covered, against the MXN 6,000,000 the board had set. The second was a values-alignment score the school's academic council maintained on a nought-to-hundred scale, a structured read on whether a program treated participants as whole persons in practice rather than in its copy; the council's provisional reading of the April design was 40. The third was a projected participant-experience score on the same scale, ICL's forecast of how transformational the cohort would rate the program, provisionally 30. The fourth was the number of people who actually enrolled.

Market research gave her the shape of both audiences but not the price at which either would stop buying. She had the professors' position in writing and no meeting with them yet.

## What Alcántara Controlled

By May 15, Alcántara had to commit six things in the lock document. Four were the framing, the tuition, the cohort target, and the five budget lines summing to MXN 18.0 million. The fifth was the share of the teaching design given to one-to-one coaching: the planning file offered a coaching-led design at about 40% of the teaching hours, a mixed design at about 25%, and a content-led design at about 10%. The sixth was the weight, stated on a nought-to-hundred scale, that the senior faculty's judgment carried in the design.

She then had to present it twice on the same afternoon. To the board she could put the costed statement of enrollment and margin, or an assurance that the numbers would come, with the model held back, or the argument that this cohort should be judged on the mission rather than on the MXN 6,000,000. To the faculty she could open the coaching ratio and the experiential design and build the values components with them; or answer them in the school's own language and leave the teaching design as drafted; or set the design on the board's authority over the budget and bring them the result. And the lock document required her to state, in one line, how closely what the program did matched what the brochure said.

## Closing

Alcántara put the dean's brief on the left of her desk and the planning file on the right, and wrote the five budget lines on a sheet between them. The board met on May 15. She had five weeks, one envelope of MXN 18,000,000, three professors who had not yet agreed to teach, and a price to name.
