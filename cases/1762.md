# Petrolia: Redesigning the Global Mobility Program

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1762 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1762 — *Petrolia Global Mobility Redesign* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On a Tuesday morning in late April, Daniela Rojas-Beltrán opened her laptop on the seventh floor of Petrolia's Edificio Central in San José and found three policy folders waiting for her, sent overnight by the chief human resources officer. The cover note ran two paragraphs and left nothing open to interpretation: the expatriate program was straining at every joint, cost per assignee had climbed well past what the company had planned for, and the workers' council had put its concerns about the program in writing. A package architecture, a plan for moving the existing assignee population onto whatever the redesign produced, and a set of talking points for the managers who would field the questions were due on her desk in three weeks. The three folders held three different packages. The memo did not say which one to pick, or whether to mix them.

Daniela was Head of Global Mobility, three years into the seat, and the operational owner of a portfolio of international assignments spread across four countries. The CHRO had handed her the design problem, not the answer.

## The Protagonist

Daniela had spent her career inside global mobility functions, arriving at Petrolia after several years running relocation logistics for a smaller regional employer. She led a team of three — a senior mobility manager, an analyst, and a coordinator who handled immigration filings — plus a panel of external relocation vendors. She reported through the talent organization to the CHRO, who had the executive committee's backing for the redesign but had left its specifics, including the budget envelope, to Daniela's judgment.

## The Decision

Over the three weeks, working stage by stage, Daniela faced two linked calls at every point. The first was where a given role or group of roles belonged among the three packages. The second was a governance move — a choice about how to handle a complication attached to that stage, whether a monitoring procedure, a timing question, or the wording of a message to the field. The two calls compounded: a placement made without its governance move well handled left exposure on the table even when the placement itself was sound.

## The Company and its Setting

Petrolia was an integrated energy company headquartered in San José, Costa Rica, with production operations in Brazil, a midstream joint venture in Mexico, and downstream retail together with a small green-hydrogen pilot in Spain. Global mobility administered the company's international assignments across those four countries, with an assignee compensation and benefits envelope of roughly USD 14.8 million against an annual operating budget of roughly USD 2.1 million. The function sat inside HR, and its work reached five country- or unit-level line managers who hosted or sent assignees day to day — in Spain's retail network, Costa Rica's refining operations, Brazil's production unit, Mexico's midstream business, and the new hydrogen pilot in Andalucía.

## The Situation

The redesign covered 30 roles split across four groups of work (see Exhibit 1). Traditional carried roughly 3.1 times a role's base salary and the company carried the full tax exposure; 360° Rotation carried roughly 1.9 times with the company's tax exposure capped rather than fully absorbed; and Digital-Nomad Hybrid carried roughly 1.25 times with the residency exposure sitting with the assignee rather than the company.

Separately, 26 existing assignees were still on the legacy Traditional package, mid-contract, grouped into four tranches with real dates attached rather than a clean slate (see Exhibit 2). If nothing changed, the cost trend behind the CHRO's memo would keep widening, the workers' council's written concern would sit unanswered, and the twenty-six existing assignees would remain on terms nobody had revisited since they were signed.

## What Daniela Controlled

For each of the four role groups, Daniela decided how many positions to place in each of the three packages, paired with a governance move chosen from a short menu specific to that group's complication. For the Brazil roles and, separately, the Andalucía roles, the menu asked how far to formalize the residency exposure attached to any Hybrid placement there: fold the question into the mobility team's existing quarterly review without a dedicated procedure; document a monitoring procedure with a named owner and a standing review cycle; or leave the procedure for later, until more pilot experience existed. For the Spain retail and corporate-rotation group, the linked move was how directly the redesign would respond, in that role family, to the workers' council's concerns. A comparable equity question sat inside the refining-rotation group's shortlist practice, and a comparable readiness question sat inside how early the Andalucía line manager was briefed on his group's procedure.

With placements set, Daniela turned to the twenty-six legacy assignees. For the nine-person tranche whose leases and school years lined up, she chose a target date: January 2027, a simpler calendar-year date; March 2027, a later date; or September 2026, tied to the point where the leases and school year both ended. For the seven-person mid-contract tranche, she chose a mechanic: a renegotiation-only approach applied case by case with no standing buyout on offer; a buyout for those moving to a different package paired with renegotiated terms for those staying Traditional; or a single date, applied uniformly for administrative simplicity, on which every case flipped. For the Brazil group, she set how long to hold them on legacy terms before transitioning — waiting the two years needed to clear the residency rule, which kept the tranche on its current terms longer, or transitioning sooner, which carried the residency-linked tax exposure instead. For the family-and-school group, she decided whether to move them at the next ordinary contract break, or carry them through to the end of their children's international-school program in 2028.

The final stretch was the two-page pack the five line managers would use with their own teams. For the first three FAQs, Daniela chose the answering style: a generic answer deferring the specifics to HR; cost named plainly as the primary explicit reason; or role-specific reasoning tied to the placement logic itself. For the harder question of whether the exercise was a cost cut in different clothes, she chose the framing: modernization language that left cost out of the conversation; the cost change named plainly, with where the savings would go; or disputing that cost had anything to do with the redesign at all. Last, she set what a line manager should do with a question the pack did not cover: log it, promise an answer within 48 hours, and route it to a named HR contact; forward everything past the five FAQs straight to HR, with HR handling the follow-up directly; or leave it to each manager's own judgment, on their own timeline and through whatever channel they chose.

Throughout, Daniela was watching a small set of running numbers: how many of the 30 roles were placed and in which package; a defensibility reading that moved against her the more the portfolio concentrated in one package and the more unresolved residency flags accumulated; separate progress readings, out of 100, for the cohort-transition plan and the line-manager pack as each took shape; and the average all-in cost, in multiples of base salary, across everything placed so far.

## Closing

By the second week of May, Daniela had a partial matrix, an empty tranche table, and a blank page where the line-manager pack was supposed to go. The folders were still open on her desk, three weeks was closer to two, and the CHRO was going to want to know, in September, why the portfolio looked the way it did.
