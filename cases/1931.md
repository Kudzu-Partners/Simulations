# Clínicas Raíz: The Viability Gate

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1931 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1931 — *Base-of-Pyramid Health* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

The letter from Clínicas Raíz's anchor impact investor reached Marisol Colop on Monday, March 2, 2026. She read it twice, and then read its third paragraph a third time. The fund had frozen the second tranche of GTQ 4.0 million. It would release the money only against a plan that did three things at once: reach more low-income patients than Raíz reached in 2025, put the company on a path to operational break-even within eighteen months, and earn an external ESG and social rating high enough to open the fund's blended co-financing facility — up to GTQ 6 million of concessional capital on top of the tranche. The letter set a deadline of ninety days. Raíz's finance manager had costed the runway without the tranche at five months.

In those ninety days Colop had to settle how Raíz read its own patients, what the low-income offer contained and how it reached people, what quality signal the company bought, where it set the consultation fee, and what she put in front of the fund's investment committee at the end.

## The Protagonist

Marisol Colop, 43, had been chief executive of Clínicas Raíz S.A. since the venture's second year. She was a family physician who had spent six years in public primary care in the western highlands before joining the group of doctors who founded Raíz in 2021, and she had run the Quetzaltenango clinic herself for eighteen months before taking the chief executive's seat. She had finished an MBA in 2023 at the fund's urging. She was the person the founding physicians trusted to refuse a clinical request on cost grounds, and the person the fund called when a covenant slipped.

## The Decision

Colop had to produce, inside ninety days, a plan the fund's investment committee would approve. The plan was not one decision but four, taken in sequence, each constraining the next: a reading of who Raíz actually served against who it said it served; the specification of the low-income offer; the quality signal and the consultation fee; and the presentation to the committee, in which she chose how much of the arithmetic to show.

## The Company and its Setting

Clínicas Raíz S.A. was a for-profit primary-care company headquartered in Quetzaltenango, Guatemala, founded in 2021 by four physicians and a small venture team. It ran six storefront clinics and eighteen community health promoters across the rural and peri-urban western highlands, offering general consultations, maternal-and-child care, chronic-disease follow-up for diabetes and hypertension, basic laboratory work and a small generics pharmacy.

In 2025 Raíz recorded 48,000 patient visits and revenue of GTQ 14.4 million, at an EBITDA margin of 6% — GTQ 0.86 million of surplus on the year (see **Exhibit 1**). Two facts shaped every decision. First, 72% of revenue came from cash-paying patients with no insurance, so the consultation fee, GTQ 300, was money out of a household's pocket on the day of the visit. Second, roughly half the population Raíz aimed to serve lived hours from a storefront, which was why the promoter network existed at all.

The market had moved while Raíz was arguing internally. A competing low-cost chain had begun advertising clinical accreditation. Raíz held no third-party certification of any kind.

## The Situation

The fund's quarterly portfolio review had triggered the letter. Its monitoring scorecard put Raíz's access index — a composite measure of reach into the poorest income quintiles, scored from 0 to 100 — at 52, down from 61. A patient survey commissioned the previous autumn found that 28% of households in the poorest quintile had skipped a needed visit in the preceding six months because of cost. The fund's external social rating for Raíz stood at 30 out of 100, and that rating was the key to the blended facility. The scorecard's fourth line, which the fund called gate risk and which combined runway, institutional trust and the exposure that came with closing clinics, stood at 55 out of 100, where lower was better.

Inside the company the argument had hardened before the letter arrived. The finance manager wanted the consultation fee raised; his model showed that GTQ 5 on the fee added roughly GTQ 0.9 million to the margin line and removed an estimated 6,000 visits by patients in the poorest quintile. The medical director wanted the fee cut by GTQ 10 and a membership introduced, which brought in visits Raíz had not staffed for. Two non-governmental organisations that referred patients to Raíz had asked, in the same fortnight, what verification of clinical quality Raíz could show them.

If the ninety days ended without a plan the committee accepted, the tranche went to another venture in the fund's portfolio, the runway ran to its five months, and Raíz would be choosing which clinics to close.

## What Colop Controlled

The first decision was the reading. Colop could decompose the access index by income quintile and work from what the poorest two quintiles showed; work from the average patient across all 48,000 visits; work from the cash-paying 72% who funded the clinics; or work from total visit volume as a single aggregate count. Alongside it she had to name the binding constraint behind the falling index — out-of-pocket affordability, clinical quality, awareness of Raíz among people who had never used it, or clinic capacity — and decide how much of the ninety days to spend quantifying the affordability map, from none of it to all of it, before committing to an offer.

The second was the offer. The bundle could be built as core essentials, with consultations, maternal-and-child care and chronic-disease follow-up and everything optional deferred; as a broad bundle containing everything Raíz could clinically deliver; or as a tiered offer with a low-cost essential tier and paid add-ons beside it. She had to set the share of the offer delivered through promoters rather than storefronts, anywhere from none to all of it, against the cost of training and supervising them. And the generics pharmacy could run at a thin margin inside the offer, run as a profit centre marking up medicines to fund consultations, or close, with patients referred elsewhere.

The third was the pair the committee would open first (see **Exhibit 2**). Raíz could buy third-party clinical accreditation, sign the industry's voluntary self-regulation code, or buy neither. Against that sat the fee: raise it by GTQ 5, hold it at GTQ 300, or cut it by GTQ 10 and add a membership. And she had to set the cross-subsidy — the share of the poorest patients' visits funded by higher-paying patients and services — from none of them to all of them.

The fourth was the pitch. She could take the committee a tradeoff account naming where access and margin diverged and which measure she had privileged; a scorecard showing margin, access and rating all rising together; or a short pack presenting the headline numbers as they stood. She had to commit to an eighteen-month subsidy path — declining as cross-subsidy and membership volume grew, flat with further grant money sought later, or rising to buy more patients now. She had to say what part the rating had played: the driver the plan was designed around, a questionnaire completed after the decisions were made, or a compliance formality. And she had to decide how firmly to hold, in front of the fund, the position that viability and impact were to be solved together rather than traded.

The fund would score her on four lines and on nothing else: the annual EBITDA impact of her plan against the GTQ 0.86 million the company earned in 2025; the access index she had inherited at 52; the external social rating at 30; and gate risk at 55. It would also track how much of the GTQ 4.0 million tranche her plan committed.

## Closing

At half past eight Colop set the ten o'clock meeting with the finance manager and the medical director, and asked for three things on the table when it started: the fund's letter, the monitoring scorecard, and the finance manager's demand model. Ninety days, four decisions to take in sequence, and the access index still reading 52.
