# Stop-n-Go: The Price on the Board

**Case Code:** STR-2026-758 · **Companion simulation:** Express 758 — *Petrol Station Stand-Off* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 9:30 on the evening of Sunday, July 19, 2026, Helen Marsh stood on the forecourt of Stop-n-Go with the price-board remote in her hand. Across the roundabout, 200 metres away, the Petrolia sign glowed over an empty forecourt. Both stations would change their boards at six the next morning, as they did every Monday, and neither had to say in advance what it would post.

Marsh had one decision to make before dawn, and she would make it again every Monday for the six weeks of the school summer holidays: hold her unleaded price at £1.50 a litre, or cut it to £1.40.

## The Protagonist

Marsh was 41 and had run Stop-n-Go for eight years. Her father had opened the station in 1994 beside a motorway junction in the East Midlands, and she had taken it over when he retired. Before that she had managed a builders' merchant for a decade. She knew her margins to the penny and her regular customers by their cars. She had never met Petrolia's manager, Darren Kell, except to nod to at the local business association.

## The Decision

Each Monday, Marsh would choose one of two prices for the week. There was no third option: the fuel supplier's delivery terms and the station's costs left £1.50 as the normal price and £1.40 as the lowest price she could post without selling at a loss on the fuel itself. Petrolia faced the same choice on the same morning. The summer weeks were the busiest of the year, and her bank would review the station's overdraft in September on the strength of what the six weeks earned.

## The Company and its Setting

Stop-n-Go was an independent station with six pumps, a small shop and a car wash. Petrolia was a franchise site of a regional chain, built in 2019 on the other side of the same roundabout. Between them, the two stations had the junction to themselves: the next fuel on the motorway was 22 miles away, and most drivers leaving the junction could see both boards before they chose.

That visibility shaped the business. Drivers who pulled off the motorway went to whichever station was cheaper, even by a few pence. When the two boards showed the same price, the traffic split roughly in half. Fuel margins in the United Kingdom were thin, and the shop and the car wash paid most of the station's fixed costs. The fuel profit was what was left to repay the overdraft.

## The Situation

Marsh's bookkeeper, Anita Nair, had worked out from last summer's till data what a week of fuel would earn each station under each combination of prices (see Exhibit 1). If both stations held at £1.50, each would make about £500 in the week. If Stop-n-Go cut to £1.40 while Petrolia stayed at £1.50, Stop-n-Go would take nearly all the traffic and make about £750, and Petrolia almost nothing. The reverse held if Petrolia cut and Stop-n-Go did not. If both cut to £1.40, each would make about £200.

Marsh had no information about what Kell would post on Monday. The two stations had never discussed prices. Kell's head office set his targets, and Marsh did not know what they were. She also knew that a discount fuel brand, ValueFuel, had planning permission for a site a mile down the A-road. Nobody at the business association knew when, or whether, it would open.

There was one more thing Marsh could do. She had Kell's mobile number. A message suggesting that both stations keep their boards at £1.50 would take ten seconds to write. Nair had reminded her that an agreement between competitors to fix prices was illegal under the Competition Act 1998, and that the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) investigated such agreements and fined the businesses involved. Parallel prices that each station chose on its own were a different matter.

If Marsh left the board at £1.50 all summer, her result each week would depend entirely on what Petrolia posted across the roundabout.

## What Marsh Controlled

Every Monday for six weeks, Marsh would set one thing: the **unleaded price**, either held at £1.50 a litre or cut to £1.40. She would have no way to change it until the following Monday.

At some point in the summer she might also face a second choice: whether to **send Kell a message proposing that both stations hold at £1.50**. It was not a price; it was a decision about whether to contact a competitor, with its own legal exposure.

At the end of each week, Marsh would know both stations' prices, her own **fuel profit for the week**, her **cumulative profit** since the start of the holidays, Petrolia's estimated weekly and **cumulative profit**, and whether any **CMA fine** had been imposed on Stop-n-Go (see Exhibit 2). The number her bank would look at in September was her own cumulative profit over the six weeks.

## Closing

At 9:45 the Petrolia sign flickered and went back to the same numbers. Marsh put the remote in her pocket and went inside to lock the till. She would set the board at 5:50.
