# Maison Lièvre: A Season Under Two Mandates

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1831 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1831 — *Creative Director: A Season at Maison Lièvre* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of 8 September, Julien Lièvre read the board resolution that had just made him responsible for the house that carried his family's name. He had been Creative Director for eleven days. Twenty-six weeks stood between him and the fixed, non-movable runway slot on 2 March at Paris Fashion Week. The board had approved a collection budget of €2.4 million and attached a mandate the role had never had to hold at once: the reviews had to be as strong as last season's, and, for the first time, the clothes had to sell.

Before fabric cut-off, Julien had to state the collection's creative thesis, set how novel the forty-two looks in the line would be, and name which number the board would hold him to if the season went sideways. Every week spent deciding cost the atelier money it did not have to spare.

## The Protagonist

Julien Lièvre, thirty-three, had spent six years as a senior designer at two other Paris houses before his grandfather's board brought him home to lead the studio he had grown up sketching in. He was well liked by the ateliers and well reviewed by the press, but had never been handed a collection's profit and loss, and had never had to defend a budget to anyone. His promotion had come eleven days before the board resolution, in the same meeting at which the private-equity fund's newly installed chief executive read out the previous season's numbers.

## The Decision

Julien's first task was to decide, in one sitting with his studio and the house's commercial leadership, how the season would be positioned: what the collection was for, how radical it would be, and which of the board's targets he would treat as the one that could not be missed. That decision would set the terms for the season's remaining stages (see **Exhibit 2**): how the €2.4 million was split, how the finished looks were judged, and how the season was defended.

## The Company and its Setting

Maison Lièvre was a Paris ready-to-wear and accessories house, founded by Julien's grandfather and built, over decades, into a name that showed twice a year on the official Fashion Week calendar. It generated €88 million in annual revenue at an 11% EBIT margin. Some months earlier, a private-equity fund had bought a 60% stake and installed a commercially aggressive chief executive, whose reading of the last several seasons was blunt: critics loved the Maison, and cash registers did not.

The house sold through a network of wholesale doors and its own boutiques, but four department-store accounts — 22% of wholesale revenue — mattered disproportionately, and had signalled they would cut their orders for next season if this collection did not sell.

## The Situation

Last season, under Julien's predecessor, the collection had earned a rave in *Vogue Runway*. It had also sold through at only 38% of full price, against a segment benchmark of 65–70% considered healthy, and 42% of the line had gone to markdown (see **Exhibit 1**). Julien's target was full-price sell-through of at least 62%, at least three placements in the top tier of the fashion press, and the loyalty of the four anchor doors. Two internal measures started low: brand heat at 20 out of 100, tracking editorial pull and perceived novelty, and creative governance at 15 out of 100, tracking whether the studio's design choices could be explained and defended after the fact. Neither had been formally tracked before this season.

The atelier had also warned that a compressed calendar would likely add about 18% — on the order of €430,000 — to standard sample costs, landing partway through the season regardless of what Julien decided now.

If Julien repeated the choices behind last season's rave — leaning toward the boldest, most photographable pieces — the arithmetic pointed at another season near 38–39% sell-through, the same scale of markdown, and the same four doors deciding whether to keep ordering.

## What Julien Controlled

In this first sitting, Julien had to commit to a creative thesis for the season — a single sentence the house's own chief financial officer and a *Vogue* editor would both have to accept. One option was a recognisable Lièvre signature, with statement looks built to pull demand toward the house's volume pieces. A second was a sharper break from the house's codes, aimed at the week's most talked-about show. A third was reassurance, built on the bestseller evolutions and carry-overs the wholesale buyers already knew and trusted.

Alongside the thesis, Julien had to set a target novelty mix across the forty-two looks: what share would be commercial pieces — carry-overs and bestseller evolutions that funded the season — what share would be statement pieces built to earn editorial attention and the show moment, and what share would be radical, boundary-pushing pieces, the riskiest and hardest to sell. The three shares had to add to 100%.

Finally, Julien had to name which single board metric he would treat as binding for the season: full-price sell-through, the number the board had hired him to fix; the tier-one editorial placements that made the show itself the priority; or simply staying inside the €2.4 million budget.

Once the thesis was set, the €2.4 million itself had to be split three ways. Julien could weight it toward the show itself — the set, the casting, the production that framed the runway moment; toward the product, putting most of the money into samples and fabric behind a tightly focused show; or toward a bare-bones show that spent almost nothing on staging at all. Independently, he had to set euro figures for the costly statement and radical pieces, which cost three to four times as much to develop and sample as a commercial piece; for the commercial volume that funded the season; and for a contingency held back against the atelier's warned overrun, rather than committing the full €2.4 million outright.

When the atelier returned costed prototypes, Julien had to choose how the final line would be judged: a panel scoring every look on novelty, predicted sell-through, margin and editorial pull together; a pure ranking on predicted sell-through and unit margin, with the top scores making the line; his own instinct as the house's sole creative authority; or open argument, with no explicit method, in the room. Whichever he chose, he still had to resolve one look directly — his Head of Design's most radical piece, which scored worst on predicted sell-through and cost the most to make, but was also the look most likely to anchor the show. He could keep the look exactly as designed, modify it toward a more wearable, sellable version, or cut it from the line and reassign its budget. Separately, he had to decide how strictly the final selection would hold to the mix and target he had set in this first sitting.

After the show, Julien had to decide how to present the season to the board: read the result against both halves of the mandate at once — sell-through and editorial reception together, with an account of what had driven each; report on commercial performance alone; or lead with critical reception as the season's real measure. He also had to propose, for future seasons, whether the studio kept the kind of evaluation method it had just used, moved to scoring designs purely on the data, or set evaluation aside and trusted instinct. And he had to say, before the same board that had given its chief executive a veto over anything risking sell-through, how the interface between his own family authority and the house's commercial oversight would be governed going forward: a standing agreement carving out a defined allowance for statement pieces alongside commercial guardrails on the rest, deference to the chief executive on any disputed look, or insistence on his own authority as the founder's grandson. Whatever position he took, he would have to say how firmly he meant to hold it.

## Closing

The studio manager waited by the door with mood boards for the first sketches. The atelier needed an answer on thesis and mix before it could quote a single sample. Julien picked up his pen.
