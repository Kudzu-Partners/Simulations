## Abstract

Hospital Santa Catalina, a 300-bed urban tertiary hospital in a Latin American capital city, entered Q1 2026 at 78% occupancy, 22% nurse turnover, and 42 days of cash on hand. Its five performance scores — Quality, Efficiency, Financial, Workforce, and Community — all stood at 650 on a 1,000-point scale. A new Chief Operating Officer, Dr. Mariana Beltrán, had six quarters to transform the hospital before her performance would be judged against an external benchmark and a pending value-based-care contract. A dengue outbreak, known in the epidemiological record and assessed by the hospital's own infectious-disease unit as 70% likely to reach Santa Catalina by Q3, loomed as the single biggest external shock. The case puts Beltrán at her desk the day before her first quarterly-plan submission, balancing occupancy against a known "capacity-quality paradox" — the empirically observed deterioration in quality metrics above 87% occupancy — and weighing three internally coherent Q1 plans.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the trade-off between capacity utilization and quality outcomes in an acute-care setting.
2. Analyze nurse-staffing ratios as both a cost lever and a quality lever, and quantify the compounding.
3. Design a Q1 operating plan that builds resilience against a known external shock.
4. Diagnose the difference between a financially efficient and an operationally resilient configuration.
5. Recommend a plan that protects the hospital's value-based-care eligibility and defend it under challenge.

On the night of **January 11, 2026**, **Dr. Mariana Beltrán** sat in the Chief Operating Officer's office at Hospital Santa Catalina, two floors above the emergency department, and read — for the third time — the infectious-disease unit's dengue memo. Outside the window, two ambulance bays were full; a third was empty; the lights of the ED triage desk had not dimmed since she arrived at 6:30 a.m. On her desk were thirteen decision cards, one for each Q1 decision. On the wall, the Board Performance Scorecard showed five scores at 650. Her first quarterly plan was due at 7:00 a.m.

---

## The Protagonist

Dr. Mariana Beltrán, 49, had joined Santa Catalina three months earlier as its first Chief Operating Officer in a structural reorganization that had separated medical-affairs leadership (retained by the Chief Medical Officer, Dr. Patricio Villaseñor) from operational leadership (handed to her). A trained internist with a master's in health-systems management and eight years of COO experience at a 180-bed private hospital in a neighbouring country, she had been recruited expressly to steer Santa Catalina toward the value-based-care reimbursement model that the largest private insurer in the country had signalled it would roll out in 2027. Her compensation carried a two-year vesting tranche tied to four of the five scorecard scores (all except Community, which the board held as a mission-side override) and an additional gate on days-cash-on-hand — below a floor, no vesting.

---

## The Decision

Beltrán had to submit, by 7:00 a.m. on January 12, a Q1 operating plan spanning thirteen discrete decisions:

1. Bed allocation (medical / surgical / ICU / obstetrics).
2. ED diversion threshold.
3. Elective-surgery slot target.
4. Nurse-to-patient ratio (by unit).
5. Overtime budget.
6. Infection-control investment.
7. Medication-safety investment.
8. Readmissions-reduction program funding.
9. Payer-contract stance.
10. Strategic investment (one of a fixed menu).
11. Hiring plan (gross and net).
12. Discharge-planning intensity.
13. Supplier-contract renegotiation scope.

The plan would be reviewed by the Chair of the Board Operations Committee at 8:00 a.m. Occupancy target — implicit in decisions 1, 2, 3, and 4 — was the decision she thought of as "the zero-th decision."

---

## Company Background

Hospital Santa Catalina was founded in 1936 as a community mission hospital and had, over nine decades, grown into a 300-bed tertiary facility with a full medical, surgical, critical-care, and obstetric profile. The hospital was operated by a non-profit foundation under a governance board of twelve members. 2025 revenues were USD 148M, with a small operating surplus (USD 2.6M, 1.8% margin) and a balance sheet with 42 days of cash on hand — roughly half the national peer median of 82 days.

The hospital employed 1,280 people, of whom approximately 380 were nursing staff. Nurse turnover had risen from 13% in 2019 to 22% in 2025, a pattern shared by peer hospitals as competing employment at newly opened private clinics had drawn experienced staff. ED volumes had risen 14% over five years; medical-admission throughput had not; ED-to-medical-floor boarding had become a visible problem.

Santa Catalina's five performance scores, reported quarterly to the board and benchmarked against a national peer set, had been stable at 630–680 for two years. The value-based-care contract, anticipated to be offered in mid-2027 by the largest private insurer, would require: a Quality score of 720 at signing, readmissions below 12%, HAI (healthcare-associated infection) rates below a defined peer percentile, and patient-satisfaction scores above a defined floor.

## Industry and Market Context

The Latin American private tertiary-care hospital market had entered a structural transition. The traditional fee-for-service reimbursement model had come under pressure from payers and regulators in multiple countries; value-based-care and bundled-payment contracts had been piloted in seven national markets between 2022 and 2025. Hospitals with high capacity utilization but weak quality metrics had, in pilots, been systematically excluded from the new contracts.

Research on hospital operations converged on two findings. First, above an occupancy threshold of approximately 85–87%, quality metrics deteriorated non-linearly: HAI rates rose, medication errors increased, ED wait-times crossed operational and regulatory thresholds, and nurse-retention degraded. Second, staffing ratio was a stronger predictor of quality outcomes than aggregate FTE count: a hospital with a 1:5 ratio at peak demand outperformed a hospital with the same nursing FTE but a 1:4 average and 1:8 peak ratio.

## The Situation

### How we got here

Beltrán's appointment in October 2025 had followed an eighteen-month period of deteriorating ED metrics and a board-commissioned external review. The review had named three root causes: insufficient discharge-planning capacity, a chronic deferral of infection-control investment, and a tight-coupling between the elective-surgery volume target and the hospital's operating margin. The review had recommended the separation of medical and operational leadership — accomplished, in part, through Beltrán's hiring.

### What Beltrán knew

The infectious-disease unit's January 9 memo placed a 70% probability on a dengue outbreak reaching the hospital's catchment area in Q3 2026, with an anticipated 40% surge in ED volume for a six-week window. Historical data from similar outbreaks showed that hospitals entering a surge above 85% baseline occupancy transitioned into ambulance-diversion and elective-cancellation states within two weeks; hospitals entering below 80% absorbed the surge without a material quality deterioration.

### What Beltrán did not know

Whether the outbreak would, in fact, materialize at the anticipated magnitude. Whether the board's Operations Committee would accept a Q1 plan that reduced the elective-surgery volume target — the lever most directly tied to short-run margin. Whether the Chief Medical Officer, Dr. Patricio Villaseñor, would back her publicly.

### What others were saying

**Fernanda Aguilar**, CFO, pressed for maximum occupancy and minimum overtime. "Margin is one point eight percent. A cut in elective surgery is a cut in margin. Hold elective slots at 94% of capacity. Overtime within historical."

**Dr. Patricio Villaseñor**, CMO, pressed for resilience. "The dengue memo is a medical decision. Hold below 85%. Nursing ratio 1:4 in medicine, 1:2 in ICU. Infection-control investment at full."

**Héctor Ortiz**, Director of Human Resources, pressed for a structural nursing investment. "Retention first. Ratio second. A USD 1.2M retention package this quarter; a USD 800K ratio plan in Q2."

---

## Options Under Consideration

- **Option A — Margin-Maximizing (the Aguilar plan):** Elective slots at 94% of capacity; occupancy target 88%; nursing ratio 1:5 general medicine, 1:3 ICU; overtime budget within historical; infection-control investment at baseline; readmission-reduction program at baseline. Q1 expected margin USD 0.9M; Quality score projected at 645 (flat); vulnerable to Q3 dengue shock.

- **Option B — Resilience-First (the Villaseñor plan):** Elective slots at 82% of capacity; occupancy target 82%; nursing ratio 1:4 general medicine, 1:2 ICU; overtime budget +25% of historical; infection-control investment +USD 0.6M; readmission-reduction program +USD 0.4M. Q1 expected margin negative USD 0.3M; Quality score projected at 675; absorbs expected Q3 shock.

- **Option C — Phased Recovery (the Ortiz plan):** Elective slots at 88% of capacity; occupancy target 85%; nursing ratio 1:4.5 general medicine, 1:2 ICU; overtime at historical; infection-control investment +USD 0.3M; retention package USD 1.2M; readmission-reduction program at baseline. Q1 expected margin USD 0.3M; Quality score projected at 660; moderately exposed to Q3 shock.

Beltrán stacked the thirteen decision cards in the order she believed the Operations Committee would ask the questions. She had until 7:00 a.m. Occupancy was the zero-th decision. The dengue memo was at the top of the pile. Her predecessor had resigned, in part, over this question. The full beds were how, in the memo's phrase, "the hospitals fail."
