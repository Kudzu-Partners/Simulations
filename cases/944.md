# Calderwick Partners: The First Five Minutes

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-944 · **Companion simulation:** Express 944 — *CaseInterviewLive — Five Minutes to Structure a Case* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On the morning of Wednesday, April 15, 2026, Clara Ibarrola Vidal sat at the kitchen table of a shared flat in Madrid and reread an email from Calderwick Partners. Her final-round case interview was on Friday, April 17, at 10:00: one partner, one business problem, twenty-five minutes. The email repeated two lines of feedback from her first round, three weeks earlier. She had been "too quick to numbers" and "not structured enough." She had passed anyway.

She had forty-eight hours, and she did not intend to spend them on new material. She intended to settle how she would open a case: what she would ask first, how long she would stay silent, how she would lay out the problem, how she would end, and how often she would stop to check. She would rehearse that opening three times on Wednesday, on three practice cases, and take into Friday whatever she had settled.

## The Protagonist

Ibarrola was twenty-six. She had spent three years as a financial analyst at Hortavell Distribución, a food distributor in Valencia, building the monthly margin reports for its sales director, before enrolling in a one-year master's in management in Madrid. She was fast with a spreadsheet and at ease with a profit-and-loss statement. Over the winter she had practised about a dozen cases with classmates. Given an hour, she solved most of them. Given twenty-five minutes, she was usually still computing when the time ran out.

## The Decision

Ibarrola had to decide how she would run the first five minutes of a case interview, the stretch before any calculation began, and to make that decision three times, once for each practice case, as the cases grew harder (see Exhibit 2).

The advice she had collected did not agree. Jaime Lacasa, her flatmate, who had joined another Madrid consultancy the previous September, told her to name a textbook framework early: "They want to see you know the toolkit." He also told her never to let a silence run past twenty seconds, because an interviewer would read it as freezing. Pilar Ugarte, her former finance director at Hortavell, told her to ask before she assumed anything and to think before she spoke, however long that took. Rafael Ortuño, the Calderwick manager who had run her first round, had left only the two lines in the email.

## The Company and its Setting

Calderwick Partners was a strategy consultancy founded in Madrid in 2009, with about 140 consultants in Madrid and Lisbon. Most of its work was for retailers, consumer-goods companies, industrial manufacturers and private-equity funds weighing acquisitions. It hired about twelve associates a year from roughly 1,500 applications (see Exhibit 1).

The final round was a single case. The partner read a short prompt, the candidate had twenty-five minutes, and paper was allowed. Candidates who had been through the process said the partners formed a view early, often within the first five minutes. Calderwick itself did not say so.

## The Situation

The practice tool licensed by Ibarrola's master's programme worked the way the final round did. It read a prompt in an interviewer's voice. She recorded the five choices that made up her opening, ran it, and received a coach's note on each choice. The tool scored every opening out of 100 in five parts: the clarifying question, the silence, the structure, the close of the walkthrough (which the round log headed "hypothesis") and her conviction. It kept a running maturity score out of 300 across the three cases. Beside the scores it showed how an interviewer would likely read her, in one of four grades: framework cosplay, not yet structured, offer-track, or strong offer-track. When the session ended it would grade the whole of it out of 100. At 09:30 on Wednesday the maturity score stood at 0 of 300, and no reading had been given.

The three prompts were set in advance (see Exhibit 2). The first concerned a sports-retail chain in Spain and Portugal, about 120 stores, whose profit had fallen 18% in two years on flat revenue. The second concerned a European specialty-coffee brand weighing entry into Mexico, with €40 million to deploy over three years. The third concerned a private-equity fund considering a mid-sized industrial manufacturer with €280 million in revenue and a margin of 9% on earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA). Its interviewer would read the prompt flatly, cross their arms and say nothing.

If nothing changed, Ibarrola knew how she would open, because she had opened that way a dozen times: no question, straight into the numbers within seconds, a structure recalled from a course, a stop after the last branch to wait for the interviewer, and frequent checks on whether she was on track. That opening had carried her through the first round. It had also produced the two lines in the email.

## What Ibarrola Controlled

For each case she settled five things.

The **clarifying question** came first: none, launching straight into the analysis; which industry the client was in; whether the goal was a short-term recovery or a three-year repositioning; or how the client defined success, as margin, market share or cash.

Then the **silence before speaking**: none, speaking at once; about thirty seconds; about sixty seconds, writing a structure on paper; or two minutes or more.

Then the **structure**. She could take one of the two standard checklists she had memorised from her courses, one for the forces acting on an industry and one for the elements of a marketing plan, and apply it as it stood. She could take a standard checklist and adapt it lightly to the prompt. Or she could build three or four branches from the facts of that prompt alone.

Then the **close of the walkthrough**: stopping after the last branch and waiting for the interviewer; ending with a question, whether this was right so far; or ending with a statement of where she would start and why, in the form "I would start with this branch, because I think this is what is going on."

Last, her **conviction**: checking in with the interviewer about every ninety seconds; checking at each move from one branch to the next; or pushing through to a conclusion and checking once.

Ibarrola could argue most of these either way. A long silence could read as thought or as paralysis; an immediate start, as confidence or as nerves. A known checklist showed preparation and covered the ground; branches built on the spot showed thinking and could miss something a checklist would have caught. A stated view gave the interviewer something to test, and could anchor her on the wrong one. Frequent checks caught a wrong turn early and handed the interviewer the wheel.

## Closing

At 09:40 Ibarrola put a pad of squared paper beside the laptop, set her phone's stopwatch to zero and started the first prompt. The voice began: a sports-retail chain, about 120 stores, profit down 18% in two years. The stopwatch was running, and the pad was still blank.
