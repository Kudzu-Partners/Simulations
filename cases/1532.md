# Petrolia: The Three-Year View

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1532 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1532 — *Workforce Planning Under Strategic Shifts — The 3-Year View* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Wednesday, April 22, 2026, Patricia Calderón-Mora read the draft strategic plan for the third time and started a list of what Petrolia S.A. would have to be capable of doing by 2029. The draft ran twenty-eight pages and named eight bets the cabinet had agreed in working form. The word capability did not appear in it, which might or might not have been a gap.

Calderón-Mora owed the chief executive a draft workforce plan before the strategic offsite three weeks out, and the finished plan — costed, defended, and signed by the chief financial officer — before the board's joint Strategy and Talent session eleven weeks out in July. Andrés Vargas-Quesada had set the terms in the corridor after the previous Tuesday's cabinet meeting. He did not want a workforce plan that arrived once the strategy was locked; he wanted one the cabinet locked alongside it. In practice that meant the capital allocation decisions of the next three years would cite whatever she wrote.

## The Protagonist

Calderón-Mora, 51, had been chief human resources officer of Petrolia for nineteen months, arriving from a regional industrial group where she had run two plant closures. The preceding nine months had gone into infrastructure rather than plans: a skills inference layer that had tagged 91% of the workforce against a single taxonomy, a monthly attrition forecast, a reskilling-velocity dashboard, and an internal mobility marketplace. Sofía Jiménez-Aguilar, her workforce-planning lead, built the labour-supply models; Tomás Quirós-Madrigal, lent from finance for the cycle, held the cost assumptions.

## The Decision

Calderón-Mora had to produce three linked documents and defend them to five people.

The first was a capability-requirements memo translating each of the eight strategic bets into the specific capabilities the workforce would need in years one, two and three. The second was a supply-and-demand matrix, capability by capability, run under three named regimes: the strategy as written; an accelerated renewables path; and a macro-shock path with regulatory friction. The third was a sourcing architecture — for each gap, whether Petrolia would build the capability through internal reskilling, hire it externally, obtain it through an alliance or service contract, rent it from consultants or an outsourcing provider, or automate it with the agent stack the company had stood up the year before.

The five were the cabinet: Vargas-Quesada; Mariana Solís-Ramírez, the chief financial officer, who would read every footnote; Rodrigo Ureña-Vargas, the chief operating officer, who had run the production assets for twenty years; Eugenio Mora-Salazar, the lead independent director; and Luisa Esquivel-Brenes, who chaired the audit committee and was sceptical of judgement she could not verify against evidence.

## The Company and its Setting

Petrolia S.A. was the integrated energy group of Costaria, a Central American republic. It held upstream concessions in two onshore basins, a refining and lubricants complex outside the capital, 340 retail service stations, and a renewables platform of utility-scale solar, two wind farms and an early green-hydrogen pilot. Revenue in the last completed financial year was USD 9.2 billion at an EBITDA margin in the mid-teens; direct headcount was roughly 14,200, with a further 6,000 contracted. The platform contributed under 5% of EBITDA and consumed close to a quarter of growth capital expenditure (see **Exhibit 1**).

The board's posture, agreed at the November 2025 retreat, was to defend the core, build the platform and retire the dead weight. Costaria's energy ministry credited concession-holders for renewables build-out while tightening a local-content requirement on technical roles above mid-level.

## The Situation

The eight bets were uneven in what they demanded. Three — defending upstream production, recovering refining margin, completing the lubricants rationalisation — ran on capabilities Petrolia had held for forty years, and raised the opposite question: which of them could be let go without destabilising the production envelope. Two were the platform bets, and both were thin. Bet 5 committed the company to 1.2 GW of operating renewables capacity and 0.6 GW under construction by year three; Esteban Rodríguez-Solano, who ran talent for the platform, had spent nine months saying Petrolia could not operate that fleet. Bet 4 turned the 340 stations into a customer-experience network, a discipline in which the company held no seats at all.

The constraints on closing those gaps were specific and not symmetrical (see **Exhibit 2**). Market scarcity in Costaria capped external hiring for renewables asset-management engineering at eight to ten people over three years; the reskilling pathway from refining instrumentation yielded roughly forty seats over the same period. Customer-experience product management could not be reskilled into at the required depth inside the horizon, and regional consumer-technology and ride-hailing employers recruited from the same pool. Agent product ownership needed eight seats by the end of year one, one per function, against a competency profile already written and drawn from populations inside the company. Methane-management engineering needed two seats before the European methane rule began to apply the year after next; perhaps fifty people in the regional market held the skill.

Upstream production engineering ran the other way. Jiménez-Aguilar's model showed about 40% of that cohort above the age of 55 with a retirement curve falling inside the planning window. Ureña-Vargas had said what he could absorb: forty seats over three years without operational risk, and beyond that, institutional knowledge he could not replace at any price. The glide the model produced was closer to 120.

If Calderón-Mora produced nothing before the offsite, the strategic plan would lock without a capability translation and the workforce plan would follow it as a ratification exercise: the platform's 1.2 GW target would arrive with no asset-management bench, retail would keep managing stations for fuel throughput, the methane seats would go unfilled into the regulatory date, and the upstream cohort would retire on its own schedule rather than a planned one.

## What Calderón-Mora Controlled

In the first session she set three things. How finely to decompose each bet — naming capabilities at the level of the bet, at the level of role clusters, or role by role with a seniority tag and a year-by-year demand figure. Whether the list of capabilities to build and the list to retire sat in one document the cabinet endorsed together, or in two documents circulated separately. And how long the memo ran: ten pages, fourteen, or twenty.

In the second she set the modelling. How many of the twenty-eight named capabilities to carry at full paragraph depth across all three regimes — four, eight or fourteen — with the remainder in structured shorthand. How to treat the regimes: detail the central case and note the alternates in passing; detail the central case and record the alternates; or model all three to the same depth. And whether attrition ran at one rate across the regimes or varied with them, with renewables hires recruited away faster on the accelerated path and upstream retiring earlier under macro-shock.

In the third she chose, for each of the four load-bearing gaps — renewables asset management, retail customer-experience product management, agent product ownership, methane-management engineering — one of the five sourcing routes. She set the upstream retire-and-redirect number at forty, eighty, one hundred and twenty or one hundred and sixty seats over three years. And she decided whether the cost of the control framework for automated capabilities appeared as its own line in the cost stack, where Solís-Ramírez would price it, or sat inside the sourcing lines it belonged to.

She would see five readings, one per cabinet member, each starting at 50 on a scale of 0 to 100, moving up or down after each session as the cabinet reacted; their average; and the movement each session produced in each reading. The five together would decide whether the plan was adopted as a commitment that bound capital allocation for three years, or sent back.

## Closing

At the end of the afternoon Calderón-Mora wrote the eight bets down the left margin of a blank page and ruled three columns beside them, one for each year. The offsite was three weeks out. The board session was eleven. The strategy team had produced twenty-eight pages. Nobody had yet written what Petrolia would be able to do in 2029, or what it would cost to be able to do it.
