## Abstract

Café del Barrio S.R.L. was, in the case universe, a three-product independent specialty coffee shop in the Palermo comuna of Buenos Aires — approximately ARS 42m in 2025 revenue, six employees, a 72-square-metre storefront on Calle Honduras, a startup loan from Banco Ceibo with eleven months of principal remaining, a three-SKU product line (Mezcla Espresso at a current ARS 650 shelf price; Single Origin at ARS 1,100; Cold Brew at ARS 950), an unaudited variable-cost sheet the roaster had pieced together from the July 2025 green-coffee invoice and the February 2026 packaging run, and a competitive environment in which a neighbourhood chain, Café Primera Vuelta, had announced on April 14, 2026, an espresso at ARS 480 — below Café del Barrio's variable cost of ARS 420 plus the ARS 200 contribution-margin floor Beltrán had privately promised his co-founder they would never break. The April 2026 Q2 pricing cycle opened on April 23. The board of two co-founders had asked Beltrán to complete the twenty-minute *Precio Correcto* primer exercise and commit three quarter-prices the staff could print on the morning menu. The case opens at 8:12 a.m. on Thursday, April 23, 2026, at the Calle Honduras storefront as **Rodrigo Beltrán**, a 31-year-old co-founder and operator, prepares the three-price commitment.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Classify each of the twenty April variable-cost entries as a true unit variable cost versus a period expense miscoded as variable, calculate a per-cup contribution margin for each of the three products, and articulate why a price set below the variable-cost floor destroys profitability on every unit sold and why the contribution-margin floor is the operator's first and non-negotiable pricing rule.
2. Read the three demand-elasticity regimes — Mezcla Espresso at ε ≈ −1.8, Single Origin at ε ≈ −1.1, Cold Brew at ε ≈ −0.7 — against an identical 20% price increase and articulate why the same lever produces revenue collapse on one product, mild contraction on a second, and revenue expansion on a third, and why the textbook revenue-maximising price and the textbook profit-maximising price are almost never the same number.
3. Use Kent Monroe's value-perception envelope — a willingness-to-pay floor below which margin is left on the table and a willingness-to-pay ceiling above which volume collapses — to locate each product's viable pricing band and articulate why the band is narrower than intuition suggests and different for every product in the portfolio.
4. Design a competitive-response posture to Café Primera Vuelta's ARS 480 espresso announcement using Michael Spence's signalling logic — match-the-price-and-win-a-war-you-cannot-win-on-cost versus hold-and-signal-quality — and articulate why the specialty-craft signal either holds or does not hold depending on whether the buyer can verify quality independently of price.
5. Commit the three-price Q2 package and articulate why the right price is not intuition but the joint product of the contribution-margin floor, the elasticity estimate and the value-perception envelope — three lenses on one number — and why this primer is distinct from the algorithmic (sim 173) and revenue-management-hospitality (sim 006) pricing frames elsewhere in the catalogue.

On the morning of **Thursday, April 23, 2026**, **Rodrigo Beltrán** arrived at Café del Barrio's Calle Honduras storefront in Palermo at 8:12 a.m., unlocked the service counter before the 7:30 a.m. baristas finished the Mezcla grind, opened the PrecioCorrecto workbook his co-founder had finalised Tuesday evening, and re-read the sentence he had taped to the inside of the cash-register door: *"Three lenses. One price."*

---

## The Protagonist

Beltrán, 31, had been Café del Barrio's co-founder and day-to-day operator since the shop opened in August 2024, had completed an Administración de Empresas degree at the Universidad Almagro in 2017, had spent six years in supply-chain roles at a Buenos Aires specialty-food importer, and had joined his co-founder — a former specialty-coffee head roaster — in the eighteen-month build-out from mid-2023. His pricing instincts, he had told his co-founder over Sunday-evening numbers, had been shaped by two competing teachings: a university managerial-economics course that framed every price as an elasticity problem, and four years watching the specialty-food importer set prices by instinct and anchor. The co-founder had assigned him the April primer exercise on three considerations: his comfort treating variable cost as a floor rather than as a suggestion; his willingness to hold prices under visible competitor pressure rather than matching; and his command of the value-perception envelope as a buyer-side rather than seller-side construct.

---

## The Decision

Beltrán had to commit, by the 10:00 a.m. opening, three Q2 shelf prices the morning barista would print on the chalkboard menu.

1. **Mezcla Espresso.** Current price ARS 650 against a variable cost of ARS 420 and a contribution-margin floor of ARS 200. Demand elasticity estimated at −1.8 in the April panel. The competitor's ARS 480 announcement tests whether Beltrán matches (a four-point margin collapse that cascades through the espresso-as-gateway product), holds (and signals specialty quality), or raises (and loses the price-sensitive segment). A revenue-maximising price around ARS 750 and a profit-maximising price closer to ARS 950 sit on the same demand curve.
2. **Single Origin.** Current price ARS 1,100 against a variable cost of ARS 680. Moderate elasticity around −1.1. The least competed product — no direct Palermo equivalent at the ARS 1,100 tier. The envelope suggests room to move from 1,100 toward 1,250.
3. **Cold Brew.** Current price ARS 950 against a variable cost of ARS 420. Inelastic demand around −0.7. The Monroe paradox — raising price from 950 to 1,800 drops volume from 48 to 36 but lifts revenue from 45,600 to 64,800. The specialty-craft signal the cold-brew ritual carries is verifiable only to a cold-brew-literate buyer, which is most of the afternoon clientele.

Commitments: a Q2 price per product, a competitive-response posture on the espresso, and a short paragraph — the co-founder's request — explaining the price logic in terms the morning barista could repeat to a customer who asked *why has the espresso gone up* or *why hasn't it gone down*.

## Sectoral Background

The 2020–2025 Buenos Aires specialty-coffee-shop category had, in the case universe, grown from approximately ARS 9bn to ARS 28bn in current pesos, concentrated in the Palermo, Colegiales, Villa Crespo and Recoleta comunas, with three operator tiers (international chains including Starbucks and domestic chain Café Martínez; local specialty groups; independents). The 2025 Palermo specialty panel registered an average espresso price of ARS 620, an average single-origin price of ARS 1,050, and an average cold-brew price of ARS 880, with significant variance across operators and no integrated price-discipline body. Inflation in 2025 had run at approximately 211% per the INDEC index, which had compressed the effective real price of most specialty products by 14–22% year-on-year and forced every operator to raise nominal prices on a rolling quarterly or monthly schedule.

## Industry and Market Context

The 1985–2025 pricing literature produced three findings the primer sim had to respect. First, Monroe's perceived-value-pricing tradition, operationalised as a willingness-to-pay envelope rather than as a cost-plus markup, produced documented revenue readings approximately 12–28% higher than cost-plus regimes on comparable 2018–2024 independent-food-service panels. Second, the Nagle-and-Hogan elasticity tradition, with segment-level rather than aggregate elasticity estimates, produced documented gross-profit readings approximately 18–34% higher than single-elasticity regimes on comparable multi-SKU portfolios. Third, Spence's market-signalling framework, applied to quality-verifiable categories where the buyer cannot pre-taste the espresso, predicted that the higher-price, higher-quality positioning would hold against a visible lower-price competitor so long as the specialty signal (source stories, brew method, origin transparency) was legible at the point of purchase.

The post-2010 behavioural-pricing literature added two findings. Reference-price anchoring — the customer's prior-purchase memory of what an espresso costs in Palermo — was the single strongest moderator of immediate demand response to a price change, with a documented 60–80% of the total elasticity effect realising in the first four weeks. And the charm-price and nine-ending effects had smaller than catalogue-study magnitudes in specialty-food categories where the buyer is repeat and familiar with the operator.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The March 2026 monthly read registered a gross margin of 44% against a budget of 51%, driven by a 17% green-coffee cost increase the roaster had absorbed without a price adjustment and a 9% packaging-run cost increase that had landed on the February P&L. The April 14 competitor announcement from Café Primera Vuelta — the ARS 480 espresso — had landed on the neighbourhood WhatsApp operator group that afternoon. The co-founder's April 16 note: *"We do not match. We do not pretend the announcement did not happen. We explain the price on Monday."*

### What Beltrán knew

Variable cost per product as calculated from the April panel; the three elasticity estimates; the Monroe envelope's rough ceiling and floor for each product; the competitor announcement and its likely two-to-four-week duration; the Palermo reference-price range across the three products; and the contribution-margin floor of ARS 200 per cup that both co-founders had privately committed to.

### What he did not know

Whether the Café Primera Vuelta ARS 480 espresso was a three-week promotion or a twelve-month default; whether the specialty-craft signal would hold across the afternoon commuter segment whose loyalty was thinner than the weekend regular segment's; whether the Single Origin price move from 1,100 toward 1,250 would trigger a churn of the ARS 1,100 anchor customers or would be absorbed as inflation-matching; whether the Cold Brew move toward 1,200–1,400 would preserve the inelastic-demand estimate or trip a perception threshold; and whether the barista could deliver the one-paragraph price-logic explanation credibly in the fifteen-second average counter interaction.

### What others were saying

**The Co-Founder (Roaster)**: *"Do not match. If we match, the customer learns the espresso is worth 480. Once that anchor sets, it never un-sets."*

**The Morning Barista (four years with the shop)**: *"If the Mezcla goes up, the 8:00 a.m. regulars will say something. I need the one sentence. A short one."*

**The Banco Ceibo Relationship Officer**: *"The loan principal is due in eleven months. I do not care what you price. I care what the December P&L says."*

**The Palermo Neighbourhood Specialty-Coffee WhatsApp**: *"Primera Vuelta is not a specialty shop. They will run 480 for six weeks and then kill the promo. Do not match."*

**The Accountant (part-time fractional)**: *"Your variable-cost sheet is close. Milk waste is under-counted by 4–6%. Fix it before you set the Cold Brew price."*

## Options Under Consideration

Beltrán had narrowed the price space to three commitment postures.

- **Option A — Hold-Mezcla, raise-Single-Origin, raise-Cold-Brew (Recommended).** Mezcla ARS 650 unchanged; Single Origin ARS 1,100 → ARS 1,250; Cold Brew ARS 950 → ARS 1,350. Competitive response: hold and signal. The contribution-margin floor is respected on every SKU; the Monroe envelope ceiling is respected on Single Origin and Cold Brew; the Spence signal holds against Primera Vuelta because the buyer can verify specialty quality through the source-story and the brew-method visibility.
- **Option B — Match Mezcla at ARS 480, hold Single Origin, hold Cold Brew.** Rejected — Mezcla moves below the contribution-margin floor on every cup; gateway-product logic does not recover the lost margin through trading-up, because the reference-anchor now sits at 480; the Q4 loan principal cannot be covered on the revised P&L trajectory.
- **Option C — Raise all three aggressively: Mezcla to 950, Single Origin to 1,400, Cold Brew to 1,800.** Rejected — Mezcla demand collapses under the −1.8 elasticity (estimated 41% volume drop against 46% price increase, revenue falls); the Single Origin move trips the Monroe ceiling at a moment when the afternoon traffic is uncertain; the Cold Brew is the only SKU where the aggressive raise is defensible, and it is already partially captured in Option A.

Beltrán's private view was that Option A was the right commitment and that the decision most correlated with the Q4 loan-coverage outcome was the Cold Brew move, because the Cold Brew was the only SKU with a steeply inelastic demand estimate and therefore the only SKU where a price raise improved both revenue and margin. The *Precio Correcto* primer sits at the undergraduate business-pricing tier in the 2026 Express catalogue, distinct from Pacifico Pricing Power (sim 006, revenue-management hospitality, five-season seasonal curve) and LatAm PriceBot (sim 173, algorithmic dynamic pricing with regulator). Primer 291 emphasises the three-lens integration for the operator without an algorithm and without a revenue-management system; 006 emphasises the seasonal yield-management layer; 173 emphasises the algorithmic and regulatory-risk layer.
