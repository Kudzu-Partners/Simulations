# The Maritima National Economic Council: Sequencing the Single Market

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1987 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1987 — *Integrate the Region* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 2 March 2026, Nadia Salamat opened the ratified single-market protocol at the annex that mattered and read a date. The bloc's members had thirty-six months to eliminate the tariffs remaining on their sensitive lists, recognise one another's professional qualifications, and let skilled workers move without permits. The clock had started the previous Friday. What the bloc had not decided, and would not decide, was the order in which the Republic of Maritima did any of it.

That was Salamat's file. As Chief Economic Advisor she signed the national integration schedule, and the Maritima National Economic Council would settle it in four sittings: a diagnosis of the sector map at month zero, the schedule in the first half-year, the development fund in the second, and a defence of the package at the bloc's mid-transition review in month eighteen. The arithmetic did not close. Removing the sensitive-list tariffs took MRD 2.1 billion a year out of the budget, a 4.4% structural gap, and the Prime Minister's mandate ran to one sentence: the regional competitiveness index, 62 out of 100 that March, was not to fall below 60 at any point in the transition.

## The Protagonist

Salamat, 53, had been Chief Economic Advisor since 2021, after eleven years in the trade ministry and four as Maritima's lead negotiator on rules of origin. A Prime Minister who wanted a schedule tabled rather than a position defended had appointed her, and she signed what the Council sent to cabinet.

## The Decision

Salamat had to decide what Maritima opened, in what order, over what period, and who paid for the adjustment — and to keep deciding as the transition ran and the numbers moved.

## The Council and its Setting

The Republic of Maritima was an archipelago of 58 million people, with MRD 480 billion of GDP in 2025 and MRD 168 billion of exports, 35% of output. Electronics assembly was 28% of exports and the sector where Maritima's costs sat furthest below the bloc average. Processed agriculture and palm derivatives were next-largest and carried the tariff lines producing most of the revenue at risk. Garments were 14% of exports and the largest industrial employer, with 310,000 workers facing a bloc member producing at lower cost. Business services was the youngest of the four, and its asset was the people mutual recognition would make portable.

Maritima had been in the bloc since 1999, richer than four members and poorer than four; on protocols decided by majority, the median member was the deciding vote. The Council coordinated trade, labour, fiscal and competitiveness policy and held a development fund of MRD 9.6 billion for the cycle, under a statutory ceiling.

## The Situation

The dossier in front of the Council covered eight sectors. Its constraints ran to one page (**Exhibit 1**). Garments and processed agriculture between them employed 550,000 people, 310,000 of those in garments; the secretariat put displacement at between 55,000 and 90,000 jobs within twenty-four months, the range turning on how fast the lists came down. Mutual recognition was projected to pull between 8,000 and 14,000 nurses, engineers and accountants to higher-wage member states in the first year. On the bloc's own arithmetic, a full and immediate schedule was worth about MRD 6.0 billion a year in net trade gains.

Against that, the money. Credible adjustment needs came to between MRD 14 billion and MRD 18 billion; the fund held MRD 9.6 billion and could not be topped up. The MRD 2.1 billion of annual tariff revenue that would disappear was the revenue that cross-subsidised rural development, and the Finance Ministry had called the resulting gap structural rather than transitional. The protocol also set a bloc minimum: a member tabling anything slower forfeited MRD 3.4 billion of co-financing and its seat on the committee that wrote the rules of origin.

The Council was split. The trade and industry directors wanted the tariffs gone while Maritima still held the seat to shape what came next; the labour and migration directors wanted every line phased to thirty-six months.

The protocol carried one instrument for the transition itself. Where a member's subsidised exports injured a domestic industry, the affected state could impose a temporary safeguard on that line without breaching its schedule or losing co-financing. Maritima's trade-remedies unit kept an open file on the bloc's lowest-cost garment producer. At month eighteen the package went to the mid-transition review, before the Prime Minister and a Secretariat that had sent back two members' schedules.

If the Council tabled nothing the clock ran anyway: the sensitive lists came down on the bloc's timetable, the MRD 2.1 billion left the budget with nothing behind it, displacement arrived at the top of the projected range with no adjustment support funded, and the rules of origin were written by the members who turned up.

## What Salamat Controlled

At the first sitting she named, from the eight sectors in the dossier, the one Maritima would open fastest and the one where the tension between gains and jobs was sharpest and which would therefore be phased. She fixed the Council's position on the bloc's full and immediate schedule — the target to hit, a yardstick to be beaten on net welfare, or a document with no bearing on a country that intended to protect its workforce. And she set how much of the secretariat's analytical capacity went into scoring the eight sectors before committing, as a share of the total.

At the second sitting she set the instruments. Three tariff paths were open: eliminate every remaining sensitive-list tariff at once; bring some sectors down inside twelve months and stretch others across the full thirty-six; or phase every line to the thirty-six-month limit. Three labour regimes were open: recognise all bloc qualifications immediately; recognise them while funding bonded scholarships, return incentives and a wage-convergence schedule; or stage recognition profession by profession, opening those with no domestic shortage first and holding nurses and engineers back. She placed the schedule against the bloc minimum — meeting or exceeding it, tabling below it, or going faster than required — and reserved a share of the schedule as safeguard headroom, if she wanted any.

At the third sitting the fund was divided. Five claims were on the table (**Exhibit 2**), each with a ministry behind it, and Salamat set a figure in MRD billions against each. Together they could not exceed MRD 9.6 billion; an uncommitted balance stayed available for the rest of the cycle.

At the fourth sitting three decisions remained. If the exposed sectors came under pressure she could hold the schedule as tabled; suspend liberalisation altogether, which the protocol treated as non-compliance and which cost the co-financing and the seat; or trigger the permitted safeguard on the affected line and accelerate retraining. She could leave the fund as allocated or move money to trade-facilitation infrastructure or to retraining and wage insurance. She decided what the pitch to the Prime Minister and the Secretariat led with — the trade-gains figure, the employment record, or the four measures together — and recorded, as a percentage, how firmly the Council stood behind its reading of the trade-off.

Four numbers followed her. Trade gains were the net annual gain from liberalisation, in MRD billions, starting at zero. The employment effect was net jobs against a no-integration baseline, in thousands, negative when displacement outran what the opening created. The competitiveness index was the bloc's 0–100 measure of productivity and business environment, at 62 in March and floored at 60. Fiscal cost was the unfunded gap the Finance Ministry would carry, in MRD billions — what tariff elimination removed, less whatever replaced it. Beside them the secretariat kept the share of the MRD 9.6 billion committed, the first-year outflow of qualified professionals, and a running record of each sitting's move with the four numbers that stood after it.

## Closing

Salamat wrote the eight sectors down the left of a sheet of paper and ruled three columns beside them: open, phase, upgrade. The fund was MRD 9.6 billion, the need was MRD 14 to 18 billion, the floor was 60. The clock was three days old.
