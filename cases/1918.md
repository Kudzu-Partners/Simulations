# Granger Hardware & Outdoor: Nine Months on the Highway

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1918 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1918 — *Roadside Strategy* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of May 4, 2026, Tessa Granger read the trade-press item twice and then walked the length of her own store, counting the aisles MegaBuild would sell cheaper. The regional chain had confirmed a 60,000-square-foot store on the same state highway, twelve miles south of Granger Hardware & Outdoor in Logan, Utah, opening in nine months. On fasteners, paint and power tools, MegaBuild's everyday prices ran 8% to 15% below hers, across a selection several times deeper than anything she could stock.

She had those nine months and US$300,000 — her savings plus a line from the U.S. Small Business Administration carrying 9.5% interest — to decide four things and have them running before MegaBuild's doors opened. Where the store would concentrate its defence. What it would do about price. Which of three proposals on her desk would get the money. And in what order the work would happen, with her father and the lender both wanting the plan defended in numbers.

## The Protagonist

Tessa Granger, 44, had run Granger Hardware & Outdoor since 2019. Her father, Dale Granger, had opened the store twenty-six years earlier and still came in most Saturdays to work the counter. She had spent four years behind that counter before taking over buying, and knew by name most of the contractors who pulled in before seven. She drew a modest salary; what the store produced beyond it was the family's income, and the loan was secured against the building. Her father had built it by never being the cheapest and never being out of the part nobody else carried.

## The Decision

Granger had to commit, before MegaBuild opened, to a competitive position she could hold and could pay for. The capital was capped at US$300,000 and the three proposals together came to more than twice that. The nine months were not a planning window; whatever she chose had to be operating by month nine, not begun. Her father would want to hear it first. The lender would want to hear it in break-even terms.

## The Company and its Setting

Granger Hardware & Outdoor did US$3.6 million in annual revenue at a 38% gross margin and threw off roughly US$240,000 of owner cash flow after Tessa Granger's salary. The revenue split three ways (see **Exhibit 1**). Contractors were 45% of sales, about US$1.62 million — trade accounts, thirty-day credit, early-morning pickups, and a standing habit of calling the store when a job stalled for want of one fitting. Ranch and farm customers were 30%, about US$1.08 million, loyal to particular staff and particular brands. Weekend retail walk-ins were 25%, about US$0.9 million, and bought the same commodity items MegaBuild would price 8% to 15% lower.

The store carried eleven staff, a yard, and one ageing delivery van. It had no dedicated contractor counter; contractors queued with everyone else.

## The Situation

Granger kept a one-page scorecard, four readings, updated monthly. The first she called defensibility: how much of the store's position rested on something a chain twelve miles away could not simply copy — on a 0-to-100 scale it stood at 20. The second was contractor retention, the share of contractor spending the store held from one year to the next, currently 70%; below 85% the segment's economics stopped working. The third was cash strength — margin, break-even headroom and debt service taken together, again on a 0-to-100 scale, at 55. The fourth was simply how much of the US$300,000 she had committed, which on May 4 was nothing.

If Granger changed nothing, the arithmetic was undramatic. The 38% margin would hold as long as the volume did. The walk-in quarter of the business bought exactly what MegaBuild would advertise cheaper. The contractors — the largest share of the ledger — would have somewhere new to open an account in nine months, and no new reason from Granger to keep the one they had.

Her instinct, stated aloud to her bookkeeper on the second day, was to cut prices. The bookkeeper ran it: a blanket 10% cut across the commodity lines took about US$130,000 out of gross profit in a full year — more than half of what the business paid the family. It also assumed MegaBuild would not respond, which nobody in the room believed.

The three proposals had sat on the desk since the previous autumn (see **Exhibit 2**). The pro-desk package would build a dedicated contractor counter, put two delivery trucks on the road and formalise trade-credit accounts, for US$140,000. The renovation would rebuild the sales floor and broaden the assortment for US$120,000. The second store, in Brigham City, needed US$420,000 to build out — more than she had — and would add roughly US$180,000 a year of fixed overhead. A partial build at US$300,000 was possible; the contractor who priced it would not say what that opened with.

Dale Granger's view, given once and not repeated, was that the family had survived two recessions by owning a second location in neither. The lender's analyst had asked only one question in their last call: what Granger's break-even would be after the plan, and whether each borrowed dollar cleared 9.5%.

## What Granger Controlled

Four decisions, in sequence, before month nine.

**Where to concentrate.** She could concentrate the defence on the contractor segment, on the ranch and farm segment, on the weekend retail walk-ins, or spread it evenly across all three and defend the whole store at once. Alongside that she had to name what the store's durable advantage actually was: low price — the cheapest in town if the store tried; selection — out-stocking MegaBuild on breadth of inventory; service and the cost of switching — accounts, credit, the odd part, twenty-six years; or none — no local advantage surviving MegaBuild's arrival. She also had to say, as a number, how honestly her team was willing to name the aisles MegaBuild would simply win.

**What to do about price.** Four postures were open: cut roughly 10% across the commodity lines to meet MegaBuild's shelf price; raise prices across the board and spend the difference on service; hold every price where it was; or match price on about ten visible, widely known items and hold price everywhere else. On the service-differentiated lines, where Granger had some pricing power, she could discount them alongside the rest, leave them untouched, or add to them — bundling delivery and expertise into the price and raising it modestly on urgent items MegaBuild barely stocked. She had up to US$40,000 for visible price-match signage on a handful of items, and had to state how firmly she would refuse to follow MegaBuild down on commodity price.

**Where the money went.** The US$300,000 had to be divided across the pro-desk, the renovation and the second store, in any proportion. Anything above US$300,000 drew further on the 9.5% line. She also had to state how strictly she would fund only what one owner could personally run well.

**Sequence and defence.** She could hold steady through the nine months and respond once MegaBuild had opened and she could see what it did; spread the work evenly across the nine months; or put the pro-desk and the pricing changes first and finish the rest afterwards. To the lender she could argue from size, that a bigger business was a safer one; present the headline revenue upside the plan would add; or show that each borrowed dollar cleared 9.5% and name the break-even. And she had to commit to what she would be measured on: top-line revenue and store count; gross margin, contractor retention, local market share and return on invested capital; or no formal metrics at all. Last, a number for how firmly she would compete where MegaBuild was weakest rather than on price.

## Closing

At six on the evening of May 4, Granger locked the front and wrote the three proposal totals on the back of a delivery ticket: 140, 120, 420. Under them she wrote 300, and under that, 9. Her father was coming in on Saturday. The lender wanted the plan inside two weeks.
