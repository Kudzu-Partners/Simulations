# OpenSavoir Foundation: Thirty Days of Openness

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1964 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1964 — *The Open Collaboration* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday 4 May 2026 Marta Zielińska opened the edit stream for Savoir's 1,200 most politically sensitive articles and found seventy-two hours of work she could not account for. Since Friday evening, automated and paid accounts had made 41,000 edits across those pages — subtly false claims, fabricated citations, framing bent a few degrees at a time, written to survive the glance a tired volunteer gave a change at midnight. The quality index the community kept on those articles, a 0–100 score built from citation checks, review status and reader-reported errors, had fallen from 82 to 61. By the time she reached the foundation's office in Warsaw, a national newspaper had published the headline *"The encyclopedia anyone can poison."*

Zielińska had until 3 June. On that date the foundation's two institutional grantors expected a credible integrity plan, and the board expected the quality index on the targeted pages back above 78 out of 100. In the thirty days between, she had to decide how much of Savoir's openness to suspend, who would hold the authority to suspend it, and how to buy reviewing attention from people the foundation was not allowed to pay.

## The Protagonist

Marta Zielińska, 44, had been Director of Community and Trust at the OpenSavoir Foundation since 2019. She had arrived at Savoir in 2011 as an unpaid contributor, spent four years as an elected steward on the Polish edition, and joined the paid staff in 2016 arguing what later became policy: the foundation built the tools, the community wrote the rules. On Sunday night the board's executive committee had made her chair of the crisis team — five people from trust and safety, contributor relations, product and the grants office — and released a €600,000 emergency response budget to her. Her standing with the 1,900 contributors who held elevated permissions rested on never having overruled them.

## The Decision

Zielińska had to decide what the foundation would impose, what it would ask, and what it would buy — and to keep deciding as the thirty days ran.

Savoir's software offered four settings on the same dial. She could protect all 1,200 targeted articles, so that no edit appeared on them without prior approval. She could apply pending-changes review to those pages alone, so that edits from new and unregistered accounts waited for a reviewer before becoming visible, and pair it with a requirement that contested claims carry a verifiable citation. She could throttle the edit rate of newly created accounts across the whole site and change nothing else. Or she could leave the dial where it was and let the community's own patrollers work as they had been working since Friday.

Independently of the setting, she had to decide who held the authority for it. The foundation could issue the measures as an emergency directive under the terms of use, which it was entitled to do. The elected steward committees could impose them through the community's expedited consensus process. Or the two could be split, the foundation drafting and building, the committees ratifying and owning. She also had to decide whether the measures carried a published expiry date and a scheduled community review, or stayed in force until the crisis was judged over.

The €600,000 had three destinations and she set the split. Anti-abuse tooling, including a behavioural classifier that scored edits for coordination signals and cost €220,000 to deploy; contractor moderators hired for the emergency; community support, recognition and a review drive. If she deployed the classifier she had to say how it was wired: reverting on its own judgement everything it flagged, flagging and ranking suspect edits for a human to decide, or not running at all. She had to say what the review drive rewarded — a leaderboard ranked by number of edits, recognition for verified-quality reviews on the hardest articles, or no scheme, with the 1,900 reviewers asked to carry it. And whether to fast-track outside expert reviewers from universities and library associations, or keep the response inside the existing community.

On 3 June there were three more. What went to the grantors: a complete integrity plan on the deadline, a short note after it, or nothing while the technical work continued. What she told the board chair, who had already sent her his question in writing — *did you protect quality by killing the openness that makes Savoir worth protecting?* She could name what the moderation had cost in friction and what it had preserved, argue that safety had been the right purchase, or hold that openness had cost nothing. And what she left behind: a community-owned playbook for the next surge, a permanent version of the emergency measures, or nothing written down.

## The Company and its Setting

The OpenSavoir Foundation, founded in Warsaw in 2009, ran Savoir, a free multilingual open encyclopedia and one of the twenty most-visited reference sites in Europe. Savoir held 3.1 million articles across 14 language editions and served 210 million readers a month. Every word was written and curated by volunteers. Of the 48,000 contributors who edited at least once a month, about 1,900 held the elevated permissions — administrator, reviewer, steward — that allowed them to approve, revert or protect (see **Exhibit 1**).

The foundation was deliberately small: 90 paid staff and a €24 million annual operating budget, funded almost entirely by small reader donations. Staff built software and ran infrastructure and wrote almost nothing. Editorial policy came from the community, through consensus, elected committees and a thicket of rules the volunteers had written themselves. The foundation could build tools and set terms of use; it could not decree content.

## The Situation

The 1,200 targeted pages drew about 4,000 good-faith edits in a normal week, and the review queue had already been under strain before Friday: the backlog stood at 14 days and was lengthening, because 1,900 people working unpaid evenings could not read 41,000 changes. Active contributors were down 6% on the year, and the community's own estimate put one experienced reviewer at roughly 40 quality edits a month. Burnout threads had appeared on the forums over the weekend, and the internal reading of community trust and morale, kept by Zielińska's team from forum sentiment and reviewer activity, had moved to "strained" (see **Exhibit 2**).

Nothing had contained the attack. None of the 41,000 abusive edits had been reverted or neutralised at scale, and the index had not begun to recover. If it stood below 78 on 3 June, the newspaper's story would have a second act, the two grantors — €3.8 million, about 16% of the budget, their confidence recorded internally as "at risk" — would freeze disbursement, and the board would consider imposing editorial control from the top. The national election was six weeks away.

Inside the foundation the argument had already hardened. One group wanted the 1,200 pages closed by Monday evening and reopened when it was over. Another held that any measure the foundation imposed on the community was the real threat, and that the volunteers had beaten worse.

## What Zielińska Controlled

She controlled the moderation setting, the authority behind it, the expiry attached to it, the split of the €600,000, the wiring of the classifier, the recognition scheme, the outside reviewers, and on day 30 the grantor submission, the answer to the board and the standing design. Two commitments were numbers rather than choices: how much reviewer capacity above the normal weekly rate she undertook to mobilise in the first week, as a percentage, and how firmly she held — also as a percentage — that the lightest proportionate measure, run through the community, beat control imposed from above.

Six things were measured. The quality index on the targeted pages. The share of the 41,000 abusive edits reverted or neutralised. The number of active contributors. The review backlog in days. Community trust and morale. Grantor confidence.

## Closing

At half past eight Zielińska wrote the four moderation settings on the whiteboard and, beside them, the three names that could sign each one. The crisis team was due at nine. The backlog was fourteen days, the index was 61, and she had thirty days and €600,000 and 1,900 volunteers who had already been up all weekend.
