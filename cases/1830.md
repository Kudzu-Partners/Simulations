# Training That Sticks: The Advisory Pivot at Banque Aurore

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1830 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1830 — *Training That Sticks* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 12 March 2026, the board of Banque Aurore SA approved the "Advisory Pivot" — a mandate to convert 2,400 frontline branch staff from transaction processors into advisory relationship managers within twelve months. The mandate landed on the desk of Anne-Claire Duval, the bank's HR and learning-and-development manager, alongside the results of a pilot run in twenty branches that had just gone badly: staff had attended a workshop, returned to their branches, and changed nothing they did with customers.

Duval had three weeks to present a full rollout design to the executive sponsor. The first wave of six hundred staff would begin training in sixty days regardless of what she decided, and the board had scheduled a review of behavior-change evidence for month six. Whatever she designed had to convert measurable behavior in 2,400 people, on a budget smaller than the one that had just failed.

## The Protagonist

Anne-Claire Duval, 41, had run Banque Aurore's learning-and-development function for two years, arriving after eight years managing a cluster of branches in Alsace. She had inherited, not designed, the previous year's training program — the one that had just cost the bank EUR 3.1 million and changed nothing. The executive sponsor now expecting a different result was the same person who had approved that program's budget. Duval's standing at the bank rested substantially on whether the advisory pivot, the strategic bet Banque Aurore had staked its future on, actually reached the counter.

## The Decision

Round by round, Duval had to decide what the pilot's numbers actually proved, how the new training would be built and delivered, how its budget would be split, and how Banque Aurore would prove — to a board that had already been shown one set of comforting numbers that meant nothing — that this rollout was different. Every choice competed against a fixed per-learner budget and a fixed clock.

## The Company and its Setting

Banque Aurore SA was a regional retail bank headquartered in Strasbourg, with 280 branches spread across eastern France, roughly 6,800 employees, and EUR 720 million in net banking income. Its cost-to-income ratio stood at 64%, high for the sector, and the board wanted it below 58%. Branch transaction volumes had been falling for years as customers shifted to mobile banking, and the board had staked the bank's future on turning branch staff into advisory relationship managers who sold savings, insurance and credit advice instead of processing transactions. That bet lived or died on whether the training reached the branch floor (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The pilot that preceded Duval's assignment had run in twenty branches and looked, on its own terms, like a success. Eighty-two percent of participants completed it, and on the post-course survey they rated their enjoyment of the sessions 3.9 out of 5 — a score of 39 on the 0-to-100 scale the bank now used to track it. Behind those numbers sat a different picture. On-the-job observation found that only 11% of attendees had actually changed how they talked to customers. Staff rated the training's usefulness to their actual job at 2.4 out of 5 (24 on the same scale), and its trainers' credibility at 2.6 out of 5. Only 9% of branch managers had held any follow-up conversation with their staff about applying what they had learned (see Exhibit 2). The prior program had cost EUR 3.1 million in total (see Exhibit 1).

For the new rollout of 2,400 staff, the board had capped the budget at EUR 1.9 million — EUR 792 per learner, well below the EUR 1,290 per learner the failed program had spent. The new rollout carried two explicit targets: a perceived-usefulness score of 70 or higher on the 0-to-100 scale, and a transfer rate — the share of staff observed changing their behavior on the job — of 60% or higher. Duval had three weeks until her presentation to the executive sponsor. The first wave of 600 staff would start in sixty days. The board would review evidence of behavior change at month six.

If Duval changed nothing about how the bank measured or designed training, the arithmetic pointed to a rerun of the pilot at larger scale: comfortable completion and enjoyment numbers, a transfer rate still near 11%, and a cost-to-income ratio still climbing past 64% while 2,400 people kept counting cash instead of selling advisory products.

## What Duval Controlled

Duval first had to decide what the pilot's numbers actually proved. Four readings were on the table: that weak perceived usefulness and weak trainer credibility were behind it; that completion itself had been too low and needed harder pushing; that enjoyment had been too low and the sessions needed to be more fun; or that frontline staff were simply resistant to change. Alongside that diagnosis she had to name the one metric leadership should track going forward — on-the-job behavior change paired with perceived usefulness, the completion rate, the survey's enjoyment average, or the total hours of training delivered across the network — and state, as a percentage, how candidly her team was prepared to call this a transfer problem rather than a coverage problem.

Once diagnosed, Duval had to design the content itself. She could commission material tailored to Banque Aurore's own customer scenarios and products, at EUR 320 per learner; buy a generic course lightly localized with a handful of Aurore examples, at EUR 210 per learner; or purchase the off-the-shelf advisory course exactly as sold, at EUR 180 per learner. Separately, she had to decide whether to segment that content by branch type and staff seniority for an additional EUR 60 per learner, or run one identical course for all 2,400 staff at no additional cost — and how much to spend, between zero and EUR 160 per learner, on role-play and practice using the bank's real products.

Delivery came next. Duval could staff the rollout with external expert trainers from a top consultancy, at a very high cost; with Banque Aurore's own advisor-coaches, trained internally at a moderate cost; with a blend of internal coaches and e-learning delivered with spaced practice; or with e-learning and spaced practice alone, priced lowest of the four. Whichever she chose, she then had to split what remained of the EUR 792-per-learner cap across three buckets: content production and materials, the trainer or delivery format itself, and briefing the bank's 280 branch managers so they could coach and follow up with their staff after the sessions ended.

Finally, Duval had to decide how Banque Aurore would prove the rollout had worked, choosing among a post-course reaction survey alone; that survey paired with an end-of-course knowledge test; the survey paired with direct observation of on-the-job behavior; or all of that plus a measure of the advisory revenue the converted staff actually generated. And when, at the month-six checkpoint, completion came in at 88%, enjoyment at 4.1 out of 5, observed behavior change at only 31%, and advisory revenue flat, she had to choose her adjustment: re-run the same course for the remaining population, unchanged; redirect the recovery budget into manager coaching and follow-up instead; put the remaining budget into making the sessions more enjoyable; or diagnose which of content relevance, trainer credibility, or reinforcement was weakest, and correct that one. Whatever she chose, she had to state, as a percentage, how firmly she would hold her design against a sponsor who kept pointing at the cheaper e-learning option.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 12 March, Duval had the pilot's numbers in front of her and three weeks before she had to walk into the executive sponsor's office with a design. Six hundred staff would start training in sixty days regardless of what she decided. At month six, the board would ask for evidence of changed behavior — not for enjoyment scores.
