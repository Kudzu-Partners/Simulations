# Caldorra: The First Royalties

On the morning of Monday, 4 October 2027, Elena Saavedra opened the Treasury's account statement and found the line she had been waiting eighteen months to see: the first royalty payment from Caldorra's offshore gas fields had landed overnight. Beneath the statement on her desk sat two letters. One, from the association of cocoa, sugar and garment exporters, asked the government to keep the new money out of the domestic economy. The other, a petition with signatures from every province, asked the government to put it straight into citizens' hands.

Saavedra, as Minister of Finance and chair of the Resource Management Council, had until Thursday's Cabinet meeting to present the framework for dividing the royalties. After that, she would have to approve the allocation again at the close of every year of production, fifteen years in all, defending each year's choice in front of the same Cabinet.

## The Protagonist

Elena Saavedra, 52, had spent most of her career at Caldorra's Central Bank, where she ran the reserves desk through two currency crises before moving to the Treasury as deputy minister. She had been appointed minister in 2026, shortly after the Council was created by Cabinet decree. Colleagues described her as cautious with numbers and impatient with speeches. She was not a member of any party, which meant she had no constituency of her own and served at the pleasure of a President who would face voters several times before the gas ran down.

## The Decision

Saavedra had to decide how each year's royalty would be divided among three uses, and under which standing rules the money would be managed, knowing that every division would be revisited the following year under new prices and new pressure.

The three uses were a sovereign wealth fund, immediate public spending, and a direct cash dividend paid to every citizen. The shares had to add up to the whole royalty: whatever went to one use was not available to the other two. Alongside the split, three governance rules were on the table, each of which the Council could adopt or leave out: an investment mandate for the fund, a fiscal rule for the budget, and constitutional protection for the fund combined with public reporting of its accounts.

The Council's technical staff had circulated a draft framework the week before (see **Exhibit 2**). Saavedra was free to adopt it, amend it, or replace it.

## The Company and its Setting

The Republic of Caldorra was a small coastal economy that had lived for decades on agricultural exports and light manufacturing. Cocoa, sugar and garment assembly employed a large share of the workforce outside the capital and earned most of the country's foreign currency. The gas fields changed the scale of the public accounts at a stroke: the royalty regime negotiated with the foreign operating consortium would pay the Treasury in US dollars, tied to the international gas price.

Production was planned in three phases: a ramp-up as wells came on stream, a plateau at full output, and a decline as the fields depleted. Nobody in the Council expected the price to hold still across fifteen years. The engineers could forecast volumes with some confidence; nobody could forecast the price.

The Council itself had been formed to bring the Treasury, the Central Bank, the Ministry of Energy and the Ministry of Social Development under one roof. In practice its members disagreed. The Ministry of Social Development wanted visible programmes in the first year. The Central Bank's representative wanted the money held abroad. The Ministry of Energy's representative wanted a formula the industry could plan around. Opposition legislators had already announced that any savings rule the Council adopted would be a campaign issue.

## The Situation

On the day the first payment landed, the international gas price stood at US$6.50 per million British thermal units (MMBtu). The sovereign wealth fund existed in law but held US$0.0 billion. No dividend had yet been paid. The Council tracked the condition of the country on a short dashboard (see **Exhibit 1**).

The **royalty inflow** was the dollar amount the Treasury received in a year, which moved with both the gas price and the volume produced. The **sovereign wealth fund** balance was the cumulative value of the fund in billions of US dollars. **Tradable sector health** was an index from 0 to 100 that the Central Bank and the exporters' association compiled on the competitiveness of cocoa, sugar and garment exports; it opened at 100. **Political stability** was an index from 0 to 100 measuring how durable the framework looked to Cabinet, Parliament and the public; it opened at 70. The **per-capita dividend** was the cash, in US dollars, paid to each citizen in a given year. **Intergenerational equity** was the fund's balance expressed as a percentage of all royalties received to date: the share of the gas money that had been kept for later rather than used up. It opened at 0%.

The arguments in the Council ran in every direction. Saving in the fund preserved wealth for the years after the gas, but money in the fund was money no citizen could see, and a large pool of public money had a way of attracting people who wanted to control it. Public spending could build the schools, roads and port works the country lacked, but money spent at home in a hurry pushed up local prices and the currency, and budgets once raised were hard to cut. A dividend reached every household directly and bypassed the ministries, but it too was spent at home, and once paid it was difficult to reduce.

The governance rules divided the Council in the same way. Investing the fund entirely in foreign assets kept the dollars out of the domestic economy and spread the risk; critics replied that Caldorra needed capital at home and that a fund invested only overseas built nothing Caldorrans could see. A structural-balance fiscal rule would draw a formula-set amount from the fund into the budget each year, so that spending did not follow that year's gas price; critics replied that it locked the budget into a formula when a crisis might demand flexibility, and returned money from the fund to the budget whether or not it was needed. Constitutional protection with transparent reporting would put the fund out of reach of any single government; critics replied that it tied the hands of future elected majorities and would take political capital the government might need elsewhere.

If the Council approved the draft unchanged and left it in place, half of every year's royalty would go to the fund, 30% to public spending and 20% to the dividend, with all three rules in force, for fifteen years, whatever happened to prices.

## What Saavedra Controlled

Each year Saavedra set the share of the royalty that went to the sovereign wealth fund, the share that went to immediate public spending, and the share paid out as the direct citizen dividend, with the three shares totalling 100%. Each year she also decided whether the fund would be invested wholly in foreign assets, whether the structural-balance fiscal rule applied, and whether the fund stood under constitutional protection with transparent reporting. She could change any of these choices from one year to the next.

At the end of the fifteenth year the Council would deliver its final assessment: cumulative royalties received, the size of the fund and its share of those royalties, the health of the tradable sectors, political stability, and any funds lost to capture outside the rules.

## Closing

At 7:15 on Monday evening Saavedra laid the two letters side by side on the draft framework and wrote three column headings on a blank page: save, spend, distribute. The Cabinet met on Thursday. The second year's royalty would arrive twelve months later, at whatever price the market set.
