# Isaiah Varnadoe: The First Summer in Business

**Case code:** ENT-2026-2244 · **Associated simulation:** Express 2244 — *Side Hustle Startup* · **v1.0 · October 2026**
**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At nine o'clock on Saturday, 29 May 2027, Isaiah Varnadoe stood at the end of Harold Tisdale's driveway in Largo, Florida, while Tisdale looked at his overgrown lawn and asked what he charged. Varnadoe had no answer. Two other neighbors had asked the same week. He had to decide that same morning what a visit would cost and what he would cut with, because Tisdale wanted the lawn done before his grandchildren came on Monday (see Exhibit 1).

There would be four sets of decisions in his first summer in business: in May the price and the tools; on 28 June how he would find customers and keep the money; on 26 July what to do when there were more customers than hours, and which extra service to sell; and on 23 August how to run the business once school started (see Exhibit 2).

## The Protagonist

Isaiah Varnadoe, 16, had just finished his sophomore year. He had mowed his own family's lawn for three summers and had never been paid for work, sent an invoice or kept a set of books.

## The Decision

The business would start on Monday, 31 May 2027 and run through Sunday, 19 September, in four periods of four weeks. Each decision took effect from the day it was made and held for the rest of the summer. His mother, Vanessa Varnadoe, a respiratory therapist who worked twelve-hour shifts, had told him the business was his: "Your money, your decisions, your mistakes."

## The Company and its Setting

The company was one teenager and whatever he chose to cut with. The Varnadoes lived in Bayhaven Oaks, a subdivision of about 300 homes on small lots of St. Augustine grass, which in a Florida summer needs cutting every week. A visit meant mowing, edging the walks, trimming around the beds and blowing the clippings off the driveway. Adult lawn crews charged about $45 a visit for a small lot and were not taking new small accounts in June.

Varnadoe had $600 saved. He could work about 30 hours a week in June and July; school started on Wednesday, 11 August, and in the school weeks he would have about 12 hours, Saturdays and two afternoons. Each visit cost him $2.00 in gas, oil and trimmer line, plus travel at about $0.12 a minute. Florida's minimum wage was $14 an hour.

The family's old push mower was in the garage. At Gulfline Outdoor Power a salesperson had shown him a new mower, trimmer, blower and edger for $1,480 on his mother's store card, "no interest if paid in full in 12 months". A seller on a local marketplace had a used self-propelled mower, trimmer and blower for $480 and would let him test-start them first. A rental store rented mowers and trimmers by the day.

## The Situation

Most teenagers Varnadoe knew who had tried something like this had done it the same way: charged $15 a lawn so nobody said no, used the family mower, waited for word of mouth, written the money in a notebook, said yes to everyone, kept every lawn on weekday afternoons when school started and offered half when something went wrong. If nothing changed, a summer like that would earn $1,694 of profit for about 266 hours, $6.37 an hour, and end in September with 14 customers, a reputation of 18 out of 100 and records of 45 out of 100.

A friend had already told him the price: "Fifteen bucks. Nobody says no to fifteen bucks."

## What Varnadoe Controlled

On 29 May Varnadoe would choose his price for a weekly visit: by the hour, $16 an hour of mowing time; $15 a lawn, the usual first-timer way, cheap so everybody says yes; $45 a lawn, the same as the pros; or $30 a lawn, worked out from the time a visit takes, its costs and the crews' $45. And his tools: rent a mower and trimmer by the day, about $11 a visit; the new kit at Gulfline, $1,480 on his mother's store card, about 45 minutes a lawn; the used self-propelled kit after a test start, $480 from his savings, about 48 minutes; or the family's old push mower and a hand trimmer, the usual first-timer way, free, about 75 minutes a lawn.

On 28 June, four weeks in, the lawns he had were the lawns that had asked. His mother offered to post on Nextdoor, a printer quoted door hangers, and a friend said to put a flyer in every mailbox in three subdivisions because "everybody does it". Varnadoe would choose how to find customers: word of mouth only, as now, asking each customer to tell a neighbor; a flyer in every mailbox in three subdivisions, $45 of printing; paid social ads across Largo from his mother's account, $180 a period; or door hangers on the streets he already worked, a yard sign at every lawn and his mother's Nextdoor post, $75 a period. And how he would keep the money: a notebook of jobs and payments with everything in his personal account, as now; a payment app on his personal account, with customers tagging payments as friends and family; a spreadsheet of every visit, invoice, expense and mile, a separate account and 25% of each period's profit set aside for tax; or cash in his pocket and payments in his head.

On 26 July the homeowners' association had sent courtesy notices about overgrown lawns and the phone had not stopped: about six more requests that period, more than his hours allowed, with school two weeks away. Customers asked whether he did weed and feed, which the hardware store sold by the bag. His friend Tre Gaskins, 16, wanted in. Varnadoe would choose what to do when demand passed his hours: take Tre on as a partner, Tre doing the extra lawns and keeping 65%; say yes to everyone and work longer, as now; drop the customers farthest from Bayhaven Oaks; or keep a waitlist, give honest dates and charge new customers $35. And the extra service: driveway pressure washing on two weekend days with a rented washer; weed and feed from the hardware store, spread at $14 a lawn; no add-on, lawns only, as now; or hedge trimming and hand-weeding of beds on request.

On 23 August school was in its second week. At 10:40 on Saturday, 21 August, a rock thrown by his trimmer had cracked Bernadette Quisenberry's sliding glass door, and a glass company had quoted $520. Wade Ricketson, owner of Ricketson Turf Pros, had offered to take the lawns Varnadoe could not keep, or to buy his whole customer list. Varnadoe would choose the business in the school weeks: sell the customer list to Ricketson for $300 without asking the customers; keep every lawn on weekday afternoons, the usual first-timer way; a Saturday route with the month invoiced ahead and the other customers handed to Ricketson with a note, at $15 each; or stop, texting every customer on the first day of school. And the cracked door: ask Quisenberry to claim it on her own homeowners insurance; offer half, $260, the usual first-timer way; tell her the same day, pay the $520 and walk every lawn for rocks from then on; or deny it, since rocks fly and she could not prove it was him.

For each four-week period Varnadoe would see his profit in the period, with the tools' cost spread over the summer; his profit per hour, the profit divided by every hour he worked; his weekly customers, the lawns on his route at the end of the period; his reputation, what the neighborhood said about his work, on a scale from 0 to 100; his records and compliance, the books he kept and the rules he kept, on a scale from 0 to 100; the hours worked; and his profit to date. He would receive a round score out of 100 for each period.

## Closing

Tisdale was still waiting. Varnadoe looked at the lawn, then at the garage where the old push mower stood next to his bicycle, and took out his phone to check the marketplace listing one more time.
