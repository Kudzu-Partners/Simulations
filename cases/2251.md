# Eastfield Service Club: Two Thousand Backpacks

**Case code:** MKT-2026-2251 · **Associated simulation:** Express 2251 — *The Fundraiser* · **v1.0 · October 2026**
**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At a quarter past three on Monday, 25 January 2027, Nia Albright stood at the front of Room 114 at Eastfield High School in Columbus, Ohio, with a marker in her hand and an empty whiteboard behind her. Rosa Delgado-Marsh, the director of Brightwater Community Pantry, had just told the Service Club that the pantry's weekend backpack program would run out of money in March. Albright had to decide that same afternoon what goal the club would promise in public and how it would raise the money.

There would be four sets of decisions in the eight-week campaign: in January the goal and the tactic; on 8 February the appeal and how donors would be thanked; on 22 February an offer from a local store and the handling of the cash; and on 8 March the last two weeks and the report to donors (see Exhibit 2).

## The Protagonist

Nia Albright, 17, was a senior and the president of the Eastfield High School Service Club, 24 members strong. She had organized bake sales and volunteer days before, but had never led a campaign with a public goal and a deadline.

## The Decision

The club's advisor, Daniel Weller, a social-studies teacher, asked for a plan before anyone baked anything: a goal, a budget and a tactic. The campaign would end on Sunday, 21 March 2027, and the report to donors was due on Friday, 26 March. Each decision took effect from the day it was made and held for the rest of the campaign.

## The Company and its Setting

Every Friday Brightwater Community Pantry sent a backpack of food home for the weekend with 150 Eastfield students. A backpack cost $6 to fill. To finish the school year the program needed 2,000 more backpacks: $12,000. Gifts to the cause were made to the pantry, which receipted them; money the club collected went through the school's student-activity account, kept by the bookkeeper, Carol Finch.

Every two weeks the club could run bake sales, which drew about 120 buyers at $9; ask people personally, which the pantry estimated would bring about 56 gifts of $35; hold a ticketed event, about 150 tickets at $22 for $1,900 an event; or sell cookie dough through a school-fundraising company, about 90 orders of $40, of which the company kept 60% (see Exhibit 1).

## The Situation

The club's usual plan was simple: no public goal, bake sales, the pantry's statistics on a flyer, thank everyone at the end, cash in the treasurer's locker, the same effort to the last day, and a "we did it!" post with a photo.

If nothing changed, a campaign like that would raise $3,609 and deliver $2,461 to the pantry, 20.5% of the need. Donor trust would end at 49 out of 100.

Around the table, opinions came quickly. One member wanted a $25,000 goal "so people take us seriously". Another wanted last spring's $5,000 "so we can't fail".

## What Albright Controlled

On 25 January Albright would choose the public goal: set last spring's goal of $5,000; set the pantry's real need, $12,000, shown as 2,000 backpacks on a thermometer; announce no public goal and collect what the club could, as now; or set a stretch goal of $25,000. And the tactic: run bake sales after school and at games, as now; sell cookie dough through a school-fundraising company; ask personally, each member writing to about 10 adults with a link to the pantry's giving page; or hold a ticketed event every other weekend, a spaghetti dinner or a trivia night.

On 8 February the first fortnight's gifts were in. A member had a powerful idea: the story of a sophomore on the backpack list, with her yearbook photo, since the counselor had mentioned her name. Another suggested posting every donor's name and amount on the club's Instagram. Albright would choose the appeal: use the pantry's statistics on hunger in Franklin County, as now; tell donors that kids would go hungry that weekend unless they gave today; tell the story of the named sophomore on the backpack list, with her yearbook photo; or tell donors that $6 filled one backpack for one weekend, with a story the pantry had cleared and no names. And the thank-you: send a form thank-you email within a week; post every donor's name and amount on the club's Instagram; thank everyone at the end of the campaign, as now; or write a handwritten card within 48 hours, with the pantry's receipt.

On 22 February Gus Pappas, owner of Dunmore Hardware & Supply, offered to match gifts dollar for dollar up to $2,000 during the next two weeks, or up to $1,000 if the club preferred to use it in the final two. The same week, cash from the sales had been sitting in a member's locker, and another member suggested letting people pay by Venmo to his own account. Albright would choose what to do with the offer: decline it, as now; save it for the final two weeks, up to $1,000; announce it as it was, gifts matched dollar for dollar up to $2,000 until 7 March; or advertise "Every dollar doubled!" without mentioning the limit. And the cash: keep it in the treasurer's locker until someone went to the office, as now; let people pay by Venmo to a member's personal account; have a member take it home to count over the weekend; or have two members count it, fill in a deposit slip and hand it to Ms. Finch the same day.

On 8 March two weeks remained. Ms. Finch reminded the club that the report was due on 26 March and that the district's email list belonged to the district. Albright would choose the last two weeks: keep the same effort as before, as now; email the district's parent list from the office; quietly extend the deadline by a month; or go back to every donor and ask them to help close the last stretch. And the report to donors: a "we did it!" post with a photo, as now; a full report with the gross, the costs, the net to the pantry, the backpacks funded and the pantry's confirmation; count pledges not yet received as raised; or announce the gross as "raised for the pantry".

For each fortnight Mr. Weller would see the donors who gave or bought; the money raised, gross, with the total to date; the costs and losses; the net to the pantry, with the total to date; the cost to raise a dollar, the costs and cash lost for each dollar raised; the average gift; the progress to the need, the net to date as a share of $12,000; and donor trust on a scale from 0 to 100. Albright would receive a round score out of 100 for each fortnight.

## Closing

At half past three Mr. Weller put the lid back on his coffee. "The pantry needs a number by Friday," he said. Albright looked at the empty whiteboard, the pantry's budget sheet and the 24 faces around the room.
