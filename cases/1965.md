# Meridiaan Asset Management: The Ninety-Day Mandate

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1965 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1965 — *Smart or Ethical Money* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 12 January 2026, Sanne Doornbos read the two letters that had arrived overnight and saw that they described the same fund. The first came from the fund-research house whose ratings carried most of Meridiaan's platform distribution; it downgraded the Meridiaan European Equity Fund for three-year underperformance and for "ESG positioning inconsistent with its marketing." The second came from Joost Klerken, chief investment officer of the fund's largest client. Klerken gave her ninety days to deliver a fossil-free strategy classified under Article 9 of the European Union's sustainable finance disclosure rules and, in the same ninety days, to close the fund's gap to its benchmark. Otherwise the pension fund would redeem €640 million and buy an index-plus-ESG product at roughly a quarter of Meridiaan's fee.

Doornbos had been the fund's portfolio manager for twelve days. By 12 April she would have to sit in front of Klerken and the Meridiaan board and say what the fund excluded, what it charged, and whether the answer had earned its keep.

## The Protagonist

Sanne Doornbos, 41, had joined Meridiaan Asset Management in 2019 as deputy manager of the European Equity Fund, after eight years as an equity analyst in London. The board had chosen her in December 2025 over an external hire, on one argument: that an actively managed European book could be screened hard and still beat its index, and that Meridiaan could prove it. Her predecessor had left after the third consecutive year of relative underperformance. Her variable pay moved with three-year relative return and with client retention; the €640 million mandate was the largest single item in the second.

## The Decision

Doornbos had to decide what the fund would refuse to own, what it would tell investors it refused to own, what it would charge for the difference, and how much of its risk budget it would spend trying to close the gap — then defend all four to Klerken and the Meridiaan board on day ninety. The ninety days had four fixed points, on days 1, 30, 60 and 90.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridiaan Asset Management B.V. was an Amsterdam manager with €9.2 billion under management, running equity and multi-asset strategies for Dutch pension funds, insurers, and platform retail investors. Its flagship was the €2.4 billion Meridiaan European Equity Fund: benchmarked to the MSCI Europe index, charging 0.85% a year, and sold for a decade on the phrase "active skill, responsibly applied."

The client base pulled in two directions. The pension money, led by Klerken's fund, wanted stricter exclusions, a fossil-free book and an Article 9 classification — the European designation for products with sustainable investment as their objective, carrying auditable requirements. The platform and insurance money read one line: net return against MSCI Europe. The Dutch supervisor had been examining sustainability claims across the market, and the downgrade had put Meridiaan's factsheet language on the record as inconsistent with its holdings.

## The Situation

The arithmetic was short (see **Exhibit 1**). Over three years the fund had returned 60 basis points a year less than MSCI Europe after fees, and about 25 basis points a year more before them. The 0.85% fee was 85 basis points; 25 minus 85 was the gap, almost exactly. Tracking error stood at 2.1%, low for an active fund. The internal ESG integrity score, a 0-to-100 measure of how far the factsheet's sustainability claim was supported by the holdings, stood at 48. Net flows had been negative for two quarters, at −€180 million in the last one, and the client-service dashboard carried the €640 million mandate as At Risk.

Nadia El Amrani, the responsible-investment analyst, had costed the screening options (see **Exhibit 2**). A full Article 9, fossil-free screen would remove roughly 22% of MSCI Europe by market capitalisation, pushing tracking error toward 4.0–4.8% and leaving a concentrated book. Pieter Sluis, the risk and compliance officer, held a different line: whatever the fund screened, the factsheet had to understate rather than overstate it, because a disclosure the holdings did not support would draw a supervisory inquiry, and the outflows would not stop at one fund.

Bram Osinga's quantitative desk had ranked European sectors and managers by trailing return and wanted the portfolio tilted to the top of that ranking — the fastest route to a positive number was to own what had recently worked. The same desk's back-test showed top-quartile performance persisting weakly, concentrated at the extremes, and reversing often once transaction costs were counted. Osinga read the same back-test as evidence the effect was real at the extremes, and argued ninety days was too short a window to close the gap any other way.

Against that sat the fee. Klerken could buy an index-plus-ESG product at roughly 0.20% a year. Cutting Meridiaan's to about 0.55% would remove roughly €7.2 million of annual revenue at the fund's current size, and the firm had never repriced a flagship.

If nothing changed, the outcome was arithmetic. Gross alpha of 25 basis points against a fee of 85 would keep producing a net gap near 60; flows would keep running out at something like −€180 million a quarter; the €640 million would leave on day ninety; and the board's alternative, converting the flagship to a low-fee passive ESG tracker, would arrive on its own.

## What Doornbos Controlled

At day one she named the dominant driver of the net gap: stock selection, ordinary noise that would revert on its own, the fee, or the cost of the exclusions the fund already ran. She stated what evidential weight the trailing-return rankings carried — strong, none, or weak and reversing after costs — and classified the fund's past good years as demonstrated skill, as unproven until decomposed, or as luck.

At day thirty she set the screen: light exclusions as they stood, a negative screen with an ESG-rating floor, a best-in-class tilt moving weight to higher-rated names within each sector, or the full Article 9 fossil-free book. She set the factsheet claim separately — matched to the holdings, Article 9 regardless of what they supported, or a step below what the book delivered. And an excluded energy name had just risen 18% on an earnings beat: hold the screen, soften it and buy the name back, or drop it across the excluded list.

At day sixty she set four things. A weight from 0 to 100 governing how much of the fund's active risk followed the trailing-return rankings rather than valuation and quality. A tracking-error budget of about 2%, about 3%, or about 5%. The fee: hold at 0.85%, cut to about 0.55%, or raise it on the argument that a fossil-free Article 9 product commanded a premium. And a flows plan: run the book to the investment case alone, carry a liquidity sleeve and a pre-agreed trade-out queue, or fund any redemption from the most liquid holdings first. Strong relative performance would pull in about €250 million; lagging would trigger a €200 million redemption met by selling.

At day ninety she gave the verdict — the realised number for this portfolio in this period, a statement of values held regardless of the number, or a statement that the screen was a cost the fund tolerated for the client. She proposed the Year-2 mandate: active and responsible sized to the evidence, conversion to a low-fee passive ESG tracker, or the full fee with the book held close to the index. She met Klerken's demand for proof of skill with a full attribution, with the fund's best quarter, or with a challenge to the benchmark. And she recorded, from 0 to 100, how firmly she stood behind it.

She was judged on five things: net alpha against MSCI Europe after fees, tracking error, the fund's assets and net flows, the ESG integrity score, and whether the €640 million mandate moved off At Risk.

## Closing

At 8:20 that Monday morning Doornbos wrote four dates on the whiteboard behind her desk — day 1, day 30, day 60, day 90 — and under the first a single question: what is actually wrong with this fund? Klerken's letter lay beside the downgrade. She had ninety days, one portfolio, and a firm that had never had to prove whether its screening cost money or made it.
