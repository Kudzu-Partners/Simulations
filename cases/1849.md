# Bandeira Labs: The Nine-Month Runway

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1849 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1849 — *Startup Inside* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On the morning of Monday, 16 March 2026, Renata Vasconcelos laid three one-page briefs across her desk in São Paulo and read them for the third time before anyone else arrived. Each described a different business Bandeira Labs could fund with its one remaining runway: nine months, one venture, no restart if the choice went wrong. Eduardo Malta, the business-unit liaison who had championed one of the three since January, was already in her inbox asking when he could tell his retail contact that Bandeira was moving. She had to choose which idea to fund within the week, and by the six-month mark she would have to tell the board, in front of the same committee that had watched three ventures fail late, whether the choice had produced a business worth scaling.

## The Protagonist

Renata Vasconcelos, 41, had led Bandeira Labs, the innovation unit of Bandeira Logística S.A., since its creation the previous year. She had spent nine years in Bandeira's core logistics operation before the CEO asked her to run the venture unit, drawn by her record of pilots that either scaled or were shut down inside a quarter, never left to drift. She had not designed the new funding rule the CEO had just imposed on her unit, but she had argued for something like it after watching the previous three ventures grow costlier the longer they went unexamined. The first cohort under the rule was hers to prove out.

## The Decision

Renata had to decide, in stages, which of three pre-screened ideas to fund and on what basis; how to scope an initial test of that idea before building much of it, and what would count as proof; how to read evidence arriving partway through the runway that only partly matched the plan, and what to do about it — keep going, change direction, or stop; and, at the end, what to tell the investment committee about how the runway had been spent.

## The Company and its Setting

Bandeira Logística S.A., founded in 2002 and based in São Paulo, moved roughly 480,000 parcels a day for e-commerce and retail clients across all five Brazilian regions, employed 6,400 people, and had booked R$2.1 billion in revenue the previous year. Its margins were compressing: marketplaces were building their own delivery networks, and discount-driven rivals were undercutting price on routes Bandeira considered its own.

In response, the board had created Bandeira Labs with a ring-fenced annual innovation budget of R$9 million and a mandate to launch new digital businesses adjacent to the core. In its first three years the unit had launched four ventures and shut down three of them late, after each had already scaled past the point where a course correction was cheap — burning roughly R$14 million on products that grew before anyone had confirmed customers wanted them (Exhibit 1). The CEO's response was the rule Renata now worked under: no venture would receive growth capital without evidence that demand was real, every venture would run on a fixed runway, and every venture would face a forced decision point at a fixed date rather than being allowed to drift.

## The Situation

The three ideas were not equally easy to place. RotaSmart was an AI-driven route-optimisation service sold to other mid-size carriers — a large addressable market, but one in which the buyers were Bandeira's own competitors, the enterprise sales cycle ran nine to twelve months, and the build was expensive. EntregaFlex was a same-day micro-fulfilment service for small e-commerce sellers, closest to Bandeira's existing operations and fastest to market, in a market with thin margins and entrenched incumbents; it was also the idea Eduardo Malta was pushing, on the strength of one retail client who had verbally expressed interest. DevolveJá was a reverse-logistics platform handling returns for retailers — a painful, underserved problem fitting naturally alongside Bandeira's existing routes, though nobody yet knew whether retailers would pay for it, and it would require integrating with each retailer's own systems (Exhibit 2).

Whichever idea she chose, the unit's full team would burn cash at roughly R$1.0 million a month against the R$9 million budget, leaving nine months before the money ran out. A disciplined test of demand was budgeted at R$0.9 million to R$1.6 million; building a fuller version of the product before that evidence existed would cost R$4 million or more and consume much of the runway on its own. To earn a follow-on internal round of R$25 million, the venture would need, by the six-month mark, at least 35% of interviewed customers to commit to a paid pilot, evidence that early users kept using the product, and a plausible account of how the business would make money. If Renata simply funded whichever idea carried the most momentum, spent the runway building rather than testing, and read encouraging noise as proof, the venture would arrive at month six the way its three predecessors had: expensive, unvalidated, and out of both time and cash.

## What Renata Controlled

For the idea itself, Renata could fund RotaSmart, EntregaFlex, or DevolveJá, grounding the choice in the sponsor's enthusiasm, market size, or a scored comparison of problem severity, strength of evidence, time to validation, strategic fit, and runway fit. She had to name the single assumption she was least sure of — whether engineering could build the product on time, whether there was even a riskiest assumption worth naming, or whether customers would actually pay — and she set a personal dial on how far evidence, rather than enthusiasm, drove the decision.

For the initial test, she could build a full working version for the sponsor to demonstrate, put up a landing page and waitlist, or run the service manually for about twenty customers to see whether they would pay. She had to decide whether success meant sign-ups and an enthusiastic demo reaction, more verbal interest from the sponsor's contacts, or signed paid commitments with a credible path to profitability; she committed a specific sum, in reais, to the test; and chose whether to test several hypotheses at once or concentrate on the single riskiest one.

When results arrived around the midpoint of the runway, she had to decide how to read a data set telling two different stories — treating a wave of sign-ups and a well-received demo as proof, pushing ahead as though the plan had not changed, or reading the results segment by segment for a signal an average might hide. She then had to choose whether to keep scaling toward the R$25 million round, change the target segment while cash remained, or return the unspent capital and close the venture; she set a dial for how much weight money already spent should carry, and decided how many months of remaining runway to set aside before the next move.

Finally, before the investment committee, she had to settle on a closing plan — asking for the full R$25 million regardless of the evidence, asking for three more months to make the same case again, or matching the ask to what the evidence supported — and decide what to put in front of the committee: a polished demonstration and a sign-up count, the sponsor's enthusiasm and the client's verbal interest, or the validated numbers themselves. When a senior executive pushed her, in that meeting, to skip further testing and scale immediately, she had to choose whether to go along with it, split the difference between scaling and testing, or hold her ground with a cheap way to check first — and decide, on a scale she set herself, how firmly to make that case.

Across all four stages, Renata was measured on four things: how much of what she believed about demand had actually been confirmed, how close the venture had come to enough paying customers committing to a pilot, how many of the nine months remained before the fixed decision date, and how much of the nine million reais remained uncommitted, with no possibility of topping it up.

## Closing

By nine that morning, Renata had three one-page briefs on her desk, an unanswered message from Eduardo Malta, R$9 million and nine months to spend it, and a committee six months out that would want to know how the time and the money had been used.
