# Lumiform S.A.: The Discount Before the Annual Meeting

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1997 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1997 — *Liquidity Matters* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 11 May 2026, an institutional fund with a record of public campaigns published a nine-page note on Lumiform S.A. Its argument was arithmetic. Lumiform's shares changed hands at 7.2 times enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA); its competitors changed hands at 9.0 times. The note put the gap at roughly 20 per cent and attributed it not to the plants but to the difficulty and cost of buying the shares at all, and to a structure that gave the founding family more votes than capital. It asked to meet the company before the annual general meeting, ten weeks away.

Hélène Bastide, Lumiform's chief financial officer, had the note on her screen in Lyon before the market opened. By midday she also had a call from the registrar: a holder linked to the founding family had signalled it might sell 9 per cent of the shares — about €39 million at the prevailing price — and wanted an answer within the quarter. Bastide had ten weeks to decide what Lumiform would change, in what order, and what she would tell the board, the family and the fund it was worth.

## The Protagonist

Bastide, 46, had been chief financial officer since 2022. She joined in 2014 as group treasurer and had spent her time as CFO on working capital and the Poland refinancing. Her long-term incentive was denominated in Lumiform shares. She had raised the thinness of the share register with the board twice since 2023 and had been asked both times to keep the meeting on margin.

## The Decision

Four things had to be settled before the annual general meeting, each narrowing the next: what was holding the multiple down; what to change about the listing and the governance structure; what to do about investors and the 9 per cent block; and what to commit to in public. The block holder wanted an answer inside the quarter. A weak answer, the note said, would be taken to other shareholders.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumiform S.A., founded in Lyon in 1991, made specialty materials for industrial customers: €480 million of annual revenue, €76 million of EBITDA — about 16 per cent of revenue — and roughly 2,300 employees across plants in France, Spain and Poland. It was listed on Euronext Paris, on the standard segment.

The register had three parts: the founding family held 28 per cent, carrying double voting rights; institutional investors held 31 per cent; a long tail of retail holders the rest. Lumiform had no investor-relations function; the register was administered inside the finance department. Sell-side coverage had fallen to a single analyst, whose model was the only published view of Lumiform.

Trading was thin. Average daily volume was €1.1 million and the bid-ask spread averaged 65 basis points, so a fund wanting a position of size had either to accumulate it over weeks or to accept moving the price against itself both ways (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Situation

The fund's numbers were ones Bastide recognised (see **Exhibit 1**); four of them were what her plan would be measured against.

The first was the multiple — what the market paid for each euro of EBITDA. At 7.2 times against a peer group at 9.0, each additional turn was worth about €76 million of enterprise value. The second was the spread: 65 basis points, the cost of getting in and out, which a market-making contract had been indicated to bring towards about 40. The third was the share of the register held by professional investors, 31 per cent. The fourth was money: the 1.8-turn gap stood for roughly €137 million of enterprise value, and a plan could be scored by how much of it came back.

If Lumiform changed nothing the arithmetic was undramatic. The spread would stay at 65 basis points, the register at 31 per cent institutional, one analyst would keep publishing the only model, and 7.2 times would keep being applied to whatever EBITDA the plants produced.

One thing inside the ten weeks was not optional: the block, €39 million of stock against €1.1 million of daily volume, with a holder who wanted resolution inside the quarter. The market was the second risk her broker had put to her: a scenario in which a broad risk-off doubled spreads across the segment and took Lumiform's price down 8 per cent, with the peer group falling alongside it.

## What Bastide Controlled

**Week 0 — the diagnosis.** Three judgements had to be recorded before anything was proposed. Which forces most explained the gap: the operating story and margin, on the view that the numbers were undervalued and better guidance would correct that; the French mid-cap market and the macroeconomic backdrop, on the view that the discount belonged to the tape rather than to Lumiform; or thin trading and the governance structure, on the view that the 65-basis-point spread and the double voting rights kept professional money away. What carried illiquidity into the price: that investors paid less for thin stocks; that thin trading raised the cost of dealing, which kept institutions out, which left the shares without the demand that lifted a multiple; or that there was no mechanism and the gap was noise that would close by itself. And what closing it was worth: nothing in substance, because a multiple was a label; about €25 million; or about €137 million. Bastide also had to record, as a percentage, how firmly she held whichever view she chose.

**Weeks 1–4 — the structure.** The double voting rights could be left intact, wound down on a published timetable agreed with the family, or abolished immediately in favour of one share one vote. The board and the listing could be left as they were on the standard segment; or independent directors could be appointed and the company moved to Euronext's more demanding compartment; or one independent director could be added and announced. A market-making contract with a liquidity provider, indicated to move the spread from 65 towards about 40 basis points, could be signed or not. And she had to set the pace: every lever inside the quarter, including a buyback and a roadshow; two or three in a stated order; or none, with a further year of study.

**Weeks 4–8 — investors, the block and the market.** On the register: leave it alone and let results speak; rely on the liquidity provider and expect cheaper dealing to bring institutions in by itself; or hire an investor-relations head, run a targeted roadshow and rebuild coverage beyond the single analyst. On the block: let it go to the open market; take it onto the balance sheet through a debt-funded buyback; or place it with long-only institutions at a discount agreed in advance. If she placed or bought it, she had to set that discount to the last traded price, anywhere from nothing to 20 per cent; her syndicate desk was split between a deep markdown that re-priced the whole stock and a modest one that might not clear the line. And if the risk-off arrived she could launch an emergency buyback, freeze the programme until the market settled, or continue and explain.

**Weeks 8–10 — the case.** The argument to the board and the fund could rest on earnings, on the tightened spread, or on the whole chain from governance and investor relations through investor demand to the multiple. The family's position could be dismissed as yesterday's problem, answered with a promise of no change, or brought into a coalition around a published sunset and independents it helped choose. And when the fund pressed for a number, she could promise the full 1.8 turns by the next quarter, decline to name a figure or a date, or commit to a stated number of turns with the measures and the horizon attached — with a percentage recording how firmly she held that the gap would close over several quarters rather than overnight.

## Closing

At seven that evening Bastide put the fund's note and the ten-week calendar side by side. The annual general meeting was ten weeks out. The block holder wanted an answer inside the quarter, and the family's representatives had asked to see her first. She had four decisions, €137 million on the page, and 7.2 times.
