# SMS-VV: The Sixty-Three-Day Contract

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1848 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1848 — *Public Value* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 9 March 2026, Renata Bittencourt, the Procurement Officer of the Secretaria Municipal de Saúde de Vale Verde (SMS-VV), learned that the vendor supplying oxygen, respiratory-equipment rental and home-care delivery to 1,900 chronic and post-discharge patients had defaulted mid-contract after its own supplier line collapsed and it could no longer guarantee deliveries. The city's emergency units were forecasting a winter respiratory surge due in 63 days.

Bittencourt had to re-contract the service — a R$22 million, twelve-month agreement — award it and have a replacement vendor mobilised within 49 days so it could ramp up before the surge, and do it in a way that would survive review by the Controladoria and the state Tribunal de Contas (TCE). Two years earlier the same service had gone to the lowest bidder with no service-level clauses and thin monitoring, and on-time delivery had slid to 84 percent before that vendor collapsed too. The mayor had told the secretariat to prove the city's procurement could move fast without losing the ability to answer for itself.

## The Protagonist

Bittencourt, 44, had run SMS-VV's 22-person procurement unit for three years. Before that she had spent nine years on the audit staff of the state's Tribunal de Contas, reviewing the same kind of municipal contracts she now had to write — a background colleagues credited with either her sharpest instincts or her habit of over-engineering every clause. SMS-VV had spent two years on the TCE's watch list after findings over slow, lowest-price awards, and the mayor called this exercise the test of whether procurement reform was real or cosmetic. If the award did not hold up, the explaining would fall to her.

## The Decision

Bittencourt's task broke into four linked choices, and under the transparency mandate every criterion, weighting and clause she wrote would become a public, auditable record.

The first was to name why the original contract had failed, then choose the legal instrument under Brazil's Lei 14.133/2021 to re-let it inside the 63-day clock. She could attribute the failure to the contract's own design, which had combined a lowest-price-only award with no service-level agreements, no monitoring and no exit triggers; to the incumbent's own supply-chain collapse; to an original budget that had simply been too small; or to carelessness among the contract managers who were supposed to check deliveries. Having named a cause, she had to choose an instrument: técnica e preço, a competitive award that weighted technical quality alongside price; pregão, the traditional lowest-price route, which typically took 75 to 90 days; diálogo competitivo, an extended negotiated dialogue with bidders; or dispensa emergencial, skipping competition to sign a single vendor immediately. She also had to decide, on a sliding scale, how explicitly her memo named the tension between speed and accountability.

The second choice was the award itself (Exhibit 2). Three bids were on the table: R$18.6 million from a bidder with a roughly 84 percent on-time track record and no service guarantees; R$21.2 million from a bidder with a roughly 95 percent track record willing to commit to service-level agreements; and R$24.3 million from a bidder offering 24-hour service commitments and telemetry with a roughly 99 percent on-time record. Bittencourt had to publish a weighting splitting 100 percentage points between headline price and technical/quality criteria, put forward one of the three bids, and set a minimum on-time-delivery threshold — between 80 and 99 percent — that any bidder would have to clear. Clinical guidance held that oxygen-dependent patients needed at least 98 percent on-time delivery, and every point below that threshold mapped to roughly 19 of the 1,900 patients placed at risk.

The third choice was how to write the contract's incentives, inside a band capped at plus or minus 8 percent of contract value — about R$1.76 million either way (Exhibit 1). She had to word the on-time-delivery commitment: as an exact, weekly telemetry-verified metric with a defined cure period and monthly audit; as a vendor promise to use "best" or "reasonable efforts" to deliver on time; or as a single annual delivery average reviewed once at year-end. Separately, she set a penalty percentage for a sustained miss and a bonus for beating it, each from zero to eight percent. And she decided whether the contract carried a measurable exit and termination trigger: defined breach thresholds with step-in rights and a pre-qualified backup vendor; a clause letting the city terminate "for cause" at its own discretion; or no exit trigger at all, leaving the twelve-month term to run its course.

The fourth choice covered how the contract would be watched once running. She could combine a telemetry dashboard, costed at roughly R$240,000 a year, with in-house management and a periodic third-party audit; manage the contract in-house with her existing 22 staff, at no added cost; rely on the live telemetry dashboard by itself; or commission an independent third-party audit with no live data feed. Partway through, delivery dipped to 95 percent in the vendor's first week and a losing bidder filed a formal challenge. Bittencourt had to decide whether to trigger the cure period on the telemetry evidence and publish the scored matrix in response, treat the dip as noise and let the legal team handle the challenge quietly, or cancel the award outright and invoke dispensa emergencial before the surge. Finally, she would present the file to a mock TCE and Controladoria panel and argue one of three cases: that the award achieved speed, lower life-cycle cost and accountability together, backed by the published matrix, the incentive math and the oversight log; that awarding the lowest price was the easiest position to defend in an audit; or that the winning vendor was simply of better quality, with no reproducible scoring model behind it. She also stated, as a percentage, how firmly she argued that speed and accountability could be achieved together rather than traded off.

## The Company and its Setting

SMS-VV was the health secretariat of Vale Verde, a municipality of 720,000 residents in the interior of São Paulo state. It ran 34 primary-care units and 4 emergency units on an annual budget of R$540 million, of which about R$95 million was procurement spend, processed by a 22-person unit across roughly 1,100 contracts a year. SMS-VV procured under Brazil's Lei 14.133/2021, the modernised procurement law that expanded instruments beyond lowest-price bidding while keeping audit bodies watching every award.

## The Situation

The numbers Bittencourt was working against were fixed before she made a single decision (Exhibit 1). The contract to be re-let was worth R$22 million over twelve months and covered 1,900 dependent patients. The incumbent's on-time delivery, against a clinical requirement of 98 percent or above, had fallen to 84 percent by the time it collapsed — a gap in which each percentage point corresponded to roughly 19 patients. The award had to be signed and the new vendor mobilised by day 49 so it could ramp before the surge at day 63; the traditional pregão route could run 75 to 90 days on its own, past the surge date. Missing day 63 meant falling back on dispensa emergencial — a legal, fast, single-vendor award that would read, in the next audit cycle, as confirmation that the reform had failed. Every week of delay was estimated to add about R$140,000 in avoidable emergency-unit admissions, and the life-cycle cost of the whole award was tracked against a ceiling of R$28.0 million.

## What Bittencourt Controlled

Bittencourt was tracked on four measures across all four choices: a composite public-value score, 0 to 100, blending agility, cost, quality and accountability; a service-quality score standing in for on-time delivery, with a clinical floor at 98 percent; an accountability score, also 0 to 100, reflecting whether the award would survive a TCE or Controladoria review; and a running count of days used against the 49-day award clock, with the surge fixed at day 63.

## Closing

By the end of that first afternoon, Bittencourt had cleared her desk of the old contract's file and opened a blank evaluation template. Forty-nine days to award, sixty-three to the surge, four decisions to write down — and every one of them due to be published before she signed anything.
