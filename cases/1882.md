# Total Reward: Rebalancing the Pay Mix at Verdeluz Retail Group

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1882 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1882 — *Total Reward* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Tuesday, 20 January 2026, Elena Vidal, Compensation Manager at Verdeluz Retail Group, opened two messages within the hour and recognized them as one problem wearing two faces. The first was a spreadsheet, already forwarded across the warehouse shift schedule, showing that two of the company's distribution centres paid up to 14% differently for people doing identical work; the works council was already calling it grounds for a grievance. The second was a resignation email, the third that fortnight from the data-and-analytics team, citing an outside offer 22% above current base pay.

Vidal had to redesign how Verdeluz rewarded its 3,200 people, and do it before the works council escalated the equity complaint and before the April pay review, the date by which the board expected a finished structure on the table.

## The Protagonist

Vidal, 41, had joined Verdeluz in 2019 as a compensation analyst and been promoted to Compensation Manager in 2023 — the year each store and warehouse had still set pay locally against its own hiring pressure. She had recommended standardising it then; the recommendation went nowhere. Whatever the leaked spreadsheet showed now, it showed the gap she had already flagged once. Her own annual bonus, like every senior manager's, was calculated partly on keeping total payroll inside whatever the board approved each January. This year that number was smaller than the three segments' combined asking price, and she was the one who had to say so out loud.

## The Decision

Vidal had to decide how to read the problem before she could decide what to do about it. It could be named a pay-level issue, solved with one flat raise across the board; a single portfolio of base pay, variable pay and benefits rebalanced segment by segment; the equity scandal alone, fixed warehouse by warehouse while the rest waited; or a market problem, addressed by matching the specialists to the outside offers. Whichever reading she chose, she then had to decide which claim governed the redesign: treating all three as legitimate but only 60% fundable, sequenced deliberately; closing the equity gap first and in full; defending the specialists first since attrition cost the most; or funding everyone's full demand and asking the board to fund the shortfall. A third question sat underneath both: why management's bonus, at 8% of total pay against a sector norm of 18–22%, failed to motivate anyone — because the bonus was tied to goals too vague to separate strong from weak performers, because the 8% figure was too small and needed to rise, or because paying managers more variable pay than frontline staff treated one group's contribution as worth more than the other's. And before acting on any of it, how rigorously to put euro figures behind both the equity gap and the market gap, rather than negotiate from impressions.

## The Company and its Setting

Verdeluz Retail Group ran 140 stores and a growing e-commerce and logistics arm out of Valencia, employing 3,200 people across three segments that wanted different things from the same payroll (see **Exhibit 2**). Frontline retail and warehouse staff — 2,200 of them, hourly and prone to turnover — valued base-pay security and benefits. Specialists and professionals — 760 people in buying, marketing, data and supply chain — were market-priced and the easiest to poach. Managers — 240 store, regional and central leaders — were accountable for results and expected variable pay that reflected them. Revenue stood at €540 million against a 5% net margin, and total annual payroll already ran to €168 million. A single across-the-board raise would have to satisfy all three with one instrument, and none of them wanted the same one.

## The Situation

The board had frozen total payroll growth at 3%, or €5.04 million, for the year — a number set before the spreadsheet leaked and before the resignations began (see **Exhibit 1**). Meeting all three segments' claims in full would cost roughly €8.3 million, meaning the approved envelope covered about 60% of legitimate demand. The warehouse gap ran up to 14% for identical roles. Specialist pay sat roughly 10% below the market median, and the offers pulling people away were running 22% above their current base; each departure cost the business an estimated €48,000 to recruit and ramp back up. Management's variable pay remained at 8% of total compensation, against peers running 18–22%. If none of this changed, the works-council grievance would keep moving toward formal proceedings, the specialists fielding outside offers would keep leaving at roughly €48,000 apiece, and a strong store manager's bonus would keep landing within a few points of a weak one's.

## What Vidal Controlled

The €5.04 million had to be split across the three segments and, within each share, across base pay, variable pay and benefits — the same euro doing a different job depending on which of the three it became. For frontline's share, she could put all of it into a permanent across-the-board base increase, route most of it into variable pay tied to performance, or weight it toward a base correction paired with cost-efficient benefits. For specialists, the share could go mostly to benefits that convert each euro of cost more efficiently, mostly to base pay moved toward the market median with a little variable on top, or to a token increase, on the view that the resignation already on record was a one-off rather than the start of a trend. Management's variable pay could be lifted from its current 8% toward the 18–22% norm, with a separate choice of how much design effort to spend tying it to results a manager could actually see.

The warehouse gap posed a separate question: close it in full immediately at roughly 40% of the whole envelope, defer it with a promise to review it next year while spending on specialists first, or close it credibly now and commit to phasing in the remainder. Reconciling internal equity with the specialists' external market pressure was its own decision — hold the internal ratio and match the market through a premium rule, hold internal equity alone and pay every segment the same band without adjusting for market, or match the external market alone and allow the internal ratio to move. And with the works council, she could impose the design on the reasoning that it was already technically sound, negotiate from the data and hold a benefits concession in reserve to trade, or give the council everything it asked, funding equity in full while the other two segments waited.

Eventually she would have to bring a finished design to the CFO and the works council together, and decide how to pitch it: as the full €8.3 million package with a request that the board find the difference, as a same-total redistribution that moved money around without changing what it motivated, or as a package that reallocated the €5.04 million across the three segments while staying within the 3% cap. She would also decide what scorecard to commit to the board — all four metrics together, budget adherence alone, or no scorecard at all — where the four were the pay-equity ratio, projected specialist retention, management motivation, and perceived fairness, meaning whether the works council itself would see the outcome as legitimate, tracked from a starting position of 30, 25, 20 and 35 out of 100, with the equity number alone carrying a stated target of 75. She would have to decide whether to claim the frozen envelope had covered every segment's full demand, name the deferred claim and commit a funded plan for next year, or stay quiet and not tell the council what had gone unfunded. And, whichever way the numbers landed, how firmly to insist to both audiences that the rebalanced mix had achieved something a flat raise could not was, in the end, also her call.

## Closing

Vidal opened a blank template on her screen: three rows — Frontline & Warehouse, Specialists, Management — and three columns — Base, Variable, Benefits. At the top, one figure was already filled in: an envelope of €5.04 million, committed so far to €0.00. The works council wanted an answer before it wanted a number. So did the three specialists who had not yet signed their counter-offers.
