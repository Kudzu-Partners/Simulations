# Lumen Coatings: Opening the Funnel

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1991 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1991 — *Opening the Funnel* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 January 2026, Anke Verstraeten read the AquaShield BV announcement a third time and carried it down to the laboratory floor, so that the ninety-five people who worked there heard it from her. The Dutch firm had launched a self-cleaning marine hull coating that cut drag and biofouling — claim for claim, the product Lumen Coatings had been building in its own laboratories for three years under the name Tritón, and which was still fourteen months from market. AquaShield had got there first by licensing a core enzyme technology from a university spin-out and co-developing the rest with a Korean shipyard.

Verstraeten had been Head of Innovation for eleven days. She had one budget cycle — this fiscal year and the €19.2 million research envelope that came with it — to put a credible alternative in front of the board, or the board would hand Lumen's research strategy to an outside consultancy.

## The Protagonist

Anke Verstraeten, 46, had joined Lumen Coatings in 2019 to run business development, after nine years at a Rotterdam industrial-gases group, latterly on a licensing desk that bought and sold know-how rather than inventing it. She was appointed on 3 January 2026 over two internal candidates from the laboratory, both of whom now reported to her. The chair's brief ran to one sentence: bring the 38 months to market closer to the industry's 24, without giving away what customers actually paid Lumen for. She had argued for her own appointment on the strength of that Rotterdam desk, had no budget beyond the research envelope, and could not sign a licence without the finance director's counter-signature.

## The Decision

Across the cycle Verstraeten had to decide how much of Lumen's technology crossed the company's boundary, in which direction, on what terms and with whom — beginning with Tritón, on which €8.4 million had gone over three years and a further €3.6 million was budgeted to finish (see **Exhibit 2**). The board would see it all at the end of the fiscal year.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumen Coatings NV, founded in Antwerp in 1981 and still headquartered in the port, made high-performance functional coatings: anti-corrosion, anti-microbial and self-cleaning surface treatments. Revenue was €240 million. Ninety-five scientists and engineers worked in one central research centre, and the €19.2 million spent on research each year — 8% of revenue — went entirely to projects conceived and executed in-house. The register recorded 140 active patents, fewer than 30% of them ever commercialised and roughly 98 sitting unused (see **Exhibit 1**). Lumen earned nothing from licensing any of them out; AquaShield earned €4 million a year from licensing out its own.

## The Situation

Analysts sized the premium self-cleaning marine segment at €22 million of revenue over its first three years and expected the first company to reach it to take 55–60%. AquaShield had reached it in January. Finished as planned, Tritón would launch fourteen months later into what remained — roughly €9 million — after the further €3.6 million.

Nothing else in the portfolio pointed anywhere different. On the current plan the 98 idle patents kept earning nothing while AquaShield collected its €4 million, and the share of Lumen's pipeline value originating outside the company stayed at zero. At the end of the cycle the strategy went to the consultancy.

The spin-out was not uncontested. AquaShield had tabled an exclusive offer to buy it outright, and its advisers wanted positions by the end of the week. If that sale closed the enzyme route closed with it, and so did Lumen's line into anti-microbial medical coatings, where the same chemistry applied.

Verstraeten had put four measures in front of the board, none of them tracked at Lumen before. The share of pipeline value coming from outside the firm stood at 0%, against a target above 30%. Average time from project start to launch stood at 38 months against the industry's 24, lower being better. Value created per euro of research spending was indexed at 1.0× on her first day and had to rise. Net value released during the cycle — what a Tritón rescue and any licensing of idle patents brought in, less whatever leaked away on poor terms — began at €0.0 million. Beside them sat one standing judgement of how exposed Lumen remained to its own closed habits, reported as high, elevated or contained. It read high.

## What Verstraeten Controlled

She had to put on record where the flow of ideas was constricted on the way in: whether ninety-five scientists already generated enough that the entry point was healthy; whether almost nothing external entered at all, there being no scouting, no in-licensing and no crowdsourcing; or whether the occasional university contact was the right model and needed only more money behind it. She had to make the matching judgement on the way out: whether every patent was a defensive asset that should never leave; whether 98 idle ones earning nothing amounted to a missing route out; or whether patents existed to block rivals and their disposal was a matter for the lawyers. And she set how much of her first weeks went into naming publicly the department's settled preference for ideas invented in its own laboratories — from none of it to all of it.

On Tritón she had three routes, set out in **Exhibit 2**: complete it with Lumen's own team and reach market in fourteen months; stop it, write off the €8.4 million and redeploy the team; or licence the spin-out's enzyme for €2.8 million on signature plus 6% of net sales and reach market in eight.

She then had to shape the €19.2 million: keep funding internal projects as before; rebalance it, funding internal strengths while licensing in and out; or move as much as possible outside and cut the internal group. She set what percentage of the envelope stayed on internal projects, from none to all, and chose a lead partner — the spin-out that held the enzyme and that AquaShield was bidding for; the Korean shipyard, with co-development capacity, market access, currency exposure and some risk of knowledge passing on; the accelerator, a continuing flow of early-stage ideas at low cost and little near-term yield; the contract research organisation, capacity for hire without ideas of its own; or no partner this cycle.

Terms followed. The inbound licence could be non-exclusive with no field-of-use clause, quickest and cheapest to sign; exclusive within marine coatings with the field of use carved out, at a higher price; or exclusive across every field, including those Lumen did not work in. Outbound, she could licence nothing; licence three idle peripheral patents to non-competing firms; or licence widely, the anti-corrosion know-how included. She had to draw the boundary itself — everything core; the anti-corrosion core, the rest available; or almost nothing truly core — and name the royalty she would accept on net sales, from 0% to 20% against the 3–8% commonly paid for enabling technology. And she had to answer AquaShield's bid: keep deliberating and return to it next quarter; sign a binding option or co-investment before Friday; or leave the spin-out to AquaShield and find another route.

What remained afterwards was the last set. She could add no new structure and let the existing team absorb the work; stand up a scouting function alone; or stand up a scouting function and an out-licensing desk with named owners and a quarterly cadence. She could commit the board to total revenue alone, to the four measures above, or to patents filed per year. And when the chair asked whether openness had cost Lumen its technical edge, she could answer that nothing had been given away and the technical edge was intact; that Lumen had monetised widely and openness had paid for itself this year; or that the openness had been selective, buying in for speed and selling only what sat idle — and say how firmly she held that.

## Closing

At six that evening Verstraeten drew three columns on her whiteboard: what came in, what went out, and what stayed behind the line. She had €19.2 million and no signature of her own. The spin-out's advisers wanted a position by Friday; the board would see all of it in one meeting eleven months out.
