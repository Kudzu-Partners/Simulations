# Meridian Digital Consulting: Priya's First Week

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1811 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1811 — *The Manager's Dilemma* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 8 June 2026, Priya Nandra opened her inbox as a manager for the first time and found two problems that would not stay in separate boxes. She had been a senior consultant on Meridian Digital Consulting's Aurora team until Friday, when the previous lead resigned abruptly and she was asked to take over, effective Monday. Both problems needed an answer before Thursday at four, when Aurora's client would sit down for a steering meeting Priya was now expected to run.

The first concerned Tom, a nine-year engineer who had quietly missed his delivery commitments for three sprints, his code failing review at over three times the team's normal rate. The second was not a message but a conversation, held in confidence the previous week: Aisha, the team's strongest performer, had accepted a verbal offer elsewhere and asked Priya, for now, to keep it to herself. Rescuing the client-critical module Tom's slippage endangered meant leaning on Aisha — the one person whose likely departure she had just promised to keep quiet.

## The Protagonist

Priya had joined Meridian three years earlier as a mid-level consultant, known for quietly fixing colleagues' escalations. She had never managed anyone. Her promotion was announced by email on Friday and took effect the following Monday, with a leadership course scheduled only after her first client meeting. Meridian promoted almost entirely from inside, and had spent eighteen months printing a values charter — Candour, Care and Courage — on every offer letter and every client pitch. Priya had signed one of those letters herself. A peer manager, passed over for the same role, had already told two colleagues the promotion was "a stretch." Fourteen people now reported to her, all of whom had watched the previous lead manage Tom by looking away from his numbers.

## The Decision

Priya had four things to work out, in order, before Thursday. First, before choosing anything, she had to work out what was going on with both of them, and name, in one sentence, which of the firm's stated values were pulling against each other. Second, she had to reason through both situations and settle what to do about Aisha's request for confidentiality, given any fix for Tom depended on someone who might resign within days. Third, she had to hold two conversations — one with Tom, one shaped by what Aisha had told her — and decide who learned what, in what order, before Thursday. Fourth, she had to present the decision to the HR business partner and the skeptical peer manager, defend it, and say what it meant for how she would lead them.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Digital Consulting Ltd. was a 240-person digital-transformation consultancy headquartered in Manchester, delivering cloud-migration, data and software work to retail, public-sector and financial-services clients on £38 million in revenue at a 12% operating margin. Aurora, the fourteen-person team Priya now led, billed its single largest client £1.9 million a year. Meridian promoted almost entirely from inside, and a first-time lead's team was, by reputation, where trouble showed up first.

## The Situation

Tom's reject rate on code review had run at 31% for three sprints, against a team norm of 9%; the previous manager had covered for him rather than raise it. He was well liked and, informally, the person newer engineers turned to. Moving him off the client-critical module would protect Thursday's deadline but would be the first hard call the team had watched their new lead make. Aisha was the obvious replacement — Meridian's highest-rated consultant on Aurora — but she had told Priya, in confidence, that she expected to resign within days, so the fix for Tom rested on someone who might not be there to see it through, and keeping HR and the project owner informed meant telling them something Priya had promised to keep quiet.

The numbers were not abstract. A missed Thursday milestone triggered a service credit of £95,000 and put a three-year, £5.7 million renewal at risk (Exhibit 1). If Tom left feeling discarded, three other long-serving engineers had already signalled they would "reconsider" their futures, at an estimated £600,000 to replace. Altogether close to £6.4 million rode on the week, on top of a 24% attrition rate running on newly promoted managers' teams. A recent internal read had also put team trust in Priya at 42 out of 100, the team's values-alignment at a neutral 50, confidence that Thursday would hold at 45, and her own standing as a manager at 40 (Exhibit 2). If nothing changed, the milestone stayed at risk, the promotion kept looking like a stretch, and the attrition curve already running above the rest of the firm had no reason to bend back down.

## What Priya Controlled

Her first move was how she started: read both situations first, naming what each person wanted and feared, before picking anything; move fast — take Tom off the module, put Aisha on it, and sort out feelings afterward; let the reject-rate percentage and the credit figure alone decide it; or hand both files to HR and let them decide. Alongside it she had to name the conflict itself: honesty with the team, loyalty to Tom and the nerve to act, all pulling apart — or, on other readings, no values question at all, just a resourcing problem, or a question about Tom alone, not the wider team, or about her own reputation, after being called "a stretch." She also had to decide, on a sliding scale rather than as a yes-or-no, how tightly she treated the two situations as connected, since Tom's fix depended on Aisha staying the week.

Next came the reasoning itself: work through both situations, naming who was affected and which obligations conflicted, then generating more than one response; trust her own read of both and treat a formal process as wasted time before Thursday; or reduce each case — Tom's and Aisha's — to a straight keep-or-replace and take whichever was cheaper. On Aisha's request for confidentiality she had three routes: keep Aisha's offer confidential while telling HR and the project owner the module needed a backup; keep the secret completely, staff Aisha regardless, and tell HR and the project owner nothing; or tell HR and the project owner exactly what Aisha had told her. Beyond that lay how many options she forced herself to generate, and how defensible she judged the result.

The two conversations came next. With Tom she could name the reject-rate facts plainly but with care, acknowledge the previous manager let it slide, and agree a path forward; open with the number and tell him he was off the module; or reassign him quietly, without discussing the reject-rate numbers or the reasoning behind it. She also had to order the disclosure — Aisha first, then HR and the project owner, then the wider team; the team only, leaving the project owner uninformed; or everyone at once in a single message — and how much she regulated her own irritation in the room, and how solid a backup owner she lined up if Aisha resigned on schedule.

Finally, presenting the decision to the HR business partner and the skeptical peer manager, she could defend it on the reasoning she had done and how it protected Thursday; defend it purely on the numbers: the reject rate and the renewal at risk; or defend it by noting she had kept everyone happy, with no confrontations that week. She had to name, out loud, the precedent she had just set — fair and candid under pressure, her title outweighing her reasoning, or no precedent at all — and close with a first-thirty-days commitment that was concrete, vague, or not yet decided. One more choice sat underneath all of it: how firmly she was willing to say the decision had cost something, rather than call it free.

## Closing

It was a few minutes past nine on Monday morning. Priya's diary showed the steering meeting fixed for Thursday at four, three days and seven hours away, with nothing else on the calendar before it that she had not just blocked out for herself. She had not yet opened a blank document for either conversation.
