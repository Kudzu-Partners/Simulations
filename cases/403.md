# CD Benimestral: The Summer of the Squad Cost Limit

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-403 · **Companion simulation:** Express 403 — *El Estadio* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, June 15, 2026, Montserrat Giménez, chief executive of Club Deportivo Benimestral, S.A.D., laid the letter from LaLiga's economic control department on the boardroom table beside the summer agenda. The league had set the club's squad cost limit for the 2026–27 season at €58.2 million. The club's squad cost, wages plus the amortisation of player registrations, stood at €82.1 million. Until the €23.9 million difference was closed, LaLiga would not register a single new player.

The board wanted four sets of decisions from her, in order, before the transfer window closed on Monday, August 31, 2026: what to do about the squad cost, which players to buy, loan and sell during the window, whose name to put on the stadium, and how to answer an American offer for part of the club. Each set would be decided on the position the previous one had left.

## The Protagonist

Giménez was 44. Before joining Benimestral she had spent years in LaLiga's economic control department, reviewing other clubs' budgets against the same limit that now bound her own. She knew how the league read a squad, a sale and a sponsorship contract. She had never had to sell a supporters' favourite to satisfy it.

## The Decision

She had to decide how far, and how, to close a gap of €23.9 million between what the squad cost and what the league allowed, while leaving the head coach a team that could stay in the division. Then she had to decide what the stadium's name and a stake in the club were worth, and to whom she owed what she knew about the majority shareholder.

## The Company and its Setting

Benimestral, founded in 1922, played at the Estadio Mestral, 22,500 seats in a provincial capital on Spain's Mediterranean coast, filled to 71% on an average matchday. In 2024 the club returned to the top division after eighteen years below it, and it finished its first season back fourteenth, four points clear of relegation. Revenue in the last audited year was €87.3 million, 44% of it from the league's television distribution and 17% from commercial income (see Exhibit 1). Wages took 72.7% of revenue, above the 70% that the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) treated as the sustainable ceiling. Earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) were a loss of €11.1 million, and net debt was €44.2 million. The club began the summer with no free cash.

Grupo Marjal Inversiones (GMI), a regional real estate group, held 61% of the shares. The Fundación CD Benimestral, the supporters' historic entity, held 18%. Florent Marchand, a French private equity investor, had bought 14% in 2023. Shareholder-members, the socios, held the remaining 7%.

## The Situation

Head coach Tomasz Krawczyk, in his third season, wanted a quality striker and had said he would resign without one.

The club's most saleable asset was Rodrigo Fonseca, a 24-year-old Portuguese attacking midfielder who had scored 12 goals and made 9 assists. Benimestral had paid €8 million for him in 2024; after two years of amortisation his registration stood in the books at €4 million. Three clubs had bid: Ravensholt City €42 million plus €5 million in add-ons, Olympique Valmorin €35 million, and Unión Deportiva de Brezales €30 million. Two fringe players could also be released, each saving €1.6 million a year in wages at a severance cost of about €0.8 million.

The board had tied the window budget to Fonseca. If he was sold, €12 million would be available for incoming players after reserves and debt service; if he stayed, €2.5 million. Whatever the club received for Javi Soria, a 20-year-old winger from the academy, could be added to it.

If Giménez changed nothing, the arithmetic would not change either. Squad cost would stay at €82.1 million against a €58.2 million limit, no new signing could be registered, the window budget would be €2.5 million, and the club would carry its €11.1 million operating loss into another season.

Two later decisions were already on the table. Velarta Bank had offered €2.1 million a year for seven years to rename the stadium, on condition of a €3.2 million digital renovation; Cerámicas Teselia offered €1.4 million a year for five years with no works required. A 1998 club statute required 60% shareholder approval for changes to the club's identity. Marchand supported Velarta. The Fundación and the socios opposed it. GMI had not decided, and its representatives had linked their vote to the second matter.

Whitcombe Sports Group, owner of the US club FC Valtera, had offered USD 28 million for 20% of Benimestral, a valuation of about €130 million. Two recent Spanish deals bracketed the number: Real Unión Olmedal had sold 35% for €45 million, and CD Juncaral 20% for €16 million.

Giménez also carried a fact that was not public. GMI's parent had €180 million of real estate loans falling due in 2027. If they could not be refinanced, GMI might have to sell its stake in the club at a distressed price.

## What Giménez Controlled

Before the window opened she settled two things. Fonseca: sold to Ravensholt City, to Valmorin or to Brezales, or kept. The fringe players: two released, one released, or both kept.

During the window she settled three more (see Exhibit 2). Lucas Petit, a 29-year-old French centre-back whose loan from Stade Brivaux was expiring and who had anchored the defence: bought outright for €9 million, kept on a one-year loan extension for a €2.1 million fee, or released. The striker: Mateus Barros, 22, from Itapiraná, for €14 million; Karim El Fassi, 27, from Guadalcrespo B, for €4.5 million; or no striker. And Soria: sold to Montclaret B for €3.5 million, or kept and developed. The club's own estimate put the value of keeping him at €6.5 million in present value, on a 60% chance that he would be worth €13.5 million in two years.

At the stadium she chose Velarta, Teselia or neither, and how to take the decision through the shareholders: force a vote with GMI and Marchand, who together held 75%; negotiate with the Fundación for consensus; or delay until the ownership question was settled.

On ownership she chose among three answers to Whitcombe. In a minority deal, Whitcombe would buy Marchand's 14% plus 6% in new shares, bringing about €14.8 million of new money into the club along with a player pathway, pre-season tours in Texas and access to the US commercial market. In a sale of control, GMI would sell 51% to Whitcombe and leave. Or she could refuse and look for league-arranged or other financing. Alongside it she decided what to do with what she knew about GMI: disclose it to all shareholders, close the Whitcombe deal quickly and quietly before the problem became public, or tell the Fundación privately about its right of first refusal on any GMI sale.

The board would follow six figures through the summer: squad cost, the cost limit, the gap between them (labelled the FFP gap, for financial fair play), cash available, EBIT, and wages as a share of revenue. After each set of decisions it recorded the key decision, its net financial effect in € million, the remaining gap and the cash position. The board's summer review would weigh three counts, the club's finances, its strategic position and the integrity of its governance, into a running assessment out of 100 that opened at zero.

## Closing

By 19:40 the boardroom had emptied. Krawczyk's message sat unanswered on her phone, and the window opened in sixteen days. Giménez opened the squad cost spreadsheet, typed €58.2 million into the top cell, and started on the line that read Fonseca.
