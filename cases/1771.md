# Helvetia Nutrition: The Activist at the Gate

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1771 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1771 — *The Activist at the Gate* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 March 2026, Ines Waldner, Chief Strategy Officer of Helvetia Nutrition AG, read a forty-page report before her coffee had cooled. Published overnight under the name "Formula First, Babies Last" by a respected international health NGO, three national consumer groups, and an advocacy network with 1.4 million followers, the report alleged that Helvetia sales representatives in two West African markets had distributed free infant-formula samples and run "baby clubs" inside health clinics — practices it said broke both the World Health Organization's marketing code and Helvetia's own published Responsible Marketing Charter. The board wanted a recommendation in ten days, before shareholders gathered for the annual general meeting.

Waldner would not be answering with a single press release. Over the coming days she and Helvetia's crisis team — general counsel, the head of sustainability, the investor-relations director, and the VP of corporate affairs — would need to size up the coalition itself, decide how Helvetia would meet it, settle what, if anything, to offer it, and commit the company to whatever it would build afterward so the next campaign did not start from zero.

## The Protagonist

Waldner, 51, had led Helvetia's strategy function since 2022, arriving from a Geneva-based consumer-health group where she had spent a decade advising on market entry rather than crisis response. She had personally drafted much of the language in Helvetia's Responsible Marketing Charter two years earlier — the public pledge to follow the WHO Code that Helvetia had issued partly to reassure the ESG-screened institutional investors who, at 41% of shares outstanding, now wanted to know what the pledge had actually changed in the field. Her chief executive had told the crisis team only that the board wanted a recommendation, not a debate.

## The Decision

Waldner's task ran in four parts, each building on the last. She first had to help the team read the coalition itself: how resourced it truly was and how far its actual demand went. She then had to settle Helvetia's posture toward it. From there she had to size a single concession. And by the AGM, she had to commit Helvetia to whatever it would do afterward, so the crisis left something standing behind it besides relief.

## The Company and its Setting

Helvetia Nutrition AG, headquartered in Lausanne and listed on the SIX Swiss Exchange, produced infant formula, plant-based beverages, and packaged foods across Europe, Latin America, and West Africa; its infant-nutrition line alone was valued at CHF 1.1 billion within a group whose total revenue stood at CHF 4.2 billion. Two years earlier, responding to pressure from its ESG-focused shareholders, Helvetia had published the Responsible Marketing Charter, a pledge to follow the WHO Code on breast-milk substitutes in every market it served, regardless of local law. The pledge had been well received at the time. It was also, the coalition's report now argued, a promise Helvetia had made publicly and had not kept in the field.

## The Situation

Within 72 hours of the report's release, the campaign's hashtag had reached 22 million impressions, three Tier-1 European outlets had covered the story, and an online petition demanding a full marketing moratorium had gathered 180,000 signatures. Two institutional investors holding a combined 9% of Helvetia's shares signaled they would co-file a shareholder resolution at the AGM unless the company responded; a pension fund managing CHF 3 billion in assets had placed Helvetia stock "under review." A major European grocery chain, whose supply contract was worth CHF 168 million a year, wanted written assurance of compliance within 21 days or it would pause renewal talks (see Exhibit 1). If Helvetia did nothing at all, analysts covering the stock were already pricing in a 4–7% share-price overhang and a trust deficit in the infant-nutrition franchise that would take years to close. The board's own clock ran faster than any of that: a recommendation was due in ten days, before the AGM.

## What the Protagonist Controlled

Before recommending anything, Waldner's team had to file two numbers of its own: how resourced it judged the coalition to be — weighing its legitimacy, money, organizational network, and reach in the media — on a scale of 0 to 100, and separately, how radical it judged the coalition's actual demand to be, from a reformist ask to change one field practice, at 0, to an absolutist demand to end formula marketing altogether, at 100. Those two reads would shape everything that followed.

On posture, three paths lay open. One option was to Resist: defend the West African practices as locally legal and contest the coalition's report point by point, trading public reassurance for the near-term revenue those practices protected in the affected markets. A second option was to Engage: acknowledge the allegations publicly, open an internal investigation, and correct whatever it found, trading a public admission for a faster return to calm. A third option was to Partner: invite the health NGO itself into a monitoring arrangement over Helvetia's own marketing practices, trading a share of managerial control for the credibility the NGO's name would lend the outcome. Choosing to partner also meant setting how tightly to bind the arrangement, on a five-point scale running from an open-ended invitation with no scope or sunset, through loose terms, a defined scope, and a defined scope with a sunset clause, up to a defined scope bounded by both a sunset clause and specified decision rights.

Alongside the posture, the team had to settle on a single concession (detailed in Exhibit 2, costed in CHF where the team had a figure and described as a standing commitment where it did not): options ran from offering the coalition nothing at all for now, through publicly reaffirming the Charter with a named accountable executive, an independent audit of West African operations at CHF 2.5 million, a third-party monitoring partnership with the health NGO itself, a regional marketing pause worth roughly CHF 40 million a year, up to a moratorium on free formula samples across the entire group.

Finally, for the AGM, Waldner had to commit Helvetia to one of six forward postures on governance: treating the episode as a one-off public-relations matter with no follow-through; restating the Charter publicly without changing anything else; reporting quarterly and publicly against everything the Charter had promised; revising field-sales incentives so bonuses no longer rewarded sample volume; standing up a permanent panel of outside stakeholders to advise the board; or combining all of the above into one full, standing non-market capability.

Across every round, the board would be watching five running numbers: Helvetia's reputational capital, which stood at 72 out of 100 and could fall much faster than it rose; the campaign's intensity, at 68 out of 100, where a lower number was the better direction; investor confidence, at 54; a governance-capability score measuring how much standing capacity existed for the next campaign, at 20; and the cumulative financial cost of the whole affair, which began at CHF 0 million. The grocery contract itself would simply read "at risk" until it was retained or lost, and the crisis as a whole would read "unfolding" until the AGM resolved it one way or the other. A ledger would also record, round by round, how closely the team's read of the coalition matched its actual profile.

## Closing

At her desk that morning, Waldner set the countdown at ten days and opened a blank page for the two numbers only her own team would see: how resourced the coalition in front of her gate really was, and how far it actually wanted to go.
