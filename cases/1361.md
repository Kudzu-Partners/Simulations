# Costaria's Ministry of Environment: Pricing the Ton

On the morning of Tuesday, April 14, 2026, Valeria Montenegro found a copy of *El Diario Costariano* folded on her desk with a leaked Cabinet memo on its front page. The memo, signed by the Finance Minister and addressed to the President, asked the Ministry of Environment for "an emissions-reduction policy package, with explicit pricing instruments, capable of achieving the Paris commitment of minus 30% by 2035, while protecting trade-exposed sectors and the bottom four income deciles." It added that no proposal would be considered viable if it imposed net costs on the median voter without a transparent way of returning the money.

The Cabinet wanted the first instrument on the President's desk in eight weeks, to take effect in January 2027. Montenegro would have to specify it, and then she would have to defend and reset it once a year, every year, until Costaria reported against its target in 2035.

## The Protagonist

Valeria Montenegro, 45, had been Director-General for Climate Change at the Ministry of Environment since 2022. She was an energy economist by training and had spent nine years in the Ministry of Finance's tax-policy unit before crossing over, which meant she had written revenue estimates for the people who would now be reading hers. She had led the team that drafted Costaria's updated climate commitment in 2024. She had not designed a price on anything that voters bought every day.

Her team was small: a chief economist, a policy designer, a political-economy adviser and a trade analyst on loan from the Ministry of Industry. The President had made clear that the package would carry her name in the Cabinet room.

## The Decision

Montenegro had to decide how Costaria would put a price on greenhouse-gas emissions, and on what terms. The choice was not one decision but a bundle of them, and every part of the bundle could be changed in each of the nine policy years from 2027 to 2035.

The first part was the instrument itself. She could recommend a carbon tax, in which the state fixed the price per tonne and let emissions respond; a cap-and-trade system, in which the state fixed the quantity of permits and covered emitters bought and traded them; or a hybrid, a tax with a quantity limit held behind it as a backstop. Around the instrument sat the rest of the design: the price, the share of national emissions it would reach, what the state did with the money, how imports from countries without a carbon price would be treated, how permits would be issued if she chose trading, and whether the state would fund clean-technology research directly.

## The Company and its Setting

Costaria was a mid-income Latin American republic of about 31 million people, with GDP of roughly US$245 billion in 2025. It emitted 165 million tonnes of carbon-dioxide equivalent a year (MtCO₂e). Power generation produced about 32% of that, industry 24%, transport 22%, agriculture and land use 12% and buildings 10% (see **Exhibit 1**). Natural gas supplied 45% of electricity and hydropower 28%; coal, oil and a small wind-and-solar share supplied the rest.

Costaria was also a small open economy. Steel, cement, fertilisers and refining together made up about 14% of GDP, sold into markets where competitors did not always pay for their emissions. The European Union had begun charging a carbon levy at its border on steel, cement and fertiliser imports, and Costarian exporters were already asking who would receive their carbon payments: Brussels or the Costarian treasury.

The international record was mixed and every faction in the debate quoted part of it. Sweden had taxed carbon since 1991 and now charged about US$137 a tonne, but had climbed to that level over three decades. Australia had repealed its carbon price in 2014; France had suspended a fuel-tax rise in 2018 after street protests; Canada had repealed the consumer side of its federal carbon price in 2025 despite returning most of the money to households.

## The Situation

The commitment Costaria had filed was concrete: emissions of 115 MtCO₂e a year by 2035, a 30% cut from 165, or about 50 million tonnes. No carbon price of any kind was in force. If nothing changed, emissions would stay close to 165 MtCO₂e and Costaria would report in 2035 that it had missed its commitment by the whole 50 million tonnes.

The pressure ran in opposite directions. A coalition of environmental groups had published an open letter demanding a tax of at least US$100 a tonne from the first year. A trade-association op-ed warned of "the de-industrialisation of the Atlantic provinces." A rival newspaper, *La Tribuna del Sur*, had asked in its headline: "Energy bills set to rise — for whom?" The Finance Ministry had already circulated a working draft that started from a tax of US$30 a tonne on 60% of emissions, with the revenue going to the general budget, no treatment of imports and no research funding (see **Exhibit 2**). The Finance Ministry described it as a placeholder rather than a position.

Montenegro's team would track six measures each year. Annual emissions, in MtCO₂e, and the reduction they represented against the 165-million-tonne baseline, with the target at minus 30%. Cumulative revenue raised by the instrument, in billions of Costarian pesos (CO$ B), starting from zero. A political-support index from 0 to 100, compiled by the Ministry's political-economy unit from polling, parliamentary positions and press, which stood at 50 and which the Cabinet read as a measure of whether the package would survive. The net benefit to the bottom 60% of households, expressed as a percentage of household income: positive if what those households received back exceeded what they paid in higher prices, negative if not, and zero at the start. And the clean research-and-development stock, the cumulative clean-technology subsidy paid out, in CO$ B, also starting from zero. Each year's log would also record the tonnes abated in that year and the revenue raised in it.

At the end of 2035 the Cabinet would judge the package on four things: whether the commitment had been met, how much political support remained, whether the bottom 60% had on average come out ahead, and how much clean research the state had built up.

## What Montenegro Controlled

Each year Montenegro set seven things. She chose the instrument: tax, cap-and-trade, or hybrid. She set the carbon price, anywhere from US$0 to US$200 per tonne. She set coverage, the share of national emissions the price reached, from 20% to 95%. She chose what to do with the revenue: send it to the general budget; return it to every resident as an equal per-person dividend; place it in a clean research-and-development fund; or split it, half to a dividend, 30% to transition assistance for affected workers and regions and 20% to clean research. She chose a border carbon adjustment for imports: none, a partial adjustment on steel and cement, or a full adjustment on all trade-exposed goods. If she chose cap-and-trade, she decided whether permits would be auctioned, with the proceeds going to the state, or allocated free to existing emitters in proportion to their historical output, which raised no revenue. And she set a clean-technology research subsidy of between CO$ 0 and CO$ 5 billion a year.

## Closing

By late afternoon Montenegro had written the seven headings on the whiteboard in her office and left the space under each blank. The Finance Ministry's draft sat on the table beside the newspaper. Eight weeks to the President's desk; nine years to the 2035 report.
