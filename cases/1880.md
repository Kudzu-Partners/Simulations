# Lead Across Continents: Redesigning the Atlas Global Executive MBA

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1880 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1880 — *Lead Across Continents* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, April 14, 2026, Ingrid Halvorsen read the letter twice before forwarding it to anyone. Two days earlier, a business-education ranking had published its first responsible-leadership sub-index, and Atlas Global Business School's Global Executive MBA had placed 41st. The letter came jointly from the three largest corporate sponsors — itself a message: they wanted measurable ESG and sustainability content built into the curriculum before they renewed the 31 seats they funded, and they wanted the commitment in writing.

Halvorsen had six weeks to lock a redesigned curriculum for the September 2026 cohort. She could not add a single teaching hour, and she could not cut the core management content the outgoing cohort had rated 4.6 out of 5.

## The Protagonist

Halvorsen, 51, had directed the GEMBA for four years, after a decade in the school's corporate-relations office selling the very sponsorship seats now in question. She chaired a five-person leadership team — an academic dean, a director of corporate relations, an ESG and sustainability lead, and a director of participant experience — each with a different, defensible read on the sponsors' letter. Her scorecard, set by the rectorate, tracked global coherence, sustainability credibility, participant satisfaction, and standing with the sponsors funding nearly half the cohort.

## The Decision

Halvorsen's team did not agree on what, exactly, needed fixing. One reading of the outgoing cohort's comments held that the school had not scheduled enough ESG content yet, and adding more hours was the whole fix; a second held that ESG sat disconnected from the rest of the teaching while the three residency cities felt like separate trips rather than one journey; a third held that nothing structural was actually wrong, and the ranking placement would correct itself over the next cycle; a fourth located the weakness inside the core management teaching itself, and that was what needed strengthening first. Whichever reading she accepted would decide the rule she gave her team to design against: build ESG into the finance, strategy and operations teaching itself, rather than teach it on its own; try to satisfy every request in one draft and settle the arithmetic once the wish list was complete; hold the core steady above every other priority and give ESG only what the sponsors' letter strictly required; or add one clearly scheduled ESG block that the sponsors could easily see and count for themselves. How her team named the problem — a volume gap or an integration gap — was itself part of what they had to decide.

Whichever diagnosis she accepted, the arithmetic did not move: 480 contact hours, fixed by accreditation and by executives' limited time away from work, split 320 hours of core management, 70 of ESG and sustainability, and 90 of regional and elective content (see Exhibit 2). Any hour added to one had to come from another, and she had to set the new split. She also had to decide how ESG would be taught — keep a small dedicated ESG module as an anchor and integrate the remainder across the core courses; build one clearly scheduled, stand-alone ESG block that sponsors could see and point to directly; or fold ESG into the finance, strategy and operations cases so it lived inside the core teaching itself — and how it would be evidenced: a reflective journal, ungraded, that recorded what a participant had noticed during the sessions; a graded deliverable, folded into the core casework, that a participant had to complete to pass; or attendance at the scheduled sessions, with no separate test of what a participant could do.

A third front was regional. The current split — 210 hours in Barcelona, 150 in São Paulo, 120 in Singapore (see Exhibit 2) — had already drawn complaints of incoherence. Two complications sat alongside it: São Paulo's faculty resisted losing finance hours, and a Singapore partner had offered a sponsored operations residency at no cost to the school. Halvorsen had to reset the three cities' hours, and decide the offer — accept it in full, since it added teaching capacity the school did not have to pay for; accept it on terms that capped the extra hours, so no single city came to dominate the schedule; or decline it altogether, on the view that a donated block of teaching time was not free if it reshaped the whole programme's geography. She also had to decide how to bind the three residencies: build one leadership project that ran through all three cities and tied the residencies into a single arc; trust that three strong residencies would read as one coherent programme without any additional binding; or write a short handoff briefing between residencies, so each faculty team knew what the last one had covered. Bringing São Paulo's faculty into that decision, rather than issuing new hours from Barcelona, was its own choice about how much time to spend.

Finally, she had to decide what she would commit to in front of the sponsors and the dean: commit to ESG coverage alone, since that was the specific number the sponsors' letter had named; commit publicly to all four measures she was tracking, and be judged on the whole design at once; or commit to participant satisfaction alone, protecting the relationship with the 72 executives paying for the degree. She had to decide what she would present as proof that ESG had actually been learned — a promise that formal assessment would arrive with the following cohort; the timetable itself, showing the ESG hours that had been scheduled; or graded assignments with rubrics, taken directly from inside the core cases. And she had to decide what she would say about what had been cut to make room: name the specific duplicated core hours that had been removed, and the principle behind it; describe the cuts only in general terms, without naming what had actually gone; or argue that the reallocation, on net, had cost the core nothing that students would notice. How firmly she defended that position once challenged was a further choice, separate from the position itself.

## The Company and its Setting

Atlas Global Business School ran its flagship GEMBA from its home campus in Barcelona, with teaching residencies in São Paulo and Singapore. The programme enrolled 72 senior managers a year from 26 nationalities, generated EUR 9.4 million in revenue at a 22% margin, and fed the school's rankings and corporate-client pipeline (see Exhibit 1). Three multinational sponsors funded 31 of the 72 seats — 43% of the cohort, worth EUR 4.05 million over the next two intakes — and had just made ESG fluency a written condition of renewal.

## The Situation

The programme's contractual satisfaction floor with its sponsors was a Net Promoter Score of +40. The outgoing cohort had scored +47, above the floor, but its open comments flagged that "ESG felt bolted on" and that the three residencies carried "too much overlap." The ranking placement — 41st on the new sub-index — sat outside what the corporate-relations office considered safe for renewal conversations. If Halvorsen changed nothing structural, the sponsors' letter was explicit about the consequence: the three of them would fund only single-year seats going forward, ending the multi-year pipeline certainty the recruiting plan depended on.

## What Halvorsen Controlled

Each round, Halvorsen set the topic-hour and regional-hour splits, chose how ESG would be delivered and evidenced, decided the Singapore residency and the connective thread across the three cities, and determined how much time went into winning over resistant faculty. In the final round she set the scorecard she would defend and the proof she would offer that ESG had been learned rather than merely scheduled. She was measured on four things throughout: ESG coverage, how well ESG was integrated into — rather than added onto — the core teaching, how balanced and coherent the three-city journey felt, and participant satisfaction against the +40 floor.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of April 14, Halvorsen opened the current curriculum map alongside a blank page and drew a line down the middle. The September lock date was six weeks away. Three sponsors were waiting for an answer worth EUR 4.05 million, and 480 hours had to hold all of it.
