# Prismview Analytics: The Road to Pricing Night

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-820 · **Companion simulation:** Express 820 — *The S-1 Filing: Prismview Analytics IPO* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Monday, March 2, 2026, at 07:30, Laura Kessler-Obi sat down in a glass-walled conference room in Kendall Square, Cambridge, opposite the working group for the initial public offering (IPO) of Prismview Analytics. Prismview's registration statement on Form S-1 had been public for twenty-one days. Its filed range was US$18 to US$21 a share, for 22 million new shares, on Nasdaq under the ticker PRSM. The first item on the agenda was how to position the company to investors, and Kessler-Obi had promised the syndicate desk an answer by Wednesday.

Three things had changed since the filing. A close competitor had priced its own IPO the previous Thursday and closed below its offer price on the first day. Two strategic buyers and a large private-equity firm had approached Prismview about a sale at US$2.4 billion to US$2.8 billion. And the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) had opened an informal inquiry into the timing of revenue recognition on three enterprise contracts. The inquiry was not public, and nothing in the S-1 mentioned it.

## The Protagonist

Kessler-Obi was forty-six, a managing director and head of technology equity capital markets at the bank that held the left-lead position among Prismview's three joint bookrunners. She had worked on more than thirty technology IPOs in twenty years. She had never taken one to market with an open inquiry from the SEC's enforcement staff on the file.

## The Decision

Between that Monday and pricing night, about three weeks later, Kessler-Obi had to carry the deal through five sets of decisions (see Exhibit 2): positioning and terms; the disclosure and governance package in the S-1; price talk and deal size on the road show; the response to the SEC staff's comment letters; and, on pricing night, the offer price, the allocation and the aftermarket plan. The working group had agreed to adopt her recommendations unless the board overruled them. Every choice would be in the prospectus, the order book or the trading record, and none could be quietly withdrawn.

## The Company and its Setting

Prismview Analytics, Inc., founded in 2015 and incorporated in Delaware, sold an embedded analytics and business-intelligence platform to mid-market and enterprise companies. It had about 620 employees and 1,840 customers. Its annual recurring revenue (ARR) stood at US$180 million, growing 32% a year; net revenue retention was 118%, meaning its existing customers spent 18% more than a year earlier; and its growth rate plus free-cash-flow margin summed to 24. It held US$84 million in cash against US$95 million of venture debt, and had 108 million shares outstanding before the offering, fully diluted. At the midpoint of the range the company was valued at about US$2.54 billion (see Exhibit 1).

The market for software IPOs was uneasy. The competitor's broken deal had put the window for horizontal software issuers in question. At the same time, a wave of retail trading had pushed two small software stocks to valuations with no apparent link to their results, which made every comparison set harder to defend in front of institutional investors.

## The Situation

The positioning question came down to which companies Prismview would be compared with. Four candidate peer groups sat on the table, each with its own median valuation: artificial-intelligence-adjacent analytics at 11.4 times ARR, horizontal software at 8.6 times, data infrastructure at 14.2 times and vertical analytics at 7.1 times. Prismview's chief executive, Nathan Carrow, preferred the higher-multiple sets; the product's machine-learning features, he argued, made the lower ones a discount the company had not earned. Institutional portfolio managers, Kessler-Obi knew, would ask which set they could defend to their own investment committees.

Around the peer group sat three more terms. The range could be tightened, held or widened. Existing stockholders had asked to sell 3 million shares alongside the company's 22 million, which would give insiders liquidity and give investors a reason to ask why they were selling. And the gross spread, the underwriters' fee, had been filed at the bank's standard level, which Prismview's chief financial officer, Priya Venkataraman, wanted lower.

The inquiry was the harder problem. Prismview's outside counsel said disclosure obligations might or might not be triggered, depending on how the inquiry developed; no Wells notice, the formal warning that precedes enforcement action, had been issued. Carrow saw an informal inquiry as routine and did not want it on the cover in the same week a competitor's deal had broken. Venkataraman was less sure, because the three contracts sat inside the revenue figures every growth metric was built on.

If nothing changed, the S-1 would go to investors as filed: the current range, no mention of the inquiry, and a governance package still to be settled. The deal would keep its three-week clock, and whatever the SEC staff did next would happen during the road show rather than before it.

## What Kessler-Obi Controlled

By Wednesday she had to recommend the **peer group** from the four sets; the **price range**, tightened to US$19–21, held at US$18–21 or widened to US$17–22; whether the offering was **primary only** or included the 3 million **secondary** shares; and the **gross spread**, at 7.0%, 6.5% or 6.25%.

For the **S-1 amendment** she would recommend how to treat the SEC inquiry: a full risk factor with disclosure in the body of the prospectus, a short note in a recent-developments box, or no disclosure on the ground that the matter was not yet material. She would decide whether to include a risk factor on volatility driven by retail trading; whether the discussion of results led with non-GAAP metrics, balanced them with results under generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) and a reconciliation, or put GAAP first; the **governance package**, with separate yes-or-no calls on dual-class super-voting shares, a staggered board and a Delaware forum-selection bylaw; and whether insiders' pre-arranged trading plans would be announced at pricing.

On the **road show** she would decide whether to hold price talk at US$18–21 or raise it to US$20–22 or US$21–23; whether to keep the deal at 22 million shares or upsize it to 25 million or 28 million; whether to accelerate the timetable by a day, hold it or extend it by two days; and whether to tell the anchor long-only investors that their orders would be filled in full or keep them guessing.

When the **SEC staff's comments** came back, she would recommend how to answer them on the inquiry: update the risk factor with full disclosure, argue in a letter that the matter was not material, or wait for formal SEC action. She would also recommend how to answer any challenge to revenue recognition, by defending the method, revising it going forward without restating, or restating, and whether to disclose a customer that represented 11% of revenue by name or unnamed with the figure quantified.

On **pricing night** she would recommend the offer price: pull the deal and refile in 90 to 120 days, or price at US$20, US$22, US$23, US$24 or US$25. She would set the **allocation**, weighted to long-only funds, balanced across long-only funds, hedge funds and retail, or tilted toward hedge funds to fill more orders; the **over-allotment option**, holding a naked short for stabilization, exercising it fully on the first day, or exercising part of it; and the **first-day trading posture**: a stabilizing bid at the offer price, no bid, or penalty bids against investors who flipped their shares.

Her dashboard tracked **disclosure integrity** and **book quality**, each on a scale of 0 to 100, and the company's **implied valuation**. After each round it would also show a **deal-quality score**, a composite from 0 to 100 covering issuer proceeds, first-day performance, allocation quality, disclosure integrity, syndicate economics, the issuer-bank relationship, the governance signal and the regulatory outcome. The deal log would record each round with a line of commentary from the bank's review committee.

## Closing

At 07:55 Carrow walked in with a printout of the competitor's first-day trading chart and laid it face down on the table. Venkataraman asked whether anyone had read the SEC letter since Friday. Kessler-Obi said she had read it three times, and opened the peer-group slide.
