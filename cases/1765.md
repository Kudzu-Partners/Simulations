# San Gerardo: Worth the Device

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1765 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1765 — *Worth the Device* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 6 May 2026, Dr. Livia Conti opened a 240-page dossier that had reached her office overnight and reached the number that mattered before she reached page two. CardioNova, a European medical-device manufacturer, had filed for the adoption of VentriGuard, an implantable monitoring-and-therapy device for advanced heart failure, priced at €22,000 per patient. Azienda Ospedaliera San Gerardo's uncommitted devices budget for the year stood at €2.4 million. Whatever her committee ruled would not stay inside the hospital's own 720 beds: San Gerardo hosted the region of Lombardy's Health Technology Assessment unit, and other hospitals across the region were known to follow its verdicts.

The regional procurement cycle would close in thirty days. Conti's committee would have to set its evidence standard, build its own cost-effectiveness model, issue a recommendation with a reimbursement mechanism attached, and then defend that recommendation once the people it affected had a chance to react to it — all before that deadline, and all in public enough that the choice, once made, would be hard to quietly reverse.

## The Protagonist

Conti, 41, had led San Gerardo's HTA unit for three years, after a decade split between hospital pharmacy and a health-economics doctorate. The unit reported to the medical directorate but sat outside the cardiology department's own chain of command, a structural choice meant to keep adoption decisions answerable to evidence and budget rather than to whichever clinician wanted a device most. Conti had built the unit's standing on a single habit: no verdict left her office without a stated evidence bar and a stated cost ceiling, set before anyone in the room found out whether the device in question cleared them.

## The Decision

Conti's committee had to work through the appraisal in stages. First, before looking at whether VentriGuard passed or failed, it had to decide what a pass would even require: a spending ceiling per unit of health gained, a floor for the evidence, and which reading of the trial's claimed benefit to carry forward. Only then could it build the actual model — cost, benefit, and the difference between what the device cost per patient and what it would cost the system once every eligible patient in the region was counted. From the model came a recommendation and a reimbursement design. And because the recommendation would provoke people with real leverage, a fourth stage waited: holding the position, adjusting it, or abandoning it once challenged (see Exhibit 2).

## The Company and its Setting

Azienda Ospedaliera San Gerardo was a public teaching hospital in Lombardy, one of the hospitals the Servizio Sanitario Nazionale relied on to also perform the region's technology appraisals. That second role was the whole difficulty: a device judged worth adopting at San Gerardo became, in practice, a device Lombardy adopted, because other hospitals treated the unit's verdicts as the region's default answer. The mandate behind Conti's desk was explicit — new devices had to earn their place on clinical value and cost-effectiveness — and the cardiology service, which wanted VentriGuard approved that quarter, knew that Conti's unit applied that mandate to every filing, including this one.

## The Situation

CardioNova's case rested on one trial: 480 patients, twelve months of follow-up, funded by the manufacturer, reporting a 0.60 quality-adjusted-life-year gain per patient and a 22% cut in heart-failure rehospitalizations, with confidence intervals wide enough that the true benefit could plausibly sit well below the headline number. VentriGuard's incremental cost over standard care worked out to roughly €18,000 per patient. Set against the trial's claimed benefit, that produced a cost-effectiveness ratio of about €34,000 for every quality-adjusted life year gained — inside the commonly cited willingness-to-pay band of €25,000 to €40,000. Halve the benefit, a reading well within the trial's own confidence interval, and the same ratio roughly doubled, to about €68,000 per quality-adjusted life year, outside that band (see Exhibit 1).

Roughly 150 patients a year at San Gerardo would qualify under the widest reading of eligibility. Because other hospitals followed San Gerardo's lead, the same eligibility criteria applied across Lombardy implied something near 1,400 patients regionally — about nine times the hospital's own pool — and a regional bill that, at full uptake and full price, could run past €30 million a year, money that would otherwise fund other care across the region. San Gerardo's own uncommitted allowance for devices this year was fixed at €2.4 million. The unit's dashboard would carry six running figures through the appraisal: the cost-effectiveness ratio itself; how much of the hospital's own device allowance a given plan would use; what the same plan implied at regional scale; how many years of healthy life it would actually deliver; what fraction of the eligible patients would actually receive the device; and a composite reading of how defensible the whole verdict would look to the regional board, combining the value delivered, what it cost, how rigorously the evidence had been tested, and whether the committee's position held under pressure. If Conti's unit reached the thirty-day mark with no ruling, the regional board would convene regardless, VentriGuard would remain filed with no reimbursement mechanism attached to it, and the cardiology chief — who wanted it approved without restriction — would have the opening he had already said he would use to take the matter to the medical board and the regional press.

## What Conti Controlled

In the appraisal's first stage, Conti had to fix a willingness-to-pay ceiling per quality-adjusted life year: €25,000, the band's strict floor; €34,000, the band's midpoint value; €40,000, the band's upper edge; or €50,000, above the band entirely. She had to decide what the dossier needed to clear before it counted as evidence at all — the single sponsor-funded trial as submitted, an independent study confirming it, or outcomes tracked past two years — and which reading of the benefit to carry into her own model: the trial's full 0.60 claim, a pessimistic case of 0.30, or a mid-range estimate of 0.47.

Next came the model itself. Conti had to define which patients counted as eligible — advanced heart-failure cases alone, roughly 45% of the pool; advanced plus moderate cases, roughly 75%; or every heart-failure admission the hospital saw — set the uptake she expected within that group, somewhere between one-fifth and the full pool, and choose whether to model the hospital's own budget alone, or the regional total her verdict would set.

From there came the recommendation, and the terms attached to it. Conti could adopt VentriGuard for every eligible patient without restriction, adopt it for one defined sub-group only, admit it conditionally through managed access while the evidence matured, or reject it outright until the evidence was stronger. Whichever she chose, she also had to set a reimbursement tier: full reimbursement with no controls on volume, a capped annual volume of implants, a rebate paid only when patients responded, or a co-payment shared with the patient — and, if volume itself needed a hard number, an annual ceiling somewhere between 30 and the full pool of 150.

The fourth stage was reaction. The cardiology chief, who wanted the device approved without restriction, could be met by holding the committee's position without change, adjusting the eligibility or tier modestly, or loosening access to end the disagreement. CardioNova could be met by declining its offer and holding list price, accepting a 15% price-volume discount, or negotiating an outcomes-based arrangement tied to results. And a patient-advocacy group asking for wider access for moderate-risk patients could be met by holding eligibility where it already stood, opening it through a coverage-and-evidence arrangement, or opening access broadly to meet the demand.

## Closing

By the second week of May, Conti had the dossier, the trial's confidence intervals, and a blank template for the recommendation the regional board expected in thirty days. The cardiology chief had already asked when the committee would rule.
