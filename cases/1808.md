# Aurélie Executive Learning: The Four-Way Squeeze

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1808 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1808 — *Design the Custom Program* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 3 March 2026, Camille Duverger, Program Director of Custom Programs at Aurélie Executive Learning, opened a request for proposal from Continentale Energie's chief human resources officer. Continentale wanted a flagship leadership program for its top 300 managers, and an answer in ten weeks. Two rival business schools and one global consultancy had received the same brief, and the consultancy was already expected to quote about 15% less.

Continentale was Aurélie's largest single relationship, worth an estimated EUR 3.1 million over three years, and its one-page brief read simply: "our managers manage operations well but cannot lead transformation." Duverger had ten weeks to turn that sentence into a diagnosis, a design, a price and a pitch — without losing the business to a cheaper bidder or winning it in a way her own finance office would not let her keep.

## The Protagonist

Camille Duverger, 44, had directed Aurélie Executive Learning's Custom Programs unit since 2019, after nine years in corporate leadership design. She had joined on the argument that a custom program was only as good as the gap it closed, not the catalogue it was assembled from. Her evaluation moved with the division's contribution margin and its anchor-account renewals. She had watched the unit's win rate slide from 48% to 34% in two years, and had watched three other programs close below the margin floor after their scope grew past what had been priced.

## The Decision

Duverger's first task was diagnostic. Her discovery pack of interviews, a survey extract and talent-review data on the 300 managers supported several readings of the client's sentence. One located the problem in the roster: the wrong 300 people in those jobs. A second took the brief as the complete diagnosis and called for exactly the course it named. A third, from the transcripts, read an influence-and-courage gap — managers avoiding open conflict and freezing on hard trade-offs. A fourth, matching the brief's wording, read a strategy-knowledge gap — managers needing more frameworks and analytical tools. The evidence to anchor it on could be an external benchmark of what peer companies' programs covered; all three sources — interviews, survey and talent data — cross-checked together; the one-page brief itself, treated as a complete statement of need; or the six interviews, taken alone as the richest source. She also had to set how many named behaviours the program would change, with success defined as completion and attendance across the 300 managers, as a 360-degree lift plus a transfer-to-work index, or as satisfaction and net-promoter scores per module.

The second stage was architectural. Four formats were on the table: a mostly asynchronous digital curriculum, running across all ten eventual cohorts; an all-live, fully bespoke format with every session in person; a blend of in-person intensives and digital content; or a lecture series the division had used before. A faculty allocation followed, across three pools (see Exhibit 2): the two star academics the client knew by name, with only 24 days between them; affiliate practitioners, scalable at roughly EUR 2,000 a day; and Continentale's own executives teaching alongside Aurélie's faculty at no fee. A further choice was what would carry the learning back into the job: coaching circles running between the taught modules; extra content modules covering additional material; a business-challenge project with spaced practice and manager involvement; or no dedicated mechanism to carry the learning back at all.

Duverger's third task was financial: she had to set the price against the EUR 1.8 million cap and the 35% margin her finance office required (see Exhibit 1), and close the gap between the sixteen weeks the board wanted and the 22-to-26-week timeline this scope normally took (see Exhibit 2): pay design overtime to compress the full build into sixteen weeks; tell the board the program could not go live before week twenty-four; commit to sixteen weeks without yet resolving where the extra time would come from; or launch a reduced first cohort within sixteen weeks while the rest was built in parallel. Alongside price and launch sat a position on the 20%, EUR 360,000 fee tied to six-month behaviour change: negotiate a floor and ceiling sharing the risk; decline the impact-linked structure for a fixed fee; build the measurement approach into the design from the start; or accept the fee structure as proposed. Finally, she had to set how firmly the team would hold the line against scope changes after signature.

The last stage was the pitch itself, to Continentale's CHRO and a procurement lead. Duverger had to decide what to lead with: the price itself, and flexibility in negotiating the fee; the diagnosed gap and the behaviours the journey would change; or the two star faculty names and the school's brand and rankings. She also had to answer the objection that the consultancy's bid ran roughly 15% lower: hold the price and answer on diagnosed relevance and measured transfer instead of cost; trim a module out of the design to bring the price down; or match the gap by cutting about EUR 250,000 from the quoted price. And she had to answer the sixteen-week question: promise the client that everything asked for would be ready right on time; present the phased first-cohort plan and state exactly what would be ready by week sixteen; or tell the client that the program needed the full twenty-four weeks. Throughout the room she also had to judge how firmly to hold that line rather than concede ground.

## The Company and its Setting

Aurélie Executive Learning was the custom-programs division of a European business school in Cergy, north-west of Paris, set up in 2004 to design bespoke leadership programs outside the school's open-enrolment catalogue. The division billed roughly EUR 24 million a year across about 40 engagements, carried a target contribution margin of 35%, and employed 22 program managers and designers drawing on a pool of 90 faculty (see Exhibit 1). Its standing rested on one claim: that its programs were diagnosed, not assembled from a catalogue — a claim now under pressure as clients asked for faster launches and harder proof of impact, while procurement pushed on fee.

## The Situation

Continentale Energie was a EUR 9-billion European utility mid-way through an energy-transition restructuring, and its RFP asked for a program to build "transformation leadership" in the 300 managers who would carry it, delivered in cohorts of 30 (see Exhibit 1). The client had set a budget cap of EUR 1.8 million and wanted the first cohort live in sixteen weeks (see Exhibit 2). Twenty percent of the fee — EUR 360,000 — would be paid only if the program produced measurable behaviour change six months after launch, assessed through 360-degree feedback and a transfer-to-work index, not end-of-course satisfaction.

On the dashboard tracking the bid, four gauges stood at their opening values: design fit to the diagnosed need, at 16 out of 100; contribution margin, held at the 35% target until a price was set; faculty feasibility — whether the promised team could really be delivered — at 60 out of 100; and launch readiness for a cohort going live in sixteen weeks, at 10 out of 100. None of the EUR 1.8 million was yet committed. A catalogue program at full price risked reading as generic and losing to the consultancy; a program promising everything the brief seemed to ask risked costing more than the division could recover under its own margin floor.

## What Duverger Controlled

Each stage committed her bid team to a decision carried into the next: a diagnosis, a design and faculty plan, a price and launch plan, then a pitch. She was measured throughout on four things — how well the design fit the diagnosed need, the contribution margin the engagement protected, whether the promised faculty could really be delivered, and whether a first cohort could genuinely go live in sixteen weeks.

## Closing

Duverger printed the one-page brief and the unit's faculty roster and laid them side by side on her desk. Ten weeks were on the clock, and the bid team was due at nine.
