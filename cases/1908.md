# Volunteer Outdoor Equipment Co.: Short the Tent, Long the Basics

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1908 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1908 — *Agile or Lean?* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, April 13, 2026, Elise Trammell read two reports that had landed overnight and found that they described the same failure. The first was a stockout list: the Ridgeline 2P, the performance tent whose sell-through had tripled in three weeks after an influencer featured it, was filling 61% of retailers' orders. The second was an inventory aging report: US$22 million of classic tents and daypacks sitting in Knoxville and Reno, months older than they should have been and heading into the wrong end of the season. Volunteer Outdoor Equipment Co. could not ship what its customers wanted and could not sell what it already owned.

Trammell had five weeks. The chief executive, Wade Kessler, wanted the third-quarter plan before the board in the third week of May, and had told her the lead banker would read it too. The demand behind the Ridgeline would be spent in roughly eight weeks, and the bank had said another month at the current draw on the revolving line would start a conversation about covenants. Three clocks, one supply chain, one plan to write.

## The Protagonist

Elise Trammell, 44, had been VOEC's Director of Logistics and Supply Chain since 2022. She had joined in 2016 as demand planning manager and had been promoted on the strength of the Vietnam programme: eleven suppliers consolidated into one, landed cost per unit down four years running. Half her bonus moved with landed cost per unit; the other half moved with the service level she was now missing. The lane she was being asked to reconsider was the one she had built.

## The Decision

Trammell had to decide how VOEC would source, stock and watch its products for the rest of the year, and commit that answer to the board and the bank before the demand she was chasing had ended.

## The Company and its Setting

VOEC was founded in Knoxville, Tennessee, in 2003 and made outdoor and camping equipment: tents, packs, sleeping systems and cooking kit. It sold through national outdoor retailers, big-box chains and a direct channel, booked US$540 million of revenue at a 38% gross margin and a 9% operating margin, and shipped from Knoxville, Reno and a third-party network on the West Coast.

The portfolio ran on two clocks (see **Exhibit 2**). Roughly 60% of volume was "basics" — classic tents, daypacks, mid-tier sleeping bags — whose demand barely moved from year to year and whose buyers compared prices to the dollar. The rest was performance and seasonal gear: new fabrics, fashion colours, short lives, the richest margins in the company. Both had always been made by the same contract manufacturer in Vietnam and moved the same way: ocean freight on a 75-day lead time, ordered against a forecast.

## The Situation

In late March a labour action at a transpacific port collided with a typhoon backlog. VOEC's Vietnam lead time went from 75 days to about 115, and arrival dates stopped being reliable. Trammell had learned about it three weeks in, from a retailer's complaint.

Both landed in the same quarter. Overall fill rate had fallen from a 95% target to 82%; the Ridgeline sat at 61%. Finance put unserved full-margin demand for the quarter at about US$14 million. Inventory stood at US$96 million against a US$110 million revolving facility drawn to US$98 million at SOFR plus 3.0%, leaving US$12 million of undrawn room (see **Exhibit 1**). The US$22 million of slow basics would need a 20% to 30% markdown if not cleared before the next season. The planning team's own 0-to-100 responsiveness index stood at 28.

Each of the two most direct moves carried its own arithmetic. Air freight ran about US$4.20 per unit against roughly US$0.45 by ocean, eight to ten times the cost, on a Ridgeline that netted US$18 a unit and on basics that netted very little. Every dollar of extra safety stock was a dollar of the revolver: at US$96 million of inventory against a US$110 million line, at most US$14 million of new stock could go in before inventory reached the line.

The team had split. Tomás Delgado, in sourcing, argued that the Vietnam lane was the cheapest per unit in the industry and that a port strike was a bad quarter, not a design fault. Nadia Freels, in demand planning, argued that fill rate was the only number retailers ever saw and the answer was stock behind everything. Kessler had asked what she would say if the same thing happened next spring for a different reason.

If nothing changed, the quarter was already legible. Fill rate would stay near 82%, the Ridgeline window would close with roughly US$14 million of full-margin demand unserved, the US$22 million of basics would be marked down 20% to 30% at season end, and the draw would sit where the bank had flagged it.

## What Trammell Controlled

She had four sets of commitments to make, and no way to unmake them once the quarter ran.

The first was how to classify the catalogue for supply design: split it by how predictable demand was, treating basics and performance gear as two problems; keep it as one catalogue on the single chain already running; treat every item as if it might go viral and source the whole range close to home; or split it by volume, running high-volume items one way and low-volume ones the other. She also had to name where VOEC was weakest — at seeing a disruption early, at being provisioned for one in advance, or at moving quickly once one had been identified — or say it was weakest nowhere, the design sound and the quarter unlucky. And she had to decide how much weight that classification would carry over everything that followed.

The second was the sourcing architecture and the buffer (see **Exhibit 2**). She could keep the low-cost ocean lane for the basics and add a near-shore source in Mexico or the United States for the performance range, with a defined hand-off between the forecast-driven and order-driven halves of the flow; keep the single Vietnam lane for everything, the chain already built and running, and absorb the longer lead time; or move the whole portfolio near-shore, basics included, and run every line on a short lead time. She then had to size the safety-stock build for the quarter, anywhere from nothing to US$30 million, and say where it would sit: on the variable performance items and the hand-off point, spread evenly across the catalogue, or on the basics that made up 60% of volume.

The third was the response to the shock itself. She had to set the share of the Ridgeline replenishment that would fly rather than sail, from none to all of it, and state what governed which units went by air: a per-item margin test that flew only units whose recovered margin beat the freight premium; loading whole containers to clear the backlog and reconciling margins afterwards; or flying nothing and waiting for the ocean arrivals. She had to settle the US$22 million of slow basics: clear it now at a controlled 20% to 25% markdown, hold it a season on the view demand would return, or move it all immediately at 40% or more. And she had to set visibility spending at nothing, at US$2 million for supplier early-warning signals, or at US$4 million for a control tower and demand sensing together.

The fourth was what she would commit in front of the board and the bank: a two-chain configuration with selective buffers and the visibility spend as a standing cost; near-shore sourcing and heavy stock across the whole portfolio, held as standing cover; or a return to the single lane once the disruption had passed. She would also have to say which investments were permanent capability and which were one-off responses to this quarter, whether the design would help against a supplier fire or a collapse in demand rather than a port strike, and how firmly she would hold that position when the banker asked.

## Closing

At 7:20 that morning Trammell drew a line down a legal pad and wrote "basics" on one side and "performance" on the other. The board packet closed in five weeks; the Ridgeline demand in eight; the bank's month was already running. She had one chain and US$12 million of room.
