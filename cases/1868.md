# Sahel Digital Services: Rebuilding the Payments Squad

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1868 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1868 — *The Authentic Leader* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 6 April 2026, Amaka Obi learned two things within the same hour: she was the new lead of Sahel Digital Services' twelve-person Payments Platform Squad, which kept the transaction-switching software running for three Nigerian banks, and the firm's largest client — worth NGN 1.6 billion a year, 17% of everything Sahel billed — had filed a 90-day notice to re-tender its contract, citing "instability and declining responsiveness." The previous squad lead had been walked out on Friday for bullying. There was no settling-in period: the client wanted a remediation plan within fourteen days, and the squad she was inheriting had not trusted a manager in over a year.

Four decisions lay ahead of her in the two weeks that followed: how she read the causes of the team's collapse and what she let herself promise on day one; how she ran her first real meeting with a squad that had buried three leaders in two years; which single retention move she made under a deadline that would not wait; and how she handled something a junior engineer had already told her privately — that one of her two most experienced engineers had been inflating the ticket-completion count the client's auditors were now scrutinizing.

## The Protagonist

Amaka Obi, 34, had spent six years in Sahel's engineering organisation, most recently as a senior platform engineer, before her director asked her that Monday to take the squad. She had never led a team this large, this business-critical, or this damaged. She knew two of the twelve well — Femi Adisa and Grace Eze, the senior engineers who held most of the platform's institutional knowledge — and she knew, from her own years on the floor, how the previous lead's removal had landed. Her appointment carried no grace period: if the client walked, the layoffs would fall on the team she had just inherited.

## The Decision

The first decision was where to locate the collapse: three leaders in two years — one dismissed for bullying — teaching the team its managers were not safe; the delivery pipeline and its metrics, with trust something to address once delivery itself stabilized; a squad she read as cynical and resistant, needing its most negative voices replaced; or her own need to prove herself different, quickly, before the team decided otherwise. Tied to it was which relationships would decide whether the squad held together: the client, whose renewed contract seemed to promise the team would follow; the HR partner, who could manage people while she managed delivery; the two flight-risk seniors and the junior who had confided in her; or her own manager, whose good opinion also carried weight. And what she chose not to promise in week one: staking her name publicly on saving the contract; committing only to the small things she could actually keep; promising outright the team would keep its jobs; or staying carefully vague until she understood more — beneath all three, a matter of degree: how candidly to name the credibility gap a burned team would read in her.

The second decision was how to open her first real meeting with the squad: with a finished turnaround plan already written and ready to hand out; by naming the past honestly and diagnosing it with the team before planning; or by looking forward only, without dwelling on what had happened. Alongside it sat a matter of degree — how candidly to speak about the contract risk and her own uncertainty, since too little would read as evasive and too much would tell two engineers the ship was sinking. She also had to decide whether the squad's working norms — how decisions got made, how mistakes got surfaced, how they talked to the client — were left for later; built together with the team; or hers to write and announce. And whether her one visible commitment that week was deferred as too risky so early; a small, certain one she could actually keep within days; or a bold promise of a retention package and a guaranteed win.

The third decision, with the fourteen-day clock running, was the single development or retention move she would make: redistributing the platform's knowledge so it depended on fewer people; personal stay-conversations across the whole squad; retention bonuses for the two engineers weighing outside offers; a team-wide skills and resilience investment; or a visible stretch assignment for the junior who had raised the truth (see Exhibit 2). She also had to decide how to treat Adisa and Eze: treating everyone identically and hoping the seniors felt included; extending the same proactive care to loyal performers as to the loudest exit risk; or matching whatever a competitor offered. And whether that action came after the client plan; instead of it, with the deadline left to slip; or alongside it, within the same fourteen days — and how deliberately she signalled that trust to the watching team.

The fourth decision was the one she had been carrying since her first morning: what to do about the inflated tickets. She could hand the matter to the HR partner and stay out of it herself; raise it privately, require the record corrected, and offer a way back; quietly drop it to keep a valuable engineer and avoid alarming a client already auditing that number; or confront it in front of the whole squad, publicly, to make the point unmistakable. She still had to decide what to tell the client about its own metric: name an individual as the sole cause; disclose the corrected baseline honestly, owning the fix as a team; or present clean numbers and hope the gap went unnoticed. And whether her standard meant removing him regardless, accepting the capacity loss immediately; bending for the person she could least afford to lose; or applying it to him exactly as to everyone else. Whatever she chose, she still had to project the ninety-day numbers — trust, engagement, performance, retention — and decide how honest that pitch to the client would be.

## The Company and its Setting

Sahel Digital Services Ltd. was a Lagos-headquartered technology-services firm of 620 employees, with delivery centres in Lagos and Abuja, serving banks, telcos and government agencies across West Africa. It had grown fast through Nigeria's banking-digitisation boom, and its people practices had not kept pace: attrition ran at 28%, against a board target of roughly 15%, and engagement scores sat bottom-quartile in its peer set. Revenue stood at NGN 9.4 billion. The Payments Platform Squad was simultaneously Sahel's most business-critical unit — a single outage hit millions of cardholders — and its most damaged.

## The Situation

The numbers Obi inherited were blunt (see Exhibit 1). Team trust sat at 2.4 out of 5 and engagement at 2.7 out of 5, both bottom-quartile on the latest pulse survey. Delivery performance — squad capacity against client demand, 0–100 — stood at 55. The probability both flight-risk seniors would still be there in ninety days was estimated at 35%, and a behavioural-integrity measure, tracking how closely what she said matched what she did, sat at a neutral 50 out of 100. Adisa and Eze together held roughly 70% of the platform's institutional knowledge; losing either was expected to cut capacity by close to 30%. If nothing changed, the fourteen-day plan would arrive with nothing behind it, and the re-tender would very likely end the contract.

## What Obi Controlled

Across the fortnight, Obi controlled her diagnosis on day one, the design of her first team meeting, her one retention move and its timing against the client's deadline, and — when the ticket-inflation question surfaced — how she handled it and whether her standard held for everyone alike. She would be judged on whether trust, engagement, performance and retention had moved, and whether what she said in week one still matched what she did by its end.

## Closing

At 9 a.m. that Monday, Obi closed her laptop, looked at the empty desk that had belonged to the man walked out on Friday, and picked up the phone to schedule the squad's first meeting. She had fourteen days, a team that had stopped believing its managers, and a truth only she and one junior engineer knew.
