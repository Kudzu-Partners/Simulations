# Lakeshore Regional Health: Talent Under Pressure

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1890 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1890 — *Talent Under Pressure* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Thursday, 11 June 2026, Deirdre Kavanagh counted the papers on her desk for the third time and got nine again — seven resignation letters and two stress-leave certificates from occupational health. All nine had arrived inside seven days, seven of them from two of the six inpatient units she ran. The nine registered nurses (RNs) were 8% of the nursing workforce on the medicine units of Lakeshore Regional Health.

At 4:15 that afternoon the chief executive of the regional health network telephoned. He wanted a stabilisation plan on Tuesday, 16 June: how the Medicine and Acute Care division would hold safe care through the summer, what it would cost against the 1.4 million Canadian dollars (C$) of in-year flexibility she had left, and what she intended to do about the artificial-intelligence (AI) pilot the network had placed on her units. A reporter had called the communications office twice about emergency-department offload delays.

## The Protagonist

Deirdre Kavanagh, 44, had been Director of Medicine and Acute Care for three years. She had trained as an RN and spent eight years on the wards, four as charge nurse on 4 West — one of the two units now below ratio. She had run the division's last two budget cycles without exceeding them, and her performance review carried its throughput, wait-time and quality targets.

## The Decision

Kavanagh had to decide how the division would be organised and staffed with the people it still had, what the C$1.4 million would buy, whether the pilot would proceed and on what terms, and how she would sequence and measure it over 90 days. The first version was due in five days.

## The Company and its Setting

Lakeshore Regional Health was a 480-bed teaching hospital in a mid-size Ontario city, part of a regional health network. Its Medicine and Acute Care division ran six inpatient units and 210 beds with 620 clinical full-time equivalents across nursing, allied health and support, on a publicly funded operating budget of C$118 million a year (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two constraints framed everything. The network had capped agency staffing at no net new full-time equivalents, a cost control that was not moving inside five days. And the C$1.4 million was the whole of the in-year flexibility, covering incentives, redesign and technology together.

The medicine units were also the network's pilot site for a scheduling and clinical-decision-support tool delivered in April, on which it wanted a go/no-go inside this plan. Two years earlier a network-wide documentation system had gone live on those units over a weekend, run badly for four months, and been partly rolled back. The local bargaining unit's president had told Kavanagh in May that her members read the new pilot as a headcount plan with a software budget.

## The Situation

The nine who left had not been an average nine. They held a disproportionate share of the division's senior charge, code-leadership, wound-care and preceptorship capability — the people who ran the floor at 3 a.m., taught the new graduates and carried the complex dressings. Two units had breached the medicine safe ratio of one nurse to four patients at peak.

Four numbers moved with her decisions, and the network read all four alongside her occupancy figures (see **Exhibit 2**). Safe coverage — the share of the competencies the unit's staffing standard required that were actually rostered — stood at 62% on the worst-affected unit. The staff well-being index stood at 41 out of 100 against a network target of 65 or better. A turnover-intent index kept by human resources stood at 70, lower being better. And staff trust — frontline and bargaining-unit confidence in divisional leadership and in the pilot — stood at 35.

Overtime hours on the affected units were up 38% and sick time was climbing. Each experienced RN who left cost roughly C$65,000 in recruitment, orientation and lost productivity, and four to six months to full competency.

If nothing changed by Tuesday, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The two units stayed below ratio and beds closed. The four seniors known to be job-searching left, at that cost and that delay each. Overtime kept rising on a workforce already at 41 — the mechanism that had produced the nine resignations in the first place. And the newspaper story moved from delays to safety.

## What Kavanagh Controlled

**The framing.** Four namings of the problem were circulating: a shortfall of nine people, to be filled from whatever source; a breach of the 1:4 ratio, to be closed by spreading the remaining nurses across the units; a morale problem in a tired workforce, to be met with recognition; or a change in where competencies sat, to be met by rebuilding the distribution rather than the count. She had to pick one, and say which loss the plan put first — total nursing hours, charting and documentation throughput, the senior charge, code-leadership, wound-care and preceptorship capability, or none in particular, the losses having fallen evenly. And she had to set, as a percentage, the priority given to holding the four seniors who were looking.

**The model of care.** Four redesigns had been put to her. One raised each nurse's patient load above 1:4 and moved assignment-making from the units to the managers' office. One held the current model in place until the network lifted the cap on agency staffing. One moved the units to team nursing, RNs leading teams and tasks reassigned to allied health, personal support workers (PSWs) and unit assistants. One pooled the remaining nurses into a cross-unit float, deployed by the charge nurse against the widest competency gap each shift. Whichever she chose, she had to divide the C$1.4 million across three lines and commit all of it: preceptorship and competency transfer; allied health, PSW and unit-assistant scope work; and retention incentives and well-being. And she had to settle how assignments and schedules were set — centrally by managers, by self-scheduling kept where convenient and overridden when the census demanded, or by self-scheduling and team control built into the model.

**The pilot.** Four stances were available: deploy scheduling and clinical decision support division-wide the following week; run a scoped pilot on one use case on one willing unit, expanding on evidence; postpone it without a date; or decline it outright. If she proceeded, she had to say which function went first — scheduling optimisation or clinical decision support — and how the tool was governed: run as configured with manual override on request; a written policy stating clinicians remained responsible for every decision the tool informed; or override by default, published logic and a named owner accountable for its errors. And she had to say where the roughly 40 minutes per nurse per shift the vendor projected would go: booked as a headcount reduction against the budget, captured but not committed publicly, or returned to patient time and to cutting overtime.

**The plan.** She had to sequence the three levers across 90 days — technology first, occupancy first, or workforce stabilisation first with the rest built onto a recovering base. She had to choose the chief executive's dashboard: bed occupancy and wait times, the targets her own performance review already carried; overtime cost and agency spend, the money the chief executive had asked her to account for; or leading indicators (well-being, overtime, turnover intent) together with lagging outcomes (safe-ratio compliance, throughput, coverage). She had to choose how to use her three minutes in the room — promising full recovery within a quarter and no trade-offs, attributing the position to the agency cap and the resignations and asking for money before committing, or naming the trade-off accepted and what she had chosen not to do. And she had to put a number in writing: how firmly, as a percentage, she held that well-being and retention were outcomes the division answered for rather than preferences.

## Closing

On Friday, 12 June, the nine papers were still squared on the corner of her desk, and above them on the wall the four numbers had not moved: 62, 41, 70, 35. The bargaining unit president had asked for a meeting on Monday; the vendor wanted a decision by month end. The chief executive expected her Tuesday, and she had C$1.4 million and no ninth, tenth or eleventh nurse.
