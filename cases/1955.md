# Pennine Cardiac Institute: The Fourteen-Week Appointment

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1955 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1955 — *The Credible Boss* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the afternoon of Wednesday, 4 February 2026, Helen Ashworth read Professor Alan Voss's resignation letter for the third time and then looked at the wall calendar in the board office at Pennine Cardiac Institute (PCI) in Leeds. Voss had directed the interventional cardiology unit for nine years. He had accepted a chair abroad, would leave in twelve weeks, and had personally written the unit's submission to the national cardiac registry every year since 2017.

The registry closed its annual data window on 15 May 2026 — fourteen weeks away. As Chair of PCI's board, Ashworth had to put a director in post before it closed, and she had to settle two things at once: which of the two finalists took the job, and what was built around whoever took it. The unit produced GBP 41 million of the institute's GBP 88 million of turnover.

## The Protagonist

Ashworth, 58, had chaired PCI's board since 2021. She had come to it after eleven years as director of finance and then deputy chief executive at a foundation trust. She was not a clinician. Her three years at PCI had gone into the balance sheet: she had renegotiated the private-wing contract, closed a deficit on the trials unit's overheads, and lifted the operating surplus to GBP 4.4 million. She had also chaired the nominations committee that twice extended Voss's tenure rather than plan for its end. Her own term ran to 2028, and the commissioning round that would set most of PCI's income for the following three years opened in the autumn.

## The Decision

Two finalists had come through a shortlist of nine (see **Exhibit 2**). Dr Priya Anand, 44, was an interventional cardiologist at a London teaching hospital, held a personal outcomes record in the registry's top decile, and had never held a formal management post. Marcus Reeve, 51, had run two National Health Service (NHS) turnaround mandates, carried an operating budget of GBP 60 million in his current post, and had no clinical background.

The appointment could be made four ways. Anand could take the directorship with a general-manager deputy or chief operating officer (COO) recruited alongside her. Anand could take the directorship and the whole unit, deputy included in her own remit. Reeve could take the directorship with the unit's clinical leads advising him. Or Reeve could take the directorship with a senior clinician appointed as his deputy.

Whichever pairing Ashworth chose, the board had to write down who decided what. Authority over clinical and registry matters could sit with the clinician while the manager ran operations and budgets; it could sit with the general manager for everything, clinical and registry calls included; or the boundary could be left unwritten for the two post-holders to settle in practice.

## The Company and its Setting

PCI had been founded in 1994 as an NHS-partnered foundation with a private patient wing. It ran three interventional theatres, a sixty-bed inpatient ward and a clinical-trials unit, and treated roughly 9,200 patients a year. Turnover was GBP 88 million, 60% NHS-commissioned and 40% private and trials income, on a GBP 4.4 million operating surplus. Six hundred and forty staff included 38 consultant cardiologists and a research faculty of 24.

What the institute sold to commissioners, referring cardiologists and trials sponsors was the outcomes it published in the national cardiac registry. Eleven consultants worked in the interventional unit. Two commissioners held outcome-contingent clauses: a registry ranking below the top quartile would put GBP 12 million of activity out to re-tender. A rival London centre had been recruiting PCI's registrars since the autumn.

## The Situation

Voss's resignation moved four numbers the board tracked (see **Exhibit 1**). The first was a credibility index, measuring whether the people being led believed the unit's leader understood their work; on a 0–100 scale it stood at 30 with the post vacant. The second was the registry-outcome index, the board's running read on the submission: 62 out of 100, against the 70 that marked the top quartile. The third was retention: seven of eleven unit consultants were settled, and four had signalled they might follow Voss or move to London. Each replacement cost about GBP 180,000 and took nine months to recruit. The fourth was a risk exposure, again 0–100 and better when low, combining the re-tender clause, the talent flight and a failed submission; it read 55.

GBP 6 million of trials funding named the unit's principal investigator of record, a role sponsors would only accept from a credentialed cardiologist.

If the board did nothing, the arithmetic was not dramatic. The submission would go in at an index of 62, below the 70 the re-tender clause keyed on, with no clinician of standing to author or defend it; the four flight-risk consultants would leave at GBP 180,000 and nine months each; and the trials funding would lose the post-holder its sponsors required. Ashworth had GBP 350,000 approved for the transition and no more.

The board's discussion had already split. One group read the problem as an operating problem — the rota, the budget and the commissioner relationships needed running by someone who had run them before. Another read it as a clinical one — the consultants would take direction from a colleague whose interventional data they respected, and from nobody else. A third read it as a general leadership problem — the post needed judgement and presence, and the particulars could be learned by whoever had those.

## What Ashworth Controlled

Before shortlisting closed, Ashworth had to name which reading the board would act on, state a provisional lean between the finalists or hold both open, and set how much of the board's remaining diagnostic time went into evidencing the reasoning.

At the appointment itself she chose the pairing and the decision rights, and split the GBP 350,000 across four lines: protected clinical sessions so the new director kept operating; a deputy or COO to carry the rota, the budget and the commissioners; leadership coaching for the appointee; and a retention package for the four flight-risk consultants. The four had to total exactly GBP 350,000.

Week eight would bring three things at once: two unit consultants objecting formally to the appointment, one complex case with a poor outcome bearing on the submission, and a commissioner requesting a meeting. Ashworth could affirm the director's authority publicly, take them into that meeting and route the objections back through them; she could take the meeting herself and tell the consultants the board was handling it; or she could stand back and let the director handle week eight alone. The case could be reviewed and signed by the director, kept out of the submission narrative, or audited externally on the board's instruction. The two objectors could be met by the director, met privately by the board and assured the director's remit would be constrained, or treated as within normal range and given no formal reply. She also had to say, as a percentage, how firmly the board would hold its line on the director's authority if pressed.

On 15 May the board would make a statement to the lead NHS commissioner and the consultant body. It could defend the structure — the credential, the management capacity supplied alongside it, and projections for outcomes, retention and ranking; it could defend the appointee's name and record, without the structure around it; or it could state that the submission had never been at risk, and keep retention and the week-eight case out of the statement. The board could commit to an eighteen-month horizon, to the 15 May window alone, or to a guaranteed top-decile ranking with no further departures. It could lead on the credibility index, on the registry ranking alone, or on the discipline with which the GBP 350,000 had been spent. And Ashworth had to fix, again as a percentage, how far she would hold that position under commissioner pressure.

## Closing

At 5:20 p.m. Ashworth wrote two names on the whiteboard, drew a line under them, and added a third row for what went beneath it. Voss left in twelve weeks. The registry closed in fourteen. She had GBP 350,000, four consultants she had not yet spoken to, and a board meeting on Monday.
