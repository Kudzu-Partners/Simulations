# Linnea Industrial Software: The Flex Effect

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1881 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1881 — *Flex Effect* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 3 February 2026, Elena Roig read the overnight resignation count a second time before she believed it. Eleven engineers in Linnea Industrial Software's Barcelona product-engineering group had quit in the past three weeks, each citing the same reason: a local competitor had just gone public with a four-day-friendly hybrid model, and Linnea's own rules looked rigid by comparison. The pulse survey her team had fielded that week put a number on what was left behind: 38 percent of the Barcelona unit said they were actively considering leaving within the year.

Roig was Linnea's HR Director for both business units, and the walkout reached her desk two hours before her first meeting with the CEO. The CEO's ask was direct: design a flexible-work policy covering Barcelona and Linnea's other unit in Munich, launch it within the quarter, and do it on a single fixed budget of €1,200,000 for both units combined. If Barcelona's numbers were not moving in the right direction within two quarters of launch, the CEO had already said what would replace her design: one identical return-to-office policy, applied to both units regardless of what either actually needed.

## The Protagonist

Roig, 45, had joined Linnea's Barcelona unit in 2014 as a human-resources business partner inside product engineering — the same group now losing people. She had spent the years since rebuilding the unit's compensation bands and, more recently, running a well-regarded internal-mobility programme, but she had never designed a flexible-work policy that had to hold across two countries under one number. In 2023, when Linnea combined its Barcelona and Munich HR functions under a single leader for the first time, the job had gone to her. She reported to the CEO directly and, twice a quarter, to a board that had approved exactly one figure for what came next.

## The Decision

Before choosing anything, Roig's team had to agree on what each unit's data actually meant. Was Barcelona's exodus a pay problem, best answered by quickly matching the rival's advertised offer; an engagement gap, answered with new programming for a workforce that still liked the work; eleven resignations concentrated in the product-engineering group rather than spread across the wider unit; or the daily friction of rigid hours and a long commute that flexibility could remove directly? Munich's data pointed the other way: did the unit need the same rigid-hours fix Barcelona was getting; did 9 percent turnover, low but not zero, call for a direct retention bonus; did an already-autonomous workforce need some new source of energy to lift engagement off 61; or, with almost nobody leaving, did the unit sit outside the redesign entirely? How sharply her team drew that line, before a euro was spent, was its own decision.

Only after diagnosis came design. Three policy shapes were available to either unit. Structured hybrid — a fixed weekly pattern of office and remote days that left less day-to-day discretion than flextime — carried internal evidence of adding real energy back to people's working lives rather than only removing friction, and cost Barcelona €520,000. Flextime, cheapest of the three at €220,000, let employees shift their own start and finish times inside the same five-day week, for a projected effect on the retention numbers. The compressed four-day week, the design the competitor had just adopted and the one Barcelona's own soundings rated most popular, cost the most at €600,000 and raised the sharpest coverage questions for roles that had to staff every day. Munich faced the same three shapes at different prices and fit: hybrid, laying a fixed office-remote rhythm over consultants who already ran their own diaries and had not asked for one, at €480,000; the four-day week, which would need consultants to clear it against client-site schedules they did not fully control, at €560,000; flextime, or outcome-based autonomy layered on top of what the unit already had, cheapest at €200,000; or Munich could receive whatever policy Barcelona was given instead, collapsing both units' designs into one identical rollout. Whichever combination she chose had to fit inside the same €1.2 million, together with however much — zero to €400,000 — she committed up front to preparing managers to lead people they would now see less of (Exhibit 2).

For the roughly 40 percent of both units — client-site consultants and round-the-clock support staff — who could not be made flexible under any of the three designs, Roig had to write the rule that governed them: extend flexible hours to every client-site or round-the-clock role where the schedule could realistically absorb the change; write a stated fairness principle for why those roles differed, paired with an alternative such as shift-swap autonomy or a predictable rota; or write a plain eligibility list that named who it excluded but gave no stated rationale.

## The Company and its Setting

Linnea Industrial Software, founded in Barcelona in 2004, employed 1,450 people across two business units that had grown into opposites. The Barcelona unit — 820 people, younger, collaborative, built around shared office space — delivered product engineering and customer success. The Munich unit — 630 people, older, accustomed to working independently at client sites — delivered industrial-controls consulting. Together the two units produced €198 million in annual revenue at a 13 percent operating margin (Exhibit 1). Whatever policy Roig chose would land on both of them, and nothing guaranteed it would land the same way twice.

## The Situation

The numbers Roig inherited were already organised as a dashboard. A Barcelona retention index — tracking whether the 38-percent-considering-leaving problem was reversing — opened the quarter at 25 out of 100. A Munich engagement score sat at 61, thirteen points under the unit's 74-point target. An eligibility-fairness index, tracking how the roughly 40 percent who could not flex felt about the process itself, opened at 40. A running count against the €1.2 million cap opened at zero — a cap that covered just over 60 percent of what the two units' own first-choice programmes would have cost fully funded, near €1.95 million.

She expected the design to be tested after launch, not only once at the start. Managers used to judging attendance rather than output would have to learn to lead teams they saw less of. Staff who could not be made eligible — because a client needed someone on-site on a Friday, or a support line needed coverage overnight — would need an answer they could live with, or the resentment would surface as the same turnover-intention problem the policy was meant to fix. And at least one client, used to the coverage Linnea had always provided, would notice the first time that coverage changed.

## What Roig Controlled

Each stage of the quarter asked for a different layer of the same design. First came the two-unit diagnosis above. Second came the policy per unit, the eligibility rule, and the up-front investment in manager readiness, all drawn from the one shared budget. Third, whatever surfaced once the policy was live — a manager who said he could not lead a team he could not see, a fairness grievance from the round-the-clock staff the policy did not cover, a client questioning its own Friday coverage — she would have to answer without reopening the budget: how much more to invest in manager capability, how to respond to the grievance, and how to protect the client relationship while keeping the policy intact. Fourth, at quarter's end, she would decide what to put in front of the CEO: which numbers to report, how often to check them again, and how plainly to describe what the €1.2 million had bought and what she had chosen, deliberately, not to fund.

## Closing

At eight in the morning, with the CEO meeting still two hours off, Roig opened a blank document and titled it *Flexible Work Policy — Q2*. Barcelona needed something removed. Munich needed something added that nobody had yet put a price on. The two units' own first choices ran well past what the board had approved, and the CEO had already named what would replace her design if the numbers had not moved by the two-quarter mark. She started with the one line both unit heads had already signed: the cap was €1,200,000, and it was not going to move.
