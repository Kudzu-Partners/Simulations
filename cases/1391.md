# Helio Verde Beverages: Five Charts Before the Board

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1391 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1391 — *Data Visualisation Lab — Show, Do Not Decorate* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, April 13, 2026, at 08:40, Valentina Quiroga-Mena opened an email that had not been meant for her. It carried a memo from Inés Garrido-Lobos, chief financial officer (CFO) of Helio Verde Beverages, to the executive committee, written after the previous week's quarterly review and copied to the whole analytics function by an assistant's slip. The passage that mattered ran to three sentences: *"Half the slides last week used pie charts where bars would have worked better. The 3D effects added nothing. The colour-coded map lost the board members who cannot read red against green."*

By 10:00 the memo had become an assignment. The analytics function was to take the five worst charts from the April pack, redesign each one, and turn the principles behind the redesigns into a one-page standard for its 35 analysts before the July review, twelve weeks out. Quiroga-Mena was asked to produce the prototype set in a single working session: five redesigns, one chart at a time, in the order of the April pack. Garrido-Lobos would score each one against a checklist of her own as it reached her.

## The Protagonist

Quiroga-Mena, twenty-seven, had joined Helio Verde eighteen months earlier from an economics degree and prepared the regional sales reviews on the commercial analytics desk. Like most of the function's analysts, she had learned charting in an introductory statistics course and a spreadsheet module, and built what her managers asked for in the format the corporate template offered. Two of the five flagged charts were hers. She had defended her numbers many times. She had never been asked to defend a chart's design.

## The Decision

For each of the five charts, Quiroga-Mena had to settle six things, one for each line of the CFO's checklist: the form of the chart; how much decoration stayed on the page; the visual channel that carried the key quantity; the colours; how the axis, or on the map the colour scale, was drawn; and what the title and labels said.

Andrés Peñaloza, the senior analyst who had built three of the originals, had already written to her that the board had read these charts this way for six years and would not thank anyone for surprises.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde Beverages, S.A. was the soft-drinks division of the diversified Helio Verde consumer-goods group. In 2025 it reported revenue of about US$410 million, employed 1,600 people across two bottling plants and a national distribution network, and held an estimated 44% of carbonated soft-drink volume in its home market (see Exhibit 1). Its analysts sat across commercial, finance, supply chain and marketing, and between them produced an estimated 600 to 800 charts a quarter for the board pack, the executive committee and the regional reviews. Most were recent graduates. None had been taught chart design as a way of communicating.

Garrido-Lobos had trained as a quantitative analyst at Larkmoor and had spent three quarters growing impatient with the charts put in front of her. She had read the classic literature on statistical graphics and had begun to quote it in meetings. The board included two members in their late sixties; one had told the chair that he could not tell red from green, the most common form of colour blindness, which affects roughly one man in twelve.

## The Situation

The five charts sat in the April pack exactly as the board had seen them (see Exhibit 2). The first, revenue by region, was a tilted 3D pie of five slices in five brand colours, its percentages printed in white inside the slices. The board had to rank the regions; nobody could say which of the second and third slices was larger. The second, twelve months of sales, used 3D columns with shadows, a different colour for each month, gridlines every five units and a shaded background; the board needed the year-over-year trend and the seasonal pattern. The third showed the mix of three products over five years as stacked areas in three bright colours. Only the bottom band sat on a flat baseline, and the board had argued over whether the middle product was growing. The fourth was a map of sales growth by province, shaded on a continuous scale from saturated red through white to saturated green; to the colour-blind board member both ends looked a similar brown. The fifth plotted margin against volume for 60 products, each point labelled with its code, the labels overlapping into a grey mass and the premium products marked in a colour nobody could pick out. Its purpose was to find the few products that were high-margin and low-volume, or the reverse.

The numbers were fixed; only the design could change. Garrido-Lobos's sheet scored each of the six items from zero to three, eighteen points a chart. It carried three running figures across the set: a points total, at zero before the first redesign; the share of available points earned; and a one-word record of her reaction, which began at *skeptical*. If the analysts re-ran the templates, the five charts would reach the July board as they had reached April's.

## What Quiroga-Mena Controlled

**The form of each chart.** For revenue by region she could choose a sorted horizontal bar, a flat pie, an unsorted vertical bar, or the 3D pie as it stood. For monthly sales: flat columns, a single-series area, a line with the prior year as a shaded reference, small multiples with one panel per year, or the 3D columns. For the product mix: three lines overlaid, a 100% stacked bar, small multiples with one line per product, one pie per year, or the stacked areas. For the provinces: a plain bar chart without a map, a bubble map, the shaded map, or a pie on each province. For the 60 products: a sortable table, a heatmap of margin bands against volume bands, the full scatter, a pie of margin contribution, or 60 bars.

**The decoration.** Keep it all; remove some while keeping gridlines and borders; or strip everything that did not display data. On the scatter the same question took the form of the point labels: every code, the premium products only, or the five outliers only.

**The visual channel.** What the reader's eye would decode the quantity from: length or position along a common axis, angle, area, colour, or the volume of a 3D shape. On the map the choice was a number printed on each province, the province's area scaled to its value, or its fill colour; on the scatter, colour by premium status, bubble size by revenue, or position on the two axes.

**The colours.** Each chart offered its own set: the brand palette, or a colour per month, product or point; red and green; a sequential palette that changed evenly in lightness (viridis); a single neutral colour, a dark line with light reference shading, or semi-transparent dots; three hues chosen to remain distinct to colour-blind readers; and, for the map, red against green in five classes or a red-blue diverging palette in five.

**The axis.** Start at zero across the full range; truncate it so that differences or seasonality looked larger; hide it altogether; start above zero with the break labelled; give each small panel its own scale, or share one scale across them. On the scatter the axes could be cropped close around the cloud, held tight to the data at both ends, or started at zero. On the map the axis was the colour scale: a continuous gradient, or three, five or seven classes.

**The labels.** A title naming the chart type, a title naming the topic or the product, or a title stating the argument with axes, units and a source line. On the map: no legend, a legend, or a legend with direct labels on the largest provinces. On the scatter: every point labelled, a legend only, or the outliers named with connector lines.

## Closing

At 10:15 Quiroga-Mena pulled the April revenue pie onto one screen and a blank chart onto the other. Peñaloza's note sat in her inbox, unanswered, and Garrido-Lobos's checklist sat beside it, six lines long. The first redesign would reach the CFO before lunch, and four more would follow by evening.
