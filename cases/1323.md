# Costaria Office of Higher Education Research: The Line at Seventy-Five

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1323 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1323 — *Regression Discontinuity — A Scholarship Cutoff Tale* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Wednesday, April 22, 2026, Lucía Arbeloa-Santaner, senior econometrician at the Costaria Office of Higher Education Research (OCIES), read a Ministry of Education memo that had reached the morning papers. It was marked *Pre-Decisional — Restricted Distribution* and quoted a note from the Ministry's Programs Directorate. Recipients of the Costaria National Merit Scholarship (CNMS) graduated at 78%, non-recipients at 62%, and the scholarship therefore "adds 16 percentage points." The Vice-Minister for Higher Education had already been given that number informally. She now wanted one that would survive external review, in time for the budget cabinet of Tuesday, May 19.

The Director of OCIES, Ernesto Valcárcel-Imaz, gave the file to Arbeloa-Santaner and set four review passes before the memo left the building (see **Exhibit 2**). At each pass she would settle one stage of the estimate, and he would return it with a referee's comments and a credibility score. What she filed after the fourth pass would be the Office's answer to the Vice-Minister.

## The Protagonist

Arbeloa-Santaner was thirty-seven and had spent six years at OCIES, the last three as its senior econometrician. She had come from a doctorate in labour economics and two years in the evaluation unit of a regional development lender. She had written the Office's evaluations of a student-loan reform and of a rural teacher bonus. She had never evaluated a program whose effect a Vice-Minister had already quoted, and none of her earlier files had been in the newspapers while she worked on them.

## The Decision

She had to decide, pass by pass, how to produce a first estimate of the scholarship's effect on graduation; what, if anything, separated that first estimate from the effect itself; how to specify the estimate she would put her name to; and which checks the memo would carry. The Director would score every pass. The Ministry would read only the final number and its caveats.

## The Company and its Setting

OCIES was a 28-person research unit reporting jointly to the Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Finance. Its mandate was to produce defensible evidence on what worked in Costarian higher-education policy, and to tell the Ministry in writing, on deadline, when a popular program did not do what it claimed.

The Office maintained the Costarian Higher-Education Longitudinal Register (CHELR). It linked every student who sat the national university-entrance exam, the Examen Nacional de Acceso Universitario (ENAU), to their score, scholarship status, university enrolment, background and graduation record.

The CNMS had been created in 2013 and first awarded to the 2014 ENAU cohort. It paid CRP 8,000 (Costarian pesos) per academic year for up to four years, conditional on continued enrolment. That covered tuition at most public universities and part of a student's living costs. Eligibility had a single rule: an ENAU score of 75 or more out of 100. A student who scored 75 received the award; a student who scored 74 received nothing, and no committee or appeal changed that. The Ministry had placed the line at 75 because the budget covered roughly the top 30% of test-takers. The exam was set, administered and marked centrally. In 2026 the scholarship cost the Treasury about CRP 240 million a year (see **Exhibit 1**).

## The Situation

The analysis file held 142,000 ENAU-takers from the 2014–2018 cohorts, the years for which graduation within six years could be observed. Scores were whole numbers from 0 to 100. Besides score, award and graduation, each record carried family income, parental education, prior secondary-school grades, region and type of secondary school.

The Programs Directorate's figure came from the simplest comparison the file allowed: 78% of recipients had graduated within six years, against 62% of non-recipients. With 142,000 students, the standard error on that 16-point gap was a fraction of a percentage point. Nobody at OCIES disputed the arithmetic.

If the Office sent nothing, or sent the same figure back, the 16 points would go to cabinet as the Ministry's own estimate. The CNMS budget line would be settled on May 19 on that number. Rodrigo Mancheño-Tirado, deputy director of the Programs Directorate and the author of the note, had phoned the Director on the day of the leak. The figure, he said, was simple, public and correct, and the Directorate expected OCIES to confirm it. Valcárcel-Imaz had offered no view to him or to Arbeloa-Santaner. He had asked only to see each pass.

He kept the record of the work on a review sheet. Each pass logged the method used, the estimated effect on six-year graduation in percentage points, and the estimate's distance, in percentage points, from a reference estimate the Office's methods unit had produced independently. Beside them sat a credibility score out of 100, which started at zero and was revised at every pass, and a count of referee checks run, out of four. At the close, the Director would place the file in one of three bands.

## What Arbeloa-Santaner Controlled

At the first pass she chose the **first-pass estimator**. She could compare the graduation rates of recipients and non-recipients directly. Or she could run a least-squares regression of graduation on scholarship receipt, with family income, region, parental education and type of secondary school as controls.

At the second pass she gave her **diagnosis** of the first number, choosing among four readings the Director put to her. The gap was sampling noise, and the raw comparison should stand. The ENAU score itself was the confounder, with an ability gradient in graduation driving the gap. The fix was to add the score as one more control in the regression. Or the standard errors should be widened and the estimate resubmitted.

At the third pass she specified the **estimate at the 75-point line** in four parts. The **window**: how many ENAU points on either side of 75 the estimate would draw on, anywhere from 1 to 40. The **shape of the fit** on each side of the line: a straight line fitted locally, a quadratic curve fitted locally, or a single fourth-order polynomial fitted across the whole range of scores, as much published work before 2010 had done. The **weighting** of students inside the window: triangular weights that fell off in a straight line with distance from 75, equal weight for every student, or the rounded Epanechnikov taper. And the **standard errors**: conventional ones, or bias-corrected robust ones built to account for the error a local fit made at the line.

At the fourth pass she chose the **checks** the memo would carry, and each one carried would have to be defended in writing. There were four. A test of whether the density of scores jumped at 75, the signature of students or markers pushing scores over the line. A test of whether family income, parental education and prior grades were continuous across the line. A table of the estimate at different window widths. And a table of the estimate under different shapes of fit.

## Closing

At 18:10 Arbeloa-Santaner opened the register's query window and typed the first line of the extract: every ENAU-taker from 2014 to 2018, with score, award and graduation. The Directorate's 16 points were in the newspaper and in the Vice-Minister's notes. The first pass with Valcárcel-Imaz was on Monday morning, and the cabinet would meet in less than four weeks.
