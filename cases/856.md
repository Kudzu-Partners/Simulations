# Andina Logistics: Eighteen Days to Quintero

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-856 · **Companion simulation:** Express 856 — *Pacific-Andean Corridor — International Logistics Colombia-Peru-Chile* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Thursday, April 30, 2026, Andrés Galvis Mora had six days to get 4,200 tonnes of specialty-chemical intermediates moving out of Bogotá. The cargo was bound for PetroQuímica Sur S.A., a processor in Quintero, on the Chilean coast north of Santiago, and it was sized to refill the plant's strategic inventory. If it was not on PetroQuímica's line by day 18 of the contract clock, the plant would idle and Andina Logistics would pay USD 9,500 for every day late.

Galvis, Andina's international logistics manager, had to commit to a route and a schedule buffer before the bookings closed, and then hold the plan together through dispatch, three customs services and a steering-committee review. The shipment was worth USD 38,000 in margin to Andina. Four days late would erase it.

## The Protagonist

Galvis was forty-one, an industrial engineer who had spent eight years planning ocean freight for a container line in Cartagena before joining Andina in 2018. He ran the corridor desk that booked Andina's Pacific cargo from Colombia to Peru and Chile, and he had moved PetroQuímica's cargo a dozen times. He had never moved this much of it at once under a penalty clause.

## The Decision

Galvis would take the plan through four sittings of the operations team between booking and the steering committee. At the first he chose the primary route and how many days of buffer to build into the schedule. At the second he chose how to respond to whatever the corridor had done since booking. At the third he set how the customs filings moved across three countries and whether to ask PetroQuímica for help and for time. At the fourth he locked the routing, the trigger for a fallback and the single figure he would put before the committee.

## The Company and its Setting

Andina Logistics was a Bogotá freight forwarder founded in 2011 by two managers from a global forwarding firm. It had offices in Bogotá, Buenaventura, Lima and Santiago, agents in Tumaco and San Antonio, 64 employees, and 2025 revenue of USD 18.4 million at a net margin of 4.7%. It owned no ships and no trucks; it made or lost its margin on routing and on customs time. PetroQuímica Sur was its largest client, 22% of revenue, and its 2026 contract wrote in the day-18 deadline, the daily penalty and the right to terminate once a delivery ran seven days late (see Exhibit 2).

The corridor crossed three customs services: Colombia's DIAN, Peru's SUNAT and Chile's National Customs Service. Under the Pacific Alliance the three shared a single window for foreign-trade filings, known by its Spanish initials as VUCE, which accepted submissions either in parallel or passed from one country to the next. The Alliance also ran a mutual-recognition lane for Authorized Economic Operators (AEO) that moved certified cargo through inspection faster, but only when both shipper and consignee held the status. PetroQuímica was certified in Chile. The Colombian shipper was not.

## The Situation

Galvis's team had priced three routes (see Exhibit 1). The first sailed from Buenaventura, Colombia's main Pacific port, with transshipment at Callao in Peru, to San Antonio, the Chilean port closest to Quintero: 14 days and USD 42 a tonne, on the most frequent sailings. The second sailed from Tumaco, a smaller port near the Ecuadorian border, over the same Callao and San Antonio legs: 12 days and USD 64 a tonne, with fewer carriers calling. The third was multimodal: sea from Buenaventura to Callao, then 2,800 kilometres by road to Quintero, avoiding San Antonio: 16 days and USD 78 a tonne, with mountain weather and a border crossing on the way.

The USD 38,000 margin had been priced on the Buenaventura route. PetroQuímica's price was fixed, so every extra dollar a tonne across 4,200 tonnes came out of Andina's side. The operations file listed the known risks. Buenaventura's main terminal had been in labour talks for three weeks. Without certification on the Colombian side, any inspection in Chile would run at the standard pace.

Andina's playbook for a disrupted Pacific leg listed four responses. Wait out a port queue. Divert to Tumaco, at about USD 22 a tonne more, on weekly rather than thrice-weekly sailings. Switch to the multimodal road corridor, at about USD 36 a tonne more. Or fly a 600-tonne bridge to Quintero, at about USD 480,000, to keep the plant running while the bulk went by sea.

If Galvis booked the cheapest route with no buffer and did nothing more, the cargo was scheduled to land four days inside the deadline, and the whole margin rested on the corridor running exactly to plan. Each day past day 18 would cost USD 9,500.

## What Galvis Controlled

At the first sitting he chose the primary route from the three priced options and set a schedule buffer of zero to five days; the planning team's working figure was two.

At the second sitting, with whatever had happened in the corridor since booking on the table, he chose the response from the playbook: wait, divert to Tumaco, go multimodal by road, or fly the 600-tonne bridge.

At the third sitting, with the documentation freeze forty-eight hours away, he made three choices. First, how the filings to DIAN, SUNAT and Chilean customs went through the single window: in parallel, about twelve hours in all, or country by country, 24 to 72 hours per handoff. Second, whether to ask PetroQuímica, as a certified operator, to co-sign the shipment into the mutual-recognition lane, or to file as a standard shipper. Third, whether to ask for time. PetroQuímica had signalled that it would move the deadline from day 18 to day 22 in exchange for a 3% rebate on the freight rate. Galvis could decline and defend day 18, accept the rebate on its own, or offer the rebate together with a binding volume commitment for 2027. The commercial director warned that a rebate given once tended to reappear at the next tender. The operations director warned that a volume commitment tied up next year's capacity.

At the fourth sitting he locked the plan. He could keep the routing chosen at the second sitting or hedge it by splitting the shipment with a partial air bridge, at about USD 240,000. He set the fallback trigger for day 12 of transit: either defined in advance and fired by the queue forecast on day 5, or escalated ad hoc once the queue cleared or stalled. And he chose the one headline figure he would table: the probability of on-time delivery, the lowest unit cost per tonne, or the reported gross margin.

After each sitting the operations board showed four figures. The first was the margin Andina expected to keep on the shipment, in US dollars, after extra freight, air costs, rebates and expected penalties. The second was the probability that the cargo reached Quintero by the deadline in force. The third was the expected transit in days, and the fourth the landed cost in US dollars per tonne. A round log recorded the routing in force and each of those figures. The chief financial officer, Marcela Rueda Ibáñez, would chair the steering committee. She would see where the probability and the margin had ended, and hear which figure Galvis chose to lead with.

## Closing

At 4:30 that afternoon the carrier desk in Buenaventura called to say that booking for the next sailing closed at midnight on Sunday. Rodrigo Fuenzalida, PetroQuímica's supply manager in Quintero, had already asked twice for a vessel name. Galvis had the three routes on one sheet and a blank line for the buffer.
