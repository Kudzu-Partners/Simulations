# Andicorp Manufactura: Make, Buy, or Bend

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-2003 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2003 — *Make, Buy, or Bend* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On Monday, 9 March 2026, Elena Zúñiga Bardales walked the length of the tooling shop at Lurín before the first shift and counted the machines that were not turning. The shop cost Andicorp Manufactura S.A.C. S/ 11 million a year in fixed cost and ran at roughly 60 per cent of capacity. The previous Friday the board had frozen growth capital and instructed her to take 15 per cent — approximately S/ 47 million — out of the company's operating cost within two quarters, without losing the quality edge that won its contract-manufacturing renewals.

Five months from that morning, the multinational that bought S/ 166 million of contract manufacturing from Andicorp each year — 32 per cent of revenue — would decide whether to renew. Its procurement team had tabled a clause covering both cost and service level, and a contract manufacturer in Mexico was quoting it 8 per cent below Andicorp's price. Zúñiga had two quarters to find the S/ 47 million and five months to give that client a reason to stay.

## The Protagonist

Elena Zúñiga Bardales, 49, had been chief executive of Andicorp since 2022 and its chief operating officer for six years before that. She was the first CEO appointed from outside the Velarde family, which had founded the firm in 2004 and still held the chair. Her standing inside Andicorp rested on the Arequipa line commissioning of 2019, brought in under budget because she had refused to buy capacity she could not fill. For three years she had argued, without much traction, that "we make almost everything ourselves" described a habit rather than a plan.

## The Decision

Zúñiga had to decide which of Andicorp's functions the company would keep making, which it would buy from someone else, and which it would restructure into some third shape — and she had to decide it against a cost mandate, a bank covenant and a client renewal that all fell due inside the same two quarters.

The work came in a sequence. First she had to classify the functions before any of them had a saving attached: which genuinely differentiated Andicorp and were paid for by customers, which were necessary but undifferentiated, and which were valuable and fragile at once. She also had to judge how far she trusted that classification — whether she was separating what felt core from what was core, or ratifying what the company already believed. Then she had to turn it into decisions and a number, spend a small fixed resilience budget, and defend the whole of it in front of the client and the board.

## The Company and its Setting

Andicorp Manufactura S.A.C. was a mid-market consumer-durables manufacturer headquartered in the Lurín industrial park south of Lima, with a second plant in Arequipa. Founded in 2004 by the Velarde family, it made small kitchen appliances under its own *Hogarsa* brand and manufactured under contract for two multinationals. Revenue was S/ 520 million a year at a 12 per cent EBITDA margin, roughly S/ 62 million. It employed 1,850 people.

Three cost blocks sat outside the production lines themselves (see **Exhibit 2**). The in-house tooling and mold shop was the origin of Andicorp's reputation for quality and of the speed with which it turned a client's design change into a producible part. It was also its most expensive fixed-cost unit, and the function competitors most often bought in. Two motor SKUs, critical to a mid-range appliance family and to the anchor client's line, came from a single Asian supplier on an 11-week lead time; a stockout would idle a line at a cost of S/ 180,000 a day in lost contribution. And a block of support functions — a 28-truck owned distribution fleet, an internal IT team, and a 60-seat customer-service centre in Lima — carried fixed cost without touching what any customer bought.

About 70 per cent of inputs — resins, electronics, motors — arrived through Callao.

## The Situation

In the first quarter of 2026 a sharp depreciation of the sol and a nine-week congestion backlog at Callao pushed the landed cost of imported inputs up 22 per cent, while a softening domestic economy cut appliance demand.

The arithmetic was set out in **Exhibit 1**. On unchanged operations, the EBITDA margin fell from 12 per cent to a projected 7.5 per cent, taking roughly S/ 24 million of annual profit with it. Andicorp's bank facility carried a covenant capping net debt at 3.0 times EBITDA. The ratio stood at 2.9 times. On the projected margin it moved toward 3.4 times, and two missed quarters would trigger a covenant review and a rating downgrade. Nothing in that chain required a further shock; it required only that the company continue as it was.

The board's instruction was 15 per cent out of operating cost, roughly S/ 47 million, in two quarters, with growth capital frozen. The board had not said where the 15 per cent should come from. Fernando Velarde had said, at the end of Friday's meeting, that the tooling shop was what the family had built the company on.

## What Zúñiga Controlled

Her first move was the classification: tooling shop, motor SKUs, and the fleet-IT-and-call-centre block, each filed as a core capability worth protecting, as commodity context to be variabilised, or as strategic-but-fragile — needing restructuring rather than a simple keep-or-cut.

Her second was the shape of the cut and the shape of each function. She could apply a flat 15 per cent to everything, protect the differentiator at zero and take disproportionately more from context, or defend the fixed-cost base and trim discretionary spend. For the tooling shop and for the support block she chose among three settings: *make*, keeping it in-house and unchanged; *buy*, outsourcing it — quoted at about S/ 4.5 million a year in the tooling shop's case; or *bend*, a partial move such as a nearshore co-development partner taking overflow while Andicorp kept the design-iteration work, or outsourcing the call centre while keeping the trucks. She also had to decide what to put in front of the anchor client: match the Mexican quote, offer a contractual design-iteration service level backed by whatever tooling capability she had retained, or offer nothing specific and rely on the relationship.

Her third was a resilience budget of S/ 6 million, and every sol of it was a sol not booked against the S/ 47 million. She could qualify a second source for the two motor SKUs for S/ 2.5 million; hold S/ 1.5 million of safety stock, covering part of a six-week interruption, or S/ 2 million, covering all of it; retain a nearshore tooling partner for S/ 1.5 million to stand behind a service-level guarantee; or spend S/ 4 million dual-sourcing and buffering every input as a blanket policy. The budget did not stretch to all of it.

Her fourth was the account she gave. She could lead the client and board presentation with the cost number alone, with the full picture of cost, resilience, capability guarantees and the covenant path, or with the relationship. She had to commit to an operating model for 12 to 18 months — re-fixing the cost base once the sol recovered, holding a deliberately variabilised base, or outsourcing as much as possible permanently — and to name what she would report quarterly: the savings figure, the EBITDA margin, or a full suite covering savings, margin, the covenant ratio and an index of capability and resilience.

Four numbers followed her through all of it: annualised savings booked against the S/ 47 million target; the EBITDA margin against 12 per cent and the 7.5 per cent no-action case; net debt to EBITDA against the 3.0 times covenant; and an internal 0–100 index of design-iteration speed and supply resilience, which stood at 70. Alongside them ran the procurement team's own reading of renewal risk, which in March was high.

## Closing

At 06:40 Zúñiga went up to her office, wrote the three function names on one sheet of paper, and drew a column beside them for what each one was actually worth. The board wanted S/ 47 million in two quarters. The renewal decision was five months out. The tooling shop was still running at 60 per cent.
