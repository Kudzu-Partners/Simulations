# Horizon Apparel: A Year Read Against the Record

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-793 · **Companion simulation:** Express 793 — *The Ethical Compass: CSR, Profit and Doing the Right Thing* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, March 3, 2026, Rebecca Thornfield, chief executive of Horizon Apparel, read the audit of the company's second-tier suppliers on the train from London to Manchester. One factory in Dhaka, Bangladesh, which made knitwear and jersey for Horizon through a first-tier agent, paid its workers well below a living wage and ran them on twelve-hour shifts. The auditors had found no child labour. They had found wages that did not meet International Labour Organization guidance.

The board would meet on Thursday, March 12, and Thornfield had nine days to put a recommendation in front of it. It would be the first of five calls she expected to make that year. The last would be the company's annual corporate-responsibility report, which the chair had already told her would be read against everything Horizon had actually done in the meantime.

## The Protagonist

Thornfield was fifty-one. She had spent twenty years in clothing retail, most of them in buying and merchandising, and had been chief executive of Horizon for two years. She knew the supplier base by name and had visited Dhaka twice. She had not, until that morning, seen the second tier of the chain in writing.

## The Decision

Across 2026 Thornfield had to settle five questions, one at a time and in a fixed order (see Exhibit 2): what to do about the Dhaka factory; whether and how to move the range to recycled packaging; whether to adopt a lawful tax structure that would cut the company's bill; how to handle a written complaint from inside the company's own quality team; and how to describe Horizon's position on responsibility in the annual report. Each call was made once. None could be taken back, and each would be on the record the report was read against.

## The Company and its Setting

Horizon Apparel was a UK mid-market clothing brand, headquartered in Manchester, with about 2,100 employees, 74 stores and a website that took just over a third of its orders. Its customers were value-conscious but not bargain-hunters: they paid for fit and durability and, increasingly, asked where the clothes were made. Annual profit had held at about £10 million for three years.

The sector had been under scrutiny for a decade. In 2025 a competitor had been exposed by a national newspaper for sourcing from factories with similar pay and hours, and the fallout had been severe: lost wholesale accounts, a boycott campaign and a year of headlines. Investors, campaign groups, the company's own employees and its customers all watched the sector's choices now, and they did not want the same things.

## The Situation

The Dhaka factory could be replaced. A living-wage certified supplier had quoted for the same work at unit costs 18% higher. The current supplier had offered an eighteen-month improvement plan with phased wage increases and an independent audit, at 8% more. The head of sourcing, Farhan Chowdhury, pointed out that the company could also keep the factory and monitor it, at minimal cost, or change nothing at all and protect the margin. The finance director, Martin Oduya, reminded the executive team that the £10 million profit line was the figure shareholders looked at first.

Behind the supplier question, the rest of the year's agenda was already visible. The marketing director, Sophie Lanark, had a proposal to move every pack to 100% recycled material at £0.40 more per unit. Her consumer research said 38% of customers would pay a small premium for sustainable packaging; the finance team believed fewer would pay in practice than had said so in a survey. A competitor had just launched a heavily advertised recycled-packaging line. Oduya's team, for its part, was preparing a tax structure that would route UK profits through an Irish holding company. It was lawful and would save £2.1 million a year. An investigative journalist at a national paper had been asking questions about tax arrangements in the sector.

Thornfield's position at the start of the year sat on three dials (see Exhibit 1). Annual profit stood at £10 million. The brand-reputation index, built from customer, media and campaign-group tracking, stood at 50 out of 100. And the board's ethics committee kept what it called the moral compass, a running score from −100, where every call favoured profit, to +100, where every call favoured ethics. It stood at 0.

If Thornfield did nothing, the arithmetic of the first quarter was simple. Profit would be untouched, the Dhaka factory would keep paying what it paid on the shifts it ran, and the audit would stay in the company's files. What anyone outside the company would make of it, if they ever saw it, nobody on the executive team could say.

## What Thornfield Controlled

In March, for the Dhaka factory, she could switch to the living-wage certified supplier at 18% higher unit cost; negotiate the eighteen-month improvement plan with the current supplier at 8% more; audit and monitor without changing supplier, at minimal cost; or do nothing and focus on margin.

For packaging, she could move the entire range to recycled packaging at £0.40 more per unit; switch only the premium line first, as a pilot; keep the current packaging and market it as eco-friendly; or change nothing and wait to see what rivals did.

For tax, she could accept the full structure and the £2.1 million saving; reject it and pay full UK corporation tax; or take a partial route, moving some activity offshore and keeping the rest in the UK.

In the autumn a junior quality-assurance analyst would report in writing that a senior production manager had been inflating quality-test results to hit deadlines. The manager had been with Horizon for eleven years and was well connected; the analyst asked for anonymity and protection. Thornfield could order a formal investigation and protect the analyst; investigate quietly without a formal process; ignore the report and trust the manager; or quietly fix the testing problem without investigating the report.

For the annual report, the board asked her to state where Horizon stood. She could take the position that the company's responsibility was to its shareholders, within the law; a pragmatic balance, aware of every stakeholder but grounded in profit; a claim of fully integrated responsibility, running from profit and the law through ethics to philanthropy; or an honest account that named the trade-offs the company had made and the ones it had not.

After each decision the board would see four reactions, from shareholders, employees, customers and the community and campaign groups, each ranging from strongly negative to strongly positive. The company's decision log would record what she chose and how it moved profit in pounds, the reputation index and the compass. At the end of the year the chair would rate her record as a whole, on a scale out of 100.

## Closing

The train pulled into Manchester Piccadilly at 10:52. Thornfield closed the audit, then opened it again at the page with the wage table and the shift rota. Chowdhury had asked for an answer by Friday so he could brief the agent in Dhaka before the next order was placed. The board met in nine days.
