# Vélocité Logistique: The Lyon Mega-Hub Merger

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1866 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1866 — *Lead by Example* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 8 June 2026, Camille Ferrand, the newly appointed integration manager for Vélocité Logistique's Lyon mega-hub, watched the first shift of the merged operation come apart. Three hundred and sixty employees from Vélocité and two hundred and forty from its larger northern rival, Septentrion Transport, had reported to the same building for the first time that morning, and within hours the two groups were hoarding dock equipment, missing handoffs, and blaming each other for undelivered routes. By the end of the first 72 hours, on-time dispatch had fallen from 96% to 81%. Ferrand had six weeks to get it back to 95% and ninety days to remove 70 of the hub's 600 roles for the promised synergy. She had to decide, that week, what was actually broken and what to do about it.

## The Protagonist

Ferrand, 41, had spent twelve years at Septentrion Transport before the merger board asked her to run the combined Lyon hub — a workforce that was hers to unite but that, on the ground, she barely knew half of. She had approved keeping Septentrion's dispatch software rather than running a bake-off, judging a mid-peak switch too risky, and of the first two promotions she signed off that week, both went to people she had worked with at Septentrion. Neither decision had felt like a choice between legacies; each had felt like the obvious call.

## The Decision

Ferrand's task ran in four stages. First, she had to work out what was actually breaking the operation. Second, she had to design the first six weeks of communication and choose where to spend a limited set of visible fairness moves across two unevenly trusting workforces. Third, when the 70-role cut landed, she had to design the process and decide what she would personally do while it happened. Fourth, she had to report the result and decide what would hold the hub together once the crisis passed.

## The Company and its Setting

Vélocité Logistique, founded in Lyon in 2003, ran a 900-vehicle last-mile delivery fleet across south-east France on EUR 210 million in revenue, a 7% operating margin, and 2,400 employees. Weeks earlier it had completed a "merger of equals" with Septentrion Transport, a larger northern rival — an arrangement both workforces understood to be an acquisition by the north. The combined group's case depended on the two workforces cooperating, and nowhere was that more exposed than at the Lyon mega-hub, where 600 employees from both legacies now shared one roof, one roster, one dispatch system.

## The Situation

By her first team meeting, the numbers were on the wall (Exhibit 1). On-time dispatch had fallen from 96% to 81% in 72 hours, against a client commitment of 95% within six weeks and an EUR 8 million e-retailer contract, renewing in eight weeks, whose exit clause would trigger if dispatch stayed below 90% through peak. A day-one survey put perceived fairness at 38 out of 100 among legacy Vélocité staff against 61 among legacy Septentrion staff — a 23-point gap tracking which legacy a person came from. Cross-team cooperation — shared equipment, clean handoffs — stood at 42 out of 100. Eighteen percent of staff intended to leave within six months, skewed toward the most experienced drivers and dispatchers, whom a roughly EUR 14,000-per-head replacement cost made hardest to lose. If nothing changed before the contract renewal, dispatch would still be under the 90% floor when the e-retailer's contract came up.

## What Camille Controlled

In her first week, Ferrand had to settle a diagnosis, and the same evidence supported four different ones. It could be a justice-and-cooperation breakdown — two distrustful workforces refusing to cooperate, with her own early decisions part of the cause. It could be an operations capacity problem — the hub was short of hands, and adding supervisors and overtime would push dispatch back up. It could be a systems problem — the new IT rollout was slowing everyone, and freezing further changes would let it settle. Or it could be a performance problem — the Vélocité teams were running slower, and staffing the best shifts with the Septentrion people who already looked stronger would fix it directly. She also had to name the fairness signal misfiring hardest: the pay gap between the two legacies' different scales, which everyone on the floor could see; the unchecked rumor mill, which had filled every silence with its own version of events; her own early calls — the retained software, the two promotions — reading as a rigged deal; or the view that fairness was not the problem at all, and the issue was purely operational. And she had to set, on a scale from a quiet personal commitment to an unmistakable public one, how visibly she would commit to modeling even-handed behavior before anything else was decided.

Over the six weeks that followed she had to choose how and when to communicate: hold silence until the redundancy plan was completely final, reasoning that certainty would prevent panic; run an honest all-hands that named the "merger of equals" fiction directly, paired with mixed-legacy listening sessions and published redundancy criteria; or send one polished all-hands memo from group headquarters, reassuring in tone and closed to hard questions. She also held a fixed ten points of high-visibility fairness signal to spend across five uses: a balanced leadership team, drawn evenly from both legacies; a shift roster designed jointly by both teams rather than handed down; equal access to the new IT training, regardless of legacy; visible consistency between her own asks and her own actions; and a headline perks package and an office refit for the integration leadership (Exhibit 2). A point spent on one use was a point not spent on another.

When the 70-role cut landed she had to design the process: transparent criteria with employee voice and support for those leaving; generous severance decided behind closed doors, no criteria shared, no voice; or all seventy cuts executed in week two to bank the synergy fast. She had to decide what she would do personally: have HR deliver the announcements, keep her leadership package in place, and lean on colleagues she already knew; deliver the news directly, share the cost visibly, and apply the criteria evenly across both legacies; or deliver some of it herself, trim a few perks, but keep the core leadership package. She had to decide whether selection would be even-handed: identical, audited selection criteria applied equally to both legacies, with the results checked afterward for balance; mostly even, but allowed to fall a little harder on the legacy whose day-one numbers looked worse; or weighted toward the legacy she trusted more, or that leadership preferred, reasoning that a stable core team mattered more than exact parity. Mid-round, when a rumor spread that cuts fell on the south, a peak-shift walkout was threatened, and a star dispatcher resigned, she had to choose: hold the line and push the shift through, dignifying neither the rumor nor the resignation; go to the floor with the audited criteria, hear the walkout's concern, and speak directly with the departing dispatcher; or send a firm memo denying the rumor and warning that a walkout would breach contract.

In the final round she had to decide how to present her scorecard to group leadership and the employee representative: lead with the dispatch number alone, and leave the fairness and cooperation story to HR to sort out; claim in front of both audiences that the merger was now "fully integrated," and downplay whatever fairness gap remained; or report all four measures — cooperation, fairness, adoption, turnover — against the day-one baseline, and account for the trade-offs she had made. She had to decide what would sustain cooperation from here: bonus incentives for hitting the dispatch number, so people were paid to cooperate; tighter supervision and closer handoff monitoring, so cooperation was enforced rather than chosen; or fairness mechanisms and modeled leadership built to outlast the crisis itself. And she had to state, on a scale from zero to a hundred, how firmly she would stand behind the fair-process trade-off she had made.

## Closing

On the morning of 8 June, four rounds of decisions lay ahead: six weeks on dispatch, eight weeks on the client contract, ninety days on the redundancy. She had the numbers on the wall, and a workforce watching her next move.
