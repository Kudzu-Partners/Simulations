# NorTec Transfer Office: Ninety Days for AeroFoam-7

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1893 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1893 — *From Lab to Market* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, June 16, 2026, Inês Teixeira set two letters side by side on the table of the NorTec Transfer Office meeting room in Porto. The first, from the office's patent counsel, carried a date: the international application covering AeroFoam-7 had to enter national phase within ninety days, at a cost of roughly EUR 180,000 in filing and translation fees across the target jurisdictions, or the protection would be gone in the markets on the list. The second, from the European programme that had paid for the underlying research, confirmed that the reporting period of the office's EUR 600,000 proof-of-concept seed fund closed in the same quarter, and that any part of it not committed by then went back to the funder.

Ninety days ran to mid-September. By then Teixeira had to have named a commercialization route for AeroFoam-7, committed the seed fund across the uses competing for it, and put either a signed agreement or a term sheet in front of the office's investment committee.

## The Protagonist

Inês Teixeira, 44, had directed NorTec Transfer Office since 2021. She had come to the university from eleven years in industrial licensing at a European materials group, where her job had been to buy technology rather than sell it, and she still described the difference as the most useful thing she knew. Her committee judged the office on what its disclosures turned into, and above all on how much outside money each euro of the office's own funding brought in behind it: at least one and a half external euros per internal euro deployed.

## The Decision

Teixeira had four things to settle in sequence over the quarter, each one narrowing those that followed. She had to state what actually stood between AeroFoam-7 and a paying customer. She had to choose the model by which the material would reach the market. She had to split the seed fund between protecting the invention and proving it, and pick which of three counterparties to sign. And she had to take the whole thing to the investment committee as a twelve-month plan.

## The Company and its Setting

NorTec Transfer Office was the knowledge-transfer unit of a Porto research university, working inside the U.Porto innovation ecosystem. It ran on an annual operating budget of EUR 1.8 million with a team of six, and managed a portfolio of 23 disclosed inventions. It also held the EUR 600,000 proof-of-concept seed fund, discretionary money whose purpose was to move inventions far enough along that somebody else would pay to finish them.

AeroFoam-7 was the most advanced thing in the portfolio: a recyclable aerogel insulation material invented by a materials-science group led by Professor Cláudia Nogueira. The laboratory data were strong. The material sat at Technology Readiness Level 4 — validated on the bench, not in the field. Industrial buyers of insulation bought at Technology Readiness Level 7, a prototype demonstrated in an operational environment. Closing that distance was estimated at EUR 450,000 and nine to twelve months of field validation.

## The Situation

The arithmetic on Teixeira's page was short. Full national-phase filing at EUR 180,000 plus full field validation at EUR 450,000 came to EUR 630,000 against a seed fund of EUR 600,000. The office could not both protect the invention and prove it out of its own money.

Three counterparties had made opening positions (see **Exhibit 2**). Halden Insulation Group, a European incumbent, offered EUR 250,000 on signature plus a 3% royalty for an exclusive licence. Grupo Ribadouro, a construction group in the region, offered EUR 400,000 of co-funding under a joint-development agreement, together with an option to license later. Corvo Capital, a venture fund, offered EUR 1.2 million for 35% of a spin-off, on the condition that Nogueira left the university to run it.

The patent decision had its own shape. Eight jurisdictions sat on counsel's target list; the first customers any of the three counterparties described were in two or three of them.

The committee tracked four measures, and on June 16 they stood as follows (see **Exhibit 1**). Readiness placed the material on the technology readiness scale, at TRL 4 against the TRL 7 buyers required. Funding leverage was external euros raised per internal euro deployed, at 0.0× against the funder's expectation of 1.5× or better. Value capture was the committee's 0-to-100 rating of the strategic and financial value the office still held in the asset, at 20. The seed fund counter stood at EUR 0 committed of EUR 600,000. The committee logged each move and where readiness, leverage and value capture went after it.

If Teixeira committed to nothing by mid-September, the position resolved itself without her. The application would not enter national phase and the protection would lapse in the eight jurisdictions on the list. The uncommitted seed fund would return to the funder. AeroFoam-7 would still be at TRL 4, with no partner, no protection, and a lead inventor still waiting.

## What Teixeira Controlled

Teixeira controlled the diagnosis first. She had to name what bound the asset — its readiness at TRL 4 against buyers at TRL 7 and a EUR 450,000 crossing; the seed fund at EUR 600,000 against EUR 630,000 of need; the ninety-day patent clock, after which protection would lapse in eight jurisdictions; or nothing in particular, the lab data being strong enough to sell on. She had to state what evidence TRL 7 required: a field demonstration in an operational building, further laboratory characterisation of tests the material had already passed, or a peer-reviewed paper publishing the bench data. And she had to put a number on how strictly her own team was rating the gap, from crediting the laboratory's account in full to discounting it hard.

She then controlled the model. She could license exclusively to an incumbent for cash and a royalty; she could spin the technology out into a company founded around the inventors; or she could enter a joint-development agreement in which a partner co-funded validation in return for an option to license. She controlled how the office handled Nogueira, who had never run a company and had not said whether she would go: keeping her as scientific adviser without resting the route on her taking an executive seat, recruiting an outside chief executive with Nogueira alongside as adviser, or planning on the basis that she left the university and ran the venture herself. She controlled when rights and risk moved to a counterparty — at validated TRL 7 once field data existed; at signature, taking the cash before the validation risk was resolved; or at no defined point, left to the relationship. And she set how tightly the office bound the choice of model to this asset and this team rather than to the largest headline number.

The money came next. She split the EUR 600,000 between national-phase filing and field validation in thousands of euros, with whatever remained held as reserve. She set filing scope: the two or three markets of the first customers, all eight target jurisdictions, or none of them, holding the material as a trade secret. She chose Halden, Ribadouro, Corvo, or no partner at all.

Finally she controlled the plan she carried into the committee room. She sequenced the twelve months — filing in week one with validation starting immediately and partner payments tied to milestones, validation first with filing once the data looked good, or loosely, filing in the coming months and starting validation soon. She named the exit measures she would be held to: readiness at exit, time to market, value created and the leverage ratio together; the headline deal value alone; or no hard measures, with progress reported informally. She built a contingency covering Nogueira's departure and a weak validation result in month nine, one of the two, or neither. And she set how hard she would argue the leverage-and-readiness case to a committee that liked large numbers.

## Closing

At half past seven on the evening of June 16, Teixeira printed the AeroFoam-7 dossier and wrote two columns on the cover sheet: what the office would pay for, and who would pay for the rest. National phase closed on day ninety. The seed fund closed the same quarter. Nogueira had asked for an answer before the autumn.
