# Metalúrgica São Bernardo: Eight Weeks to the Collective Agreement

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-370 · **Companion simulation:** Express 370 — *El Sindicato: Labor Relations & Workforce Productivity* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, February 3, 2026, Luciana Ferraz, Director of Human Resources at Metalúrgica São Bernardo S.A. (MSB), received the metalworkers' union's list of demands for the next collective labour agreement. SINDMETAL-ABC wanted an 11.2% wage increase, a working week cut from 44 to 40 hours with no loss of pay, and a joint Health Committee funded by the company. The agreement in force, the *Convenção Coletiva de Trabalho* (CCT), expired on March 31, eight weeks away.

The CEO had handed Ferraz two jobs that MSB had always run separately: reach an agreement without a strike, and lift output per worker at the company's three plants. Before the first session with the union she had to settle what she believed was holding productivity down, how MSB would sit at the table, and the wage figure it would open with.

## The Protagonist

Ferraz was 47. She had spent fourteen years in labour relations at two auto-parts groups in the ABC industrial belt south of São Paulo before joining MSB in 2023. She had sat across the table from SINDMETAL-ABC twice, both times as a deputy. This was the first CCT she would lead, and the first since she had started presenting absenteeism and turnover to the executive committee as a cost line rather than a staffing nuisance.

## The Decision

Ferraz faced four moments over roughly two years. In the first week of February she had to name the root cause MSB would treat as its main lever, choose the posture the company would take into the negotiation, and set the opening wage offer. At the table, she would have to decide whether to close or ask for more time, and on what terms. Once an agreement existed, she would have to spread an improvement budget across programmes and decide how much to tell the new Health Committee. More than a year later, she would have to decide what to protect if money tightened, and how to prepare for the CCT after that.

## The Company and its Setting

MSB, founded in 1968 in São Bernardo do Campo, made suspension and steering components for the carmakers' assembly plants in the Greater ABC region. It employed about 4,200 people, 3,600 of them in production, across three plants in the same industrial district. Revenue was BRL 1,840 million a year and EBITDA BRL 147 million, a margin of 8.0% (see Exhibit 1). Total labour cost was BRL 498 million a year, 27% of revenue.

SINDMETAL-ABC represented about 128,000 metalworkers in the region and ran a structured, well-prepared bargaining process. Two of MSB's last three CCTs had ended in stoppages: three days in 2020 and eight days in 2022. MSB's largest customer, a carmaker whose compact-sedan line depended on MSB's suspension line, had notified a 6% reduction in the price it paid for components, effective in eighteen months.

## The Situation

MSB sat below the sector on every labour number HR tracked (see Exhibit 1). Output stood at 87.4 units per production operator per day, against about 108 at comparable plants. Absenteeism was 6.8% of scheduled hours, against a sector benchmark of 3.9%, and the highest MSB had recorded in seven years. Voluntary turnover was 22% a year, against 9%. The employee net promoter score was −24, the worst since 2014. Accidents with time off had reached 34 in twelve months, nearly double the benchmark. The production process itself had not been updated since 2018.

Finance put the cost of absenteeism at BRL 34 million a year in rework, overtime cover and line delays, and the cost of turnover at BRL 28 million a year in hiring, training and learning curves. Closing the productivity gap in full was worth an estimated BRL 68 million a year in EBITDA. A seven-day strike, by the same estimate, would cost about BRL 48 million in customer penalties and idle fixed cost.

HR's internal analysis traced absence to five sources (see Exhibit 2): repetitive-strain injuries on lines that repeated eight movements every forty-five seconds; absence declared as common illness but correlated with work climate; a commute averaging an hour and forty-five minutes, where late arrivals accumulated into absences; childcare; and other causes.

Ferraz's team tracked two further numbers. An index of union trust, built from delegate meetings and grievance records, stood at 30 out of 100. The labour-relations team put the probability of a stoppage around this CCT at 65%. The average production wage was BRL 4,820 a month, about 7% below the sector benchmark. Inflation, measured by the IPCA consumer-price index, was 4.8%.

If nothing changed, the agreement would lapse on March 31 with a union that had struck twice in six years, output would stay a fifth below comparable plants, and the customer's price cut would land on a margin of 8.0%.

## What Ferraz Controlled

In February, Ferraz would name one of four causes as the systemic lever MSB would build its programme around: absenteeism, turnover, low motivation and work climate, or technology and processes. She would set the negotiating posture: an integrative package combining wages, working conditions and shared gains, or a distributive approach centred on the wage figure. And she would set the opening wage offer, anywhere from zero to the union's 11.2%; the working draft sat at 4.8%, the rate of inflation.

At the table, she would decide whether to close the agreement in that session or ask the union for seventy-two more hours. If she closed, she would set the final wage increase, again between zero and 11.2%; the structure of the profit-sharing payment Brazilian law calls PLR, either linked to productivity targets at between 0.8 and 1.4 monthly salaries or fixed at one additional salary; the working week, either 44 hours with a flexible hour bank, 44 hours unchanged, or 40 hours with no cut in pay; and the annual budget MSB would give the Health Committee, from nothing to BRL 3 million, with HR's draft at BRL 1.2 million.

Once an agreement was signed, she would spread an improvement budget of BRL 8 million a year across six programmes: an ergonomic redesign of the lines; active breaks with preventive physiotherapy; a partnership for transport vans to the plants; a childcare subsidy; a career-development programme; and team recognition with kaizen improvement circles. Operations had priced the first four at BRL 3.4 million, BRL 640,000, BRL 1.8 million and BRL 2.2 million a year, which together came to slightly more than the envelope before the other two received anything. She would also decide what to share with the Health Committee at its first meeting: all the data and plans, results without budget detail, or only what the law required.

More than a year into the agreement, she would decide whether to keep every programme, suspend part of them to save about BRL 2 million a year, or suspend the vans and the childcare subsidy to save about BRL 4 million. She would decide how to answer a delegate who attacked the company in public: treat it as an internal union matter, meet him and the union leadership privately, commit publicly to the programmes, or open the company's financial picture to the union. And she would choose how to approach the following CCT, ten months out: a joint productivity-sharing framework, early conversations, or waiting for the union's demands.

After each round, Ferraz would see where MSB stood on five numbers: EBITDA margin, output per operator per day, union trust, absenteeism and strike risk. The executive committee would judge the whole effort at the end on productivity, union trust, strike risk and EBITDA margin taken together.

## Closing

At 18:40 the union's list was still the only document open on Ferraz's screen. The CFO wanted a wage number by Friday, the customer's letter lay on the corner of her desk, and the first session with SINDMETAL-ABC was set for February 17.
