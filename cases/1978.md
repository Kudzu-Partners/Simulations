# Cosecha Pay: The First Eighteen Months

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1978 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1978 — *Launch Peru* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, May 11, 2026, Silvana Rivas Tello read Gonzalo Prieto's email twice, then opened the cash sheet to price it. Prieto's fund had led the pre-seed round and had spent the spring implying a follow-on. The email put terms on it: S/ 150,000 (Peruvian soles) of monthly recurring revenue and 1,000 farmers transacting on the platform, inside two quarters, or the seed round was off.

The sheet held the other half of the arithmetic. Cosecha Pay S.A.C. had S/ 600,000 in the bank and consumed S/ 95,000 a month net of revenue (see **Exhibit 1**). Rivas did the division she did every Monday: 6.3 months, the account at zero in the third week of November 2026 — the fortnight Prieto's two quarters expired. She had until then to choose a market, a source of capital, and two hires. In April the platform carried 310 transacting farmers and S/ 48,000 of revenue.

## The Protagonist

Rivas, 34, was an agronomist. She had spent seven years at an export cooperative in Ica reconciling the paperwork between a smallholder's delivery and his payment — sixty to ninety days of it, in a business where the next planting could not wait. She founded Cosecha Pay in Lima in November 2025 with Renzo Ayala, a payments engineer she met at a meet-up in Barranco; their premise was that the delay was a data problem, not a credit problem. The two held 78% of the company, and S/ 90,000 of Rivas's own money sat inside the S/ 600,000. She had not run a company before, and had never decided which farmers received money and which did not.

## The Decision

Rivas had to decide where Cosecha Pay would sell, what capital would fund it, and whom it would hire — then keep deciding, quarter by quarter, across the eighteen months her plan covered. The three were coupled: the market set what a farmer cost to acquire, the capital set how much acquiring the company could afford and on whose timetable, and the two hires the budget allowed set what it could do with either. All three had to be committed in the first quarter and defended to Prieto in November.

## The Company and its Setting

Cosecha Pay was a payments and working-capital platform for smallholder agriculture. A farmer holding a confirmed produce order was paid within twenty-four hours rather than at the buyer's convenience, and could draw an advance against that order before delivery; the buyer received a traceable record of every lot. It charged the buyer a fee on settled volume and the farmer a fee on advances, and employed ten people.

Two facts shaped everything Rivas decided. Cosecha Pay did not only move money; it lent it. Every advance was an unsecured judgment about a harvest that had not happened, and nobody there had priced credit risk. And the capital market above her head was thin: Lima had angels and pre-seed funds, few firms wrote the round after Prieto's, and the queue was long. Peruvians started companies at high rates and closed them at high rates, and the difference was rarely the idea.

## The Situation

The April numbers described a business that worked and had not scaled. A transacting farmer produced S/ 155 of revenue a month at a 60% contribution margin — S/ 93. Acquiring one cost S/ 220 in field agents, radio spots and referral incentives, repaid in about 2.4 months. Against monthly churn of 8%, a farmer was worth roughly S/ 1,163 of lifetime contribution, some 5.3 times his acquisition cost. Reach was the missing part: 310 of 3,200 registered farmers transacted in April, an activation rate of 9.7%, and the 2,890 who did not had each cost something to sign up.

Rivas had done the arithmetic of the gap (see **Exhibit 2**). Reaching 1,000 transacting farmers by the end of November meant 690 net additions in six months, about 115 a month, before replacing the 25 a month that 8% churn removed from the April base. At S/ 220 each, 690 additions cost S/ 151,800 — a quarter of the cash in the bank. At April's revenue per farmer, S/ 150,000 a month implied 968 transacting farmers, so the two halves of Prieto's bar were the same bar, provided revenue per farmer held.

Three things sat on the calendar. From July the coastal harvest thinned and farmer repayments slowed with it, a cycle Rivas had watched from inside the cooperative. Yunta, a Bogotá platform that closed a regional round in March, had begun quoting zero settlement fees to buyers in Ica and Piura. And the budget stretched to two hires before November, not three.

If nothing changed, the outcome was arithmetic. The account emptied in the third week of November, the base shed 25 transacting farmers a month and replaced them slowly, revenue held near S/ 48,000, and Rivas reached the meeting S/ 102,000 of monthly revenue and 690 farmers short.

## What Rivas Controlled

Prieto wanted a memo first. It had to name the binding constraint — the registered base, the share transacting and staying, the company's visibility, or the product's range; name the number she would be judged on — registered farmers, transacting farmers and revenue, or downloads and traffic; state the distance to the bar as substantially closed, as the full S/ 102,000 and 690 farmers, or as a target to renegotiate; and split the quarter's effort between retaining farmers and acquiring them, any share from none to all.

Then three coupled bets. **Market:** deeper into Ica, where the 310 farmers and the buyer relationships already were; the Mantaro highlands, three times the smallholders and nobody serving them; the forty mid-size agro-exporters, each a large account and a six-month sale; or all three at once, on the argument that the platform gained value with every corridor it covered. **Capital:** seed equity from Prieto's syndicate, priced on traction and slow; a development-bank innovation grant, non-dilutive, tranched against conditions on the state's calendar; revenue-based financing, funded in two weeks and repaid as a share of revenue to an agreed multiple; or a strategic investment from Grupo Valle Verde, an agro-exporter offering capital and its 900 suppliers for terms on that channel. **People:** two seats, three candidates, order recorded — a senior growth lead from a Lima consumer app, a pair of field agronomists based in the valleys, a credit-risk analyst from a microfinance bank.

The off-season and Yunta would land together, and four answers went with them. Burn: cut nothing; cut everything discretionary; or cut the non-essential lines and leave the retention and credit work standing. Yunta: match its pricing; do nothing; or deepen service with the best farmers and let the marginal ones go. Advances: continue as before; halt them; or cap them to confirmed orders and score the book. Reserve against defaults: S/ 0, S/ 40,000 or S/ 90,000.

The November meeting held three more. Lead with the 3,200 registrations and the growth rate, with the farmer count against the bar however reached, or with runway, retained farmers, unit economics and the plan. Ask for whatever the market would give, for nothing, or for an amount tied to named milestones. Answer the question Prieto put to every founder at this stage — most ventures here did not survive, why would this one — with the founders' capacity for work, with the size of the cheque, or with the specific things she had changed; and say, from nought to a hundred, how firmly she held it.

Four numbers measured her: runway, the months of cash left at the current rate; monthly revenue against S/ 150,000; transacting farmers against 1,000; and the fund's standing estimate of the odds Cosecha Pay reached a term sheet rather than closing. A fifth was hers — lifetime contribution per farmer over acquisition cost, below three times meaning the engine did not pay for itself.

## Closing

At 9:20 Rivas printed the cash sheet, wrote three headings across a blank page — market, capital, people — and laid Prieto's email face-down beside it. The account emptied in the third week of November. His two quarters ended the same week. She had two hires, cash enough to win one market properly, and eighteen months in which Cosecha Pay either became fundable or stopped.
