# Hexagone Matériaux: Pricing PolnaChem

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-2006 · **Companion simulation:** Express 2006 — *Cross-Border Deal* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 8 June 2026, Élodie Marchand set two documents side by side in her office off the avenue Kléber. The first was the information memorandum for PolnaChem Sp. z o.o., a profitable Polish specialty-coatings producer with €60 million of annual EBITDA whose owners wanted 8.5 times that figure — about €510 million — for the company. The second was Hexagone Matériaux's post-mortem on its previous cross-border acquisition, which she had helped write. It recorded two findings: the team had priced that target on its financial statements alone, and within eighteen months of closing the people who carried the customer relationships had left, taking much of the expected synergy with them.

In six weeks Marchand would put a recommendation in front of Hexagone's investment committee. A rival strategic bidder was circling PolnaChem and was expected to table an offer inside that window.

## The Protagonist

Élodie Marchand, 44, had been Group Head of Mergers & Acquisitions at Hexagone Matériaux SA since 2022. She had joined in 2016 from the transaction-services practice of a Paris audit firm and had since led eleven bolt-on acquisitions, nine of them in France, Belgium and northern Italy, all small enough that an error could be absorbed inside a trading year. The exception was the deal in the post-mortem, a Czech adhesives business bought in 2021; she had been the deputy on it and had signed the valuation model the committee approved. Afterwards the board rewrote the terms of engagement: no acquisition would reach the committee without an environmental, social and governance assessment and an assessment of trust and management retention, both reflected in the price rather than in the integration plan.

## The Decision

Marchand had six weeks and four things to settle, in sequence.

How much diligence to run with the rival's clock running, and what to make of what came back — each dossier on her desk could be read more than one way.

What price to put on the business: how much of the environmental gap belonged in the number, how much expected synergy she wrote off for the trust problem, the multiple above which she would not go, and the multiple at which she opened.

How to fund and structure the purchase inside the board's leverage ceiling.

And what she put in front of the committee — together with what she did if the rival moved while she was in the room.

## The Company and its Setting

Hexagone Matériaux SA, headquartered in Paris and listed on Euronext, supplied coatings, adhesives and engineered materials to construction, automotive and packaging customers across Europe. Group revenue was €2.1 billion, EBITDA margin 17% — €357 million — and net debt stood at 1.8 times EBITDA. It had grown mainly by buying small competitors.

Two commitments framed what Marchand was allowed to do. The board had approved an inorganic-growth mandate to enter a high-growth Central and Eastern European market by acquisition, organic entry having been rejected as too slow. And Hexagone had committed publicly to a net-zero-by-2040 pathway; investors and lenders now priced the group's cost of capital against its ESG profile rather than treating it as a reporting exercise.

The board had also fixed a number: post-close leverage was not to exceed 2.5 times EBITDA. Treasury's arithmetic, circulated with the mandate, was that funding a bid at the owners' asking multiple entirely in cash and debt would take the group to 2.7 times — through the line.

## The Situation

PolnaChem had been founded in 1994 by Marek Polnicki, who still owned it (see **Exhibit 2**). It was profitable and had the customer base and plant footprint Hexagone wanted. Comparable European coatings transactions of the previous three years had cleared at 6.5 to 7.0 times EBITDA, against the owners' ask of 8.5; every tenth of a turn on the multiple was about €6 million of price.

The environmental dossier said three things: PolnaChem's carbon intensity ran 40% above Hexagone's on the same measure; two of its sites operated without permits aligned to current EU environmental standards; and bringing those sites into compliance was estimated at €45 million of capital expenditure over three years.

The trust dossier said two. Polnicki personally held the relationships with the largest customers, several dating to the company's first decade, and the workforce was openly wary of a foreign owner. Hexagone's experience and its advisers' benchmark agreed: where a cross-border integration ran on low trust, 20 to 30 per cent of expected synergies never arrived.

The committee would judge her on four measures (see **Exhibit 1**). The deal's net present value in euro millions — synergies created, less the premium paid above the business's standalone worth, less the remediation, less whatever share of synergy the trust problem removed — stood at zero, because nothing had been decided. Completion probability, the chance the transaction closed against the rival and the synergies were realised, was a coin toss at 50%. Post-close leverage, net debt divided by EBITDA, was 1.8 times against the ceiling of 2.5. And a standing read on the unpriced remediation, transition risk and founder dependency the deal still carried — lower being safer — showed **High**.

One outcome required no decision at all: if Hexagone did not bid, the rival took the platform and the board's mandate went unmet for another year.

## What Marchand Controlled

She controlled how far diligence ran against the clock: sign on the clean financials before a competing offer landed, run the environmental work only and assume the human questions would settle after closing, or run both mandated lenses before anyone wrote a price. She controlled the reading attached to each dossier. The environmental file could be routine paperwork, a finding to quantify now, or a reason to stop. The founder's grip on the customer list could be ordinary integration risk, a measurable synergy exposure to size and structure around, or proof that a founder-owned company could not be bought across a border.

She controlled the price. The environmental gap could enter the valuation not at all, as the €45 million of remediation capital expenditure deducted from enterprise value, or in full — remediation, transition risk and the effect on Hexagone's own cost of capital. Synergies could be modelled whole, cut by 10 per cent, or cut by the full share founder dependency would take. Then she would write two numbers between 5.0 and 9.0 times EBITDA: the walk-away she would not cross, and the multiple she opened at.

She controlled the structure: the share of consideration paid in cash and debt, which left existing holders undiluted but raised leverage, the balance being Hexagone equity, which diluted them but lowered it; whether to defer part of the price against milestones — none, ESG compliance alone, or ESG compliance with customer retention; what to require of the founder, from a clean exit on standard warranties, to enhanced warranties covering the permit gap while he left anyway, to a lock-in with retention terms; and whether to fund the deal with a conventional acquisition facility or a sustainability-linked one carrying a margin ratchet of roughly 35 basis points tied to PolnaChem's decarbonisation.

She controlled the committee meeting. If the rival moved she could hold her bid, raise to win, or match it. She would choose what sat at the centre of her case: the strategic platform story, the price she had negotiated, or price, adjustments, structure, financing cost and completion probability defended as one argument. She would state what her structure pre-empted if the deal underdelivered. And she would put a number on how firmly she held that a disciplined price beat winning the asset.

## Closing

At half past seven that evening Marchand wrote the two dossiers, the comparable range and the owners' ask on a single sheet, and beneath them ruled two blank lines for the walk-away and the opening bid. The committee met in six weeks. The rival was already somewhere in the room.
