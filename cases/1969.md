# Triglav Systems: Rebuilding the Knowledge Network

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1969 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1969 — *Network HR* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 5 May 2026, Nika Bregar read Maja K.'s resignation letter beside a printout she had been carrying since April. The letter was four lines long and gave a competitor's name. The printout was the knowledge map from Triglav Systems' first organizational network analysis — a study of who at the 540-person firm actually went to whom for answers — and Maja K. sat at the middle of it, a principal architect drawn as a knot of lines joining the platform team to three of the six product squads. By eleven that morning the programme director on Triglav's flagship implementation had written to say the work had stopped: the open questions all routed to Maja K., and nobody else held the cross-squad context she carried in her head.

Bregar had until the second of Triglav's two quarterly client milestones to raise the firm's knowledge-transfer index from 41 to the 70 the board had set, and to do it without a single new hire: the chief executive had authorised zero net new headcount. If she could not show cross-squad knowledge moving again by then, leadership would reorganise Triglav into a rigid functional structure that the engineering leaders said would make the silos permanent.

## The Protagonist

Nika Bregar, 41, had led People & Network at Triglav Systems since 2022, after six years as a talent partner in the Ljubljana office of a German engineering group. She had commissioned the network study herself, over the objection of two squad leads, and had defended the cost on the argument that Triglav's product was written by people who did not write down what they knew. The study had been delivered three weeks before Maja K. resigned. Bregar had read it, circulated a summary and scheduled a workshop for June.

## The Decision

Bregar had to decide where to spend a fixed development budget and a fixed quantity of manager time on the relationships between her engineers rather than on the engineers themselves — and to keep deciding at four reviews between May and the second client milestone.

## The Company and its Setting

Triglav Systems d.o.o., founded in Ljubljana in 2007, built industrial internet-of-things (IoT) and analytics platforms for manufacturing clients across the European Union: 540 people, EUR 72 million of revenue in 2025, six product squads plus shared platform, data-science and customer-success functions.

Almost nothing Triglav sold was written down. Its architects and customer-success specialists held plant-level knowledge of client processes that two previous attempts had failed to capture. The firm had always managed its people by reporting line and headcount, and never the relationships between them.

## The Situation

The study reported four measures, and Bregar had put all four on one page for the executive committee (see **Exhibit 1**). The knowledge-transfer index scored, from 0 to 100, how readily knowledge moved between the parts of the firm that needed it; Triglav stood at 41, with cross-squad flow down 30% year on year. Network density was the share of possible squad-to-squad connections that existed: 0.06, six in every hundred, with two or three of the fifteen squad pairs holding no direct tie at all. Connector concentration was the share of all cross-team exchanges that passed through the firm's most central people: three of them carried about 45%. The fourth measure counted how many of Triglav's remaining top connectors had been secured, and it read zero of two — both sat in the flight-risk band of the March engagement survey. Against a fifth reading, for how much the network would lose to one more departure, the study entered a single word: fragile.

The resignation had converted the arithmetic into a receivable. EUR 2.1 million of contracted revenue on the stalled flagship implementation was at risk, and the client wanted a recovery date. Rok Vidmar, the chief technology officer, told the committee that a second departure of Maja K.'s reach would put Triglav's delivery capability and its European Union commitments in question.

Two views had already formed. The squad leads wanted the architect role reposted and filled from outside. The data-science lead wanted a documentation sprint, on the argument that a wiki does not resign.

Bregar's own instrument was blunt. She had a fixed development budget, the working hours of Triglav's line managers, and rotation slots the delivery schedule would release only in small numbers. If she committed none of it, the position was arithmetically plain: the index stayed at 41, the unconnected squad pairs kept routing every question through the two connectors who remained, and the executive committee took the functional reorganisation for want of an alternative.

## What Bregar Controlled

Her plan would be committed at four reviews, each of which put a set of courses of action on the table (see **Exhibit 2**).

At the first, she had to record what the gap between the reporting lines and the knowledge map meant: that the reporting lines were the firm's real structure, that the two pictures carried equal weight, or that the working structure was the pattern of ties. She had to name the network's most dangerous feature — the pay of the central engineers, the low overall level of connection, or the concentration of cross-team traffic in three people. She had to say which gaps to close first: all fifteen pairs among the six squads, the pairs already best connected, or the two or three pairs with no direct tie. And she had to set how far to read the engagement and flight-risk detail behind each connector, from a glance to the full file.

At the second, she committed the development budget. On the connectors, she could recruit an external replacement for Maja K., concentrate development spending on the two who remained, or reduce their load while building ties around them. On the mechanism for closing the gaps, she could fund a documentation sprint, move immediately to the functional structure, or fund cross-squad rotations, paired work and standing practice groups. On the line managers, she could leave them outside the plan, keep them running their own squads, or give each of them a brokering task beyond it. She also set what share of the budget went to the ties rather than to individuals.

At the third, she committed the scarce items. For succession on the at-risk connectors, she could commission written playbooks, pair each connector with two apprentices, or do neither this cycle. For rotations, she could move as many engineers as the schedule allowed, freeze movement entirely, or place a small number on the gaps her managers could staff. For where cross-team questions were routed, she could keep them on the proven connectors, designate two new central people, or spread them across several paths. And she held a retention line for the two flight-risk connectors: EUR 0, EUR 120,000 for the more central of the two, or EUR 240,000 covering load reduction, a growth path and stay conversations for both.

At the fourth, she had to answer in advance the two questions Vidmar had put to her. If a second connector signalled an exit, the plan could move that traffic onto the connector who remained, counter-offer and open a search, or let the new ties and the apprentices carry it. If a client arrived needing several squads mobilised inside a few weeks, the plan could assign the remaining connector full-time, decline or delay the work, or staff a cross-squad team along whatever ties existed by then. She had to decide what to lead with in her three minutes at the executive committee: the four measures, a record of the rotations and workshops run, or a recommendation to adopt the functional reorganisation. And she had to state, as a number, how firmly she held that resilience came from the number of paths rather than from the strength of individuals.

## Closing

Just after midday Bregar wrote the six squad names down one edge of a sheet and across the other, making a grid of fifteen cells, and began marking the ones that were empty. The first review was that afternoon. She had a budget she could spend once, managers whose weeks were already full, and two people whose names were on the map and on the survey.
