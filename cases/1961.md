# Polder Digital: The Atlas Squad's First Six Weeks

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1961 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1961 — *The Servant Leader* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 11 May 2026, Sanne Vermeulen opened two documents before anyone else on the Atlas squad had logged in. The first was the acceptance plan for a client milestone due Friday, 19 June — six weeks out, written into the contract, carrying a penalty of EUR 180,000 if it slipped and a EUR 2.4 million renewal sitting behind it. The second was the squad's latest pulse survey: engagement at 71 on a 100-point scale, a meaningfulness score of 6.1 out of 10, and three of Atlas's eight members marked "elevated" for turnover risk by Polder Digital's people-analytics team (see **Exhibit 1**).

Until that month Vermeulen had been Atlas's senior data engineer. On Monday she was its lead, for the first time in her career, and the eight names on the pulse report were hers to answer for. She had six weeks. In that time she had to decide how she would act on three of those eight names, and where she would spend the little of her own time delivery had not already taken.

## The Protagonist

Sanne Vermeulen, 34, had come to Polder Digital B.V. from the data-platform group of a Dutch insurer and had written the reconciliation logic that won Atlas the bank account. She had never managed anyone. The promotion arrived without a competition after Ingrid Sloot — Atlas's lead since the programme began — left for a client of her own. Ruben Aalders, the partner who held the bank relationship, had told Vermeulen only that the squad needed someone who understood the platform. Every person she now led had been her peer the previous month, and two of them had applied for the job.

## The Decision

Vermeulen had to decide, fortnight by fortnight, how she would act on three situations inside her own squad and where she would spend the small amount of her own time that was not already committed to delivery — and to keep deciding as the acceptance date came closer.

## The Company and its Setting

Polder Digital B.V. was a Rotterdam data-and-product consultancy of 220 staff and roughly EUR 34 million in annual revenue, building data platforms and digital products for European banks, logistics operators and public-sector bodies. It organised delivery into client-facing squads of specialists, each led by a working engineer rather than a professional manager.

The most recent firm-wide pulse had come in at 71, down from 78 the year before — the first time the number had moved against Polder in either direction that mattered. Atlas, eight specialists on the flagship data platform for the firm's largest account, was one of the squads inside that decline.

## The Situation

Polder's delivery tracker put the programme's performance index at 62 out of 100 against the 19 June acceptance criteria, and the squad's turnover-risk index at 48 out of 100, where a lower number was better. The plan to reach acceptance assumed the current eight people, at the current pace, with the module owner in place.

Three things sat in Vermeulen's notes from her first week (see **Exhibit 2**).

Bram Kuipers, the strongest engineer on Atlas and the owner of the reconciliation module on the critical path, had gone quiet. He kept his camera off in stand-ups, answered in single lines, and had declined two stretch assignments in six weeks. Vermeulen also knew that his compensation review had been deferred twice; that the bank's architecture board had made two decisions about his module without inviting him, which Sloot had previously argued about on his behalf; and that a recruiter's message to him had been visible on a shared screen in April. Polder's staffing model priced an exit on the critical path at three to four weeks of slip and about EUR 95,000 to backfill and re-ramp.

Dilan Arslan and Noor Bakker, both mid-level, had been promised roughly 40 hours of development and coaching time across the quarter — a commitment Sloot had signed off and neither had yet taken. Both were among the three members the pulse had flagged. The 40 hours were the most easily reclaimed hours in the plan.

Wouter de Groot, reliable for two years, had missed three sprint commitments in a row. He had stopped joining the optional Friday session, logged off early on most days, and had asked to come off the on-call rotation without saying why. He had raised nothing with Sloot before she left, and nothing with Vermeulen.

If Vermeulen changed nothing, the position was arithmetically plain rather than dramatic: the performance index sat at 62 with six weeks to run, the engagement number was 71 and had been falling firm-wide, three of eight people were flagged, and the acceptance date would arrive on 19 June whether or not the module owner was still at the firm.

## What Vermeulen Controlled

In the first fortnight Vermeulen controlled two readings and eight hours. The readings were her own account of why Kuipers had gone quiet — that he had stopped caring and it was a matter of attitude; that it was about money and could wait for a compensation cycle; that he had lost ownership and reason to care about work that had become a grind; or that it could not be known without asking, and the deadline meant acting on what was visible and asking later — and the same exercise for de Groot: a technical gap, slackness, something that had changed outside work, or a missed commitment that counted the same whatever caused it. The eight hours were the one-on-one time she could protect in a fortnight, to be divided in whatever proportion she chose between Kuipers, de Groot, the two mid-level members, and the four others who were watching how she started.

In the second fortnight she chose a course on Kuipers: hand him ownership of the hardest module together with the reasons the bank needed it and clear the obstacles in front of him; have a conversation and raise the frequency of check-ins while leaving the critical path where it was; or move the critical path to another engineer and raise the frequency of check-ins. She decided what happened to the 40 development hours — kept whole, halved, or moved into delivery — and what share of her own working week went on removing obstacles rather than on reviewing work.

In the third fortnight, with three weeks to acceptance and de Groot's pattern unchanged, she chose how to act: open a formal performance plan; leave it alone and let the squad settle it; or sit down, establish what had changed, and agree a load with him. She chose the commitment that came out of that: none until things settled, unchanged, or reduced but explicit with a stated check-in cadence. And she set the share of the squad's remaining capacity that went onto the critical path.

In the final fortnight she chose how the push ran — long hours from everyone, a focused pace with the working week protected, or an easier pace with the date at risk. She chose what to tell Aalders: the milestone outcome, the state of the squad, or both with the trade-off named. And she chose the operating philosophy she would state in that meeting — delivery first, harmony first, or care and standard held together — and how firmly, as a number, she held that the squad's condition had to show up in delivery rather than stand in for it.

She was measured on four things, taken again at the end of every fortnight: the engagement pulse, out of 100; the meaningfulness score, out of 10, which recorded how much purpose and ownership people said they felt in the work; the delivery performance index against the 19 June criteria, out of 100; and the turnover-risk index across the squad, out of 100 and better when low. Alongside them sat two facts: whether the milestone was accepted, and whether Kuipers was still at Polder.

## Closing

Vermeulen closed the pulse report at twenty past eight and blocked eight hours into her calendar for the fortnight, without yet writing any names beside them. The stand-up began at nine and she had not decided what to say in it. The bank's acceptance meeting was on Friday, 19 June.
