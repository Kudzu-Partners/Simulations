# Vlaamse TechWorks: One Budget Cycle

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1990 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1990 — *Careers That Last* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, September 7, 2026, Marieke Coppens read the confidential workforce review a third time and stopped at the same two lines. Vlaamse TechWorks NV had twice won its industry's "best employer" award for pay and benefits. In the twelve months to June, voluntary turnover across its 2,600 staff had risen to 17%, and to 24% among the mid-career delivery engineers who held the client relationships.

Coppens had been the company's Group People Lead for six weeks. The board had ring-fenced EUR 4.2 million for development and wellbeing and given her one budget cycle to move retention, employability and wellbeing by measurable amounts. If the numbers did not move, the money would be released back into recruitment. She had four decision points, beginning with a diagnosis due at the October board, to work out who the EUR 4.2 million was for.

## The Protagonist

Marieke Coppens, 44, had joined Vlaamse TechWorks in July 2026 from a Ghent logistics group, where she had run talent and development for a workforce of 4,000. Before that she had spent ten years in delivery-side line management at an engineering consultancy, carrying a utilisation target herself. The board had recruited her after her predecessor — the architect of the graduate programme that had won the awards — left for a competitor. Her own variable pay was tied to the same four measures the board had named. She had no operating history with any of the three groups whose budget she was about to move.

## The Decision

Coppens had to decide what was wrong with each of three workforce groups, what to do about it, how to divide EUR 4.2 million between them, and what would remain in place once the money was spent. Bart Vermeiren, chair of the works council, had written to the board in August: if another cycle passed with mid-career and senior staff receiving what he called token amounts, the council would file a formal grievance. The review rated that likelihood high. An Peeters, the delivery director, had written the same week: the graduate programme was the firm's only reliable source of engineers, and money taken off it would show up as a hiring gap within three years.

## The Company and its Setting

Vlaamse TechWorks NV, founded in Antwerp in 1998, built industrial software and factory-automation systems for manufacturers across northern Europe. It employed 2,600 people in Belgium, the Netherlands and a delivery centre in Poland, and reported FY2025 revenue of EUR 410 million at an 11% operating margin. Work was sold on named people: clients contracted for specific delivery engineers and specialists, and renewals followed them.

The workforce divided almost evenly into thirds by career stage: early-career engineers from the graduate programme, mid-career delivery and product staff, and long-tenured specialists whose expertise sat in control systems installed a decade or more earlier (see **Exhibit 2**). The regional engineering labour market was tight, and the credentials that mattered in it were portable: vendor and platform certifications travelled with the individual and were what competitors screened for.

## The Situation

Commissioned after the turnover figures and Vermeiren's first letter, the review scored the workforce on four measures the board adopted as the cycle's scorecard (see **Exhibit 1**). A career-sustainability index — one 0–100 reading combining employee health, motivation and contribution — stood at 41. Retention, the share of staff staying across a year, stood at 83%. Employability — how current the workforce's skills were against what the external market was hiring for, also 0–100 — stood at 48, most of the gap in the senior third. Wellbeing — how sustainable the effort asked of people was, again 0–100 — stood at 45, with sickness absence up 2.1 percentage points year on year.

The three groups broke differently. Early-career staff were the best developed and the most heavily loaded: always-on project schedules, and promotion criteria several of them described to the reviewers as unpublished. Mid-career staff had received no substantial development in four years or more, most were in the decade when caring for children or parents peaked, and their departure rate was 24%. Senior specialists held deep knowledge of installed systems and thin credentials in anything a recruiter screened for; their sickness-absence and disengagement scores were the worst in the company, and they were leaving at the lowest rate of the three.

Of the EUR 4.2 million development and wellbeing budget, 78% went to the early-career third, through the graduate programme, the elite training tracks and the accelerated promotion scheme. The remaining 22% covered everyone else.

Replacement cost ran to about EUR 55,000 per departure once recruitment fees, vacancy and ramp-up were counted. Turnover had run at 11.2% the year before; the rise to 17% meant about 150 more departures, roughly EUR 8.3 million on top of the EUR 24 million a year the firm already spent replacing people. Proposals already on Coppens's desk — from line managers, the works council and her own team — added up to more than EUR 4.2 million.

If nothing changed, turnover would stay near 17%, and near 24% in the group that held the accounts. At the end of the cycle the EUR 4.2 million would revert to recruitment, and Vermeiren's council would file.

## What Coppens Controlled

For the diagnosis she had to record what was driving the mid-career departures: a shortfall in skills that development spending could close; a shortfall in job design and life-stage fit; or a shortfall in pay and benefits against competitors. She had to record the same for the senior specialists: skills losing their currency in the external market; an individual withdrawal of effort; or a settled group unlikely to leave whatever was done. She had to state the single frame she would put to the board: return on the investment that paid back fastest; extension of the award-winning benefits programme; or coverage of every career stage. And she had to enter, between 0 and 100, the index reading she would be measured against.

For the plans she had to commit one intervention per group. For early-career staff: workload limits and a published growth path; more elite training and faster promotion; or no change to what they already received. For mid-career staff: a catalogue of classroom courses; a wellbeing package of an app and additional mental-health days; or internal mobility, career pathing and flexibility for carers. For senior specialists: withholding externally recognised certification; recognised reskilling with mentoring and knowledge-transfer roles; or a sabbatical and a recognition programme. She also had to set, as a percentage, how far to hold employability and wellbeing in deliberate balance rather than maximise one.

For the money she had to set the early-career share of the EUR 4.2 million anywhere between 20% and the current 78%, then split the remainder between mid-career and senior staff. She had to decide what early-career staff would receive in place of spending moved away from them: stretch assignments, mentoring roles and workload limits; a promise to revisit the question next year; or nothing. And she had to name, on the record, which single under-funded item carried the largest risk: executive coaching; senior employability and mid-career pathing; or early-career benefits.

For the final stage she had to answer a competitor recruiting the mid-career engineers her own money had just made more marketable: stop the reskilling; meet resignations with cash counter-offers; or pair raised skills with internal roles. She had to say what to do with the mid-cycle evidence: fund what was moving the measures and redesign what was not; hold every line as planned; or move money out of mid-career mobility into the senior programme. She had to decide what standing arrangement, if any, would outlive the budget cycle: none; life-stage career conversations, skills monitoring and workload limits; or an annual engagement survey. And she had to state, as a percentage, how firmly to put the case to the board.

## Closing

Coppens closed the review and wrote three headings on a single sheet — early-career, mid-career, senior — and under each a blank line for a number. The October board meeting was four weeks out. One cycle, EUR 4.2 million, and 78% of it already committed to one of the three lines before she had written a number on any of them.
