# Maasland Verzekeringen: The Stalled Rollout

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1968 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1968 — *Change Canvas* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, 14 April 2026, Annelies Kuipers opened the twelve-week adoption report for the new way of working at Maasland Verzekeringen N.V. and read the first line of it again. Daily active use of the new claims and underwriting workflow stood at 34%. The board had asked for 80% by month nine. Twelve weeks earlier, six teams had gone live to a full auditorium and a company-wide email; two of those six had since gone back to their spreadsheets.

Kuipers had nine months, twelve teams still to convert, and about EUR 790,000 left of a ring-fenced EUR 1.2 million. In month ten the platform licence came up for renewal, and the Chief Financial Officer had told the steering committee what he would do if sustained daily use across the network was still below 60%: cancel the licence and write off the EUR 1.2 million. She had to decide what the 34% meant, and then what to buy with the remaining money, in what order, and for whom.

## The Protagonist

Annelies Kuipers, 41, had joined Maasland in 2016 as a claims team leader, run the claims service centre for four years, and moved into the transformation office in 2024. She had been appointed Change Lead in January 2026, reporting to the Chief Operating Officer, Rutger Boersma, who had ring-fenced the budget and given her a single mandate: make the new behaviours stick, measurably, before the renewal decision. She had never run a programme across eighteen teams. Boersma had staked his credibility on the platform in front of the board.

## The Decision

Four readings of the stall circulated in that committee, each with an advocate. The communications lead held that people had not heard the message often enough — one launch event, one email campaign — and that the answer was more communication. The capability partner held that a single training session had not been enough and that people still could not use the tool. A line-manager champion held that managers were not using the workflow themselves or asking about it, so nothing survived the week after training. An operations representative held that a share of the staff did not want the change, would not take it up whatever was offered, and that the programme should plan around them.

The resistance number was being read three ways in the same room: as an obstacle to be pushed through with sponsor authority; as a diagnostic pointing at one specific gap; and as volatile pulse noise best set aside while the rollout proceeded. Kuipers also had to choose whom to engage first — the roughly 400 frontline staff who would use the workflow daily, the executive sponsors who could supply visible authority, or the line managers who ran the teams — and how narrowly to point the diagnosis at a single gap rather than spreading effort across all of them.

## The Company and its Setting

Maasland Verzekeringen N.V., headquartered in Utrecht, was a mid-market Dutch insurer with 2,100 employees and EUR 640 million in gross written premium. Its cost advantage over its peer group had been built over decades on careful, manual underwriting and claims handling. In 2025 it bought a low-code workflow platform to move both functions out of siloed, document-heavy processes and into cross-functional team working: daily stand-ups, shared queues, end-to-end case ownership, visual work management. Eighteen teams, roughly 600 people, were in scope.

Maasland had never run a structured change programme. Every previous system rollout had been announced by email, trained once, and left to settle. Adoption of those tools had settled near 40%.

## The Situation

The steering committee tracked five measures (see **Exhibit 1**). Daily active adoption counted the share of people in live teams who used the new workflow on an ordinary working day; it read 34%. The resistance index was a quarterly pulse score from 0 to 100 on which higher meant more resistance; it had risen from 28 before launch to 51, and the dominant free-text theme was "no one explained why, and my manager doesn't use it either." Change engagement, measured in points against the pre-launch baseline among live users, was down 14, and voluntary departures in the two reverted teams had picked up. Budget remaining stood at about EUR 790,000, EUR 410,000 having gone mostly on licences and the launch event. The fifth measure was a modelled time-to-embed: the months a team took to reach self-sustaining use. It read 7.0 months per team. The nine-month window allowed roughly 5 months per team, and only if waves overlapped.

The arithmetic of doing nothing was not in dispute. At 34% adoption and seven months to embed a team, the twelve remaining teams could not be converted inside the window; network-wide use would stay under the 60% line; the licence would be cancelled in month ten and the EUR 1.2 million written off (see **Exhibit 2**). Ingrid Molenaar, who ran claims operations, had put a shorter clock on it in the last committee meeting: if she could not see real adoption in her own area within two months, she would recommend reverting the whole division.

Two further facts sat inside the window. A group of line managers had made their scepticism plain, in the committee and in their teams. And a regulatory reporting deadline would take roughly 20% of frontline time for a month.

## What Kuipers Controlled

For the design phase in months one and two, Kuipers set three things and a percentage. Intervention intensity came at three levels: communications and e-learning only; the same plus workshops and floor coaching; or embedded change agents and manager bootcamps for every team. Communication cadence was either a one-off broadcast — a second town-hall and a fresh email campaign — or a sustained weekly two-way rhythm of team dialogue. Engagement order was frontline first, with the 400 staff trained thoroughly and managers following; managers first, with line managers enabled before frontline training; or both in parallel. She then set the share of that phase's spend front-loaded into line-manager enablement, anywhere from none of it to all of it.

For months two to seven she made four commitments. On the six live teams: stabilise them before launching anything new, launch new waves and let the reverted teams recover on their own, or stop supporting the two reverted teams and put everything into fresh waves. On finite change-agent and coaching capacity across the twelve remaining teams: the same light dose for every team, the heaviest dose for every team, or a dose matched team by team to readiness. On sequencing: all twelve at once, overlapping waves staggered at about five months per team, or strictly serial, with one wave fully embedded before the next began. And on money: commit EUR 250,000, EUR 500,000 or EUR 780,000 of the remainder to the wave plan now.

For months seven to nine she had four more. On the sceptical managers: overrule them with sponsor authority, hear what they feared and address it, or leave them to come round on their own. On the regulatory month: hold both the rollout and the reporting work at full pace, defer non-essential change activity while keeping the daily rhythm, or pause the rollout and restart afterwards. On what would hold the behaviour once the change agents left: mark each go-live as a success and let the momentum carry it, run a compliance audit that tracked non-users and escalated them to their managers, or install peer champions, sponsor presence and success metrics inside daily team routines. And on the board: lead with the strongest single number and leave the rest, ask for another nine months and another EUR 500,000, or lead with the full set of figures. Boersma had also asked her to record, as a percentage, how firmly she held her own proposition — that lasting adoption came from reinforcement and manager modelling rather than from launch energy.

## Closing

At the end of the meeting Kuipers wrote the eighteen teams down one side of a sheet and the remaining EUR 790,000 across the top. Molenaar's two months ran to mid-June; the board's target ran to month nine; the licence decision was fixed at month ten. Six teams were live, two of them already back on spreadsheets, and twelve were watching.
