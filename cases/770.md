# Kestrelmoor Analytics: The People Puzzle

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-770 · **Companion simulation:** Express 770 — *The People Puzzle: Motivating Your Team* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Monday, March 2, 2026, Imogen Thorley began her first week as a team manager at Kestrelmoor Analytics with a spreadsheet she had not asked for. Graham Ashdown, director of the firm's analytics practice, had sent it late on Friday: £12,000 for pay rises across her four-person team, to be allocated by Friday, March 13, in time for the April pay run. Under the figure he had pasted the team's February survey scores and one line of his own: *Your call. Tell me what you decided and why.*

The pay pool was the first of five people decisions Thorley would face over the next six months (see Exhibit 2). Ashdown would review the team in September. He had told her what he would look at: whether all four were still on the team, how engaged they were as a group and one by one, and whether the reasoning she wrote down matched what she actually did.

## The Protagonist

Thorley was thirty-one. She had spent six years as an analyst and then senior analyst in Kestrelmoor's forecasting group, where she had built the demand models that two of the firm's largest retail clients still used. She had never managed anyone. Her new team had been without a manager since January, when its previous lead left to join a client, and nobody had held a one-to-one with its members for seven weeks.

In February the firm had enrolled her in its new-manager programme. Its first module covered four theories of work motivation. Its one standing requirement was a decision log: for every people decision, one line recording what the manager had done and which of the four theories the decision applied.

## The Decision

Thorley had to choose, five times, a single move for a team whose four members wanted different things, and to name, each time, the theory she was applying. Every move would reach all four of them, whether it was aimed at one person or at the whole team. The first was due in eleven days.

## The Company and its Setting

Kestrelmoor Analytics was a mid-size consulting firm headquartered in Leeds. It built data models, dashboards and forecasts for retail, logistics and public-sector clients, and it billed most of its work by the analyst-day. A team like Thorley's typically combined analysts, who carried the client work; a data technician, who maintained the pipelines and databases the analysts drew on; and graduates on the firm's two-year development scheme.

In late February the firm's leadership had opened a review of its operating model. Managers had been told it would report in the spring. Nobody below the leadership team knew what it would recommend, although the word on the floor was restructure.

## The Situation

The team used first names. Ravi Bhandal, twenty-three, a junior analyst in his second year, was the most engaged of the four, and he asked at almost every opportunity when he would get a proper project of his own. Eleni Kostaki, thirty-four, a senior analyst and the team's most productive member, had said in her last appraisal that her pay was fine and that the work had stopped challenging her the year before. Martin Pryce, fifty-two, a data technician with nineteen years at Kestrelmoor, had asked twice since January whether his job would be safe next quarter. Zara Adeyemi, twenty-one, had joined the graduate scheme in September 2025 and still did not know what was expected of her (see Exhibit 1).

The firm's quarterly survey scored each employee from 0 to 100 on engagement and on intent to stay; a delivery score on the same scale tracked each person's productivity. In February the team averaged 60 on engagement, 62.5 on intent to stay and 67.5 on productivity. Eleni's intent to stay, at 45, was the lowest figure on the sheet; Martin's, at 80, was the highest. Ashdown read the team's average intent to stay as its retention risk, which he graded low, medium or high.

Nothing on the sheet was going to improve by itself. The pay pool had to be allocated before the April run, one way or another. Eleni had not asked for anything. Martin's question would not get quieter once the review reported. Ravi and Zara would keep asking theirs.

## What Thorley Controlled

At each of the five points Thorley chose one move from the options open to her and logged it against one of the programme's four theories. The programme's assessors held their own view of which theory each move belonged to, and Ashdown would see how often her tags agreed with theirs.

For **the pay pool**, she could split the £12,000 equally across the four; give the bulk to the two strongest performers, Eleni and Ravi; direct a larger share at Eleni as the team's retention risk; or give everyone a smaller rise together with written recognition of their strongest work.

For **Eleni**, she could offer a formal promotion with a pay step-up; ownership of a strategic client project; flexible working hours; a one-off cash bonus; or a completely new challenge outside her comfort zone.

For **Martin**, if the review produced the restructure the floor expected, her first move could be to reassure him one-to-one that his role was safe; to push him to embrace the change quickly; to offer him a voluntary early-retirement package; to involve him in designing the new team structure; or to say nothing and let him process it on his own.

For **Ravi and Zara**, with the budget tight by the summer, she could give Ravi a stretch assignment and pair Zara with a mentor; promise Ravi a promotion in six months; set up structured weekly check-ins for Zara alone; put Ravi on a high-visibility project and leave Zara to settle in; or concentrate on delivery for the quarter and postpone both conversations.

For **the team offsite** in September, with £5,000, she could launch a performance-based bonus scheme; run a two-day retreat on purpose and belonging; hold a job-enrichment workshop to redesign each role with more responsibility; or introduce an autonomy framework that set goals rather than hours.

After each move the next survey would show where each of the four stood on engagement, intent to stay and productivity; the team averages for engagement and productivity; Ashdown's retention-risk grade; and the share of her logged tags that had matched the assessors'.

## Closing

At 18:40 on Monday, Thorley opened the spreadsheet again. Four names, three columns of scores, one figure at the top: £12,000. Her calendar showed one-to-ones with all four on Wednesday morning. The decision log was open in the next window, and its first line was still blank.
