# Helio Verde: The First Hundred Days

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1500 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1500 — *The CEO First 100 Days — Diagnose, Don't Decree* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the evening of her ninth day as chief executive of Helio Verde S.A., Lucía Arenas-Beltrán sat in a half-furnished office in Escazú, Costa Rica, and read the single sheet her chief of staff had pinned to the corkboard. Tomás Ulloa had titled it *Diagnostic Phase — Listening Cadence*. It sorted the people she needed to hear into six groups, counted fifty touchpoints across them, and gave the vacant chief financial officer's seat a card of its own, in red ink. An hour earlier Doña Carmen Iturbide, a retired chief executive who had agreed to mentor her, had called and asked her four questions. Arenas-Beltrán had not been able to answer the third.

Her first ten-day window closed at the end of Day 10. By then she had to settle two things: how her working time would be divided across the six groups, and how the diagnosis itself would be run. The same two kinds of choice would come back nine more times, and each window would bring its own question. At the end of Day 100 she would send the chair a memo on what she had concluded.

## The Protagonist

Arenas-Beltrán had been recruited from Mexico City, after a career at a global beverages company running successively larger commercial businesses across Latin America. She had never run a multi-category food business or worked in Costa Rica. Manuel Beltrán-Rojas, the chair — a former bank chief executive, and no relation — had told the board that Helio Verde needed a leader who had not yet learned which questions were not allowed to be asked.

She had been promoted, again and again, for being the first person in the room with a recommendation. Analysts, several of her new colleagues and part of the board expected the same of her now.

## The Decision

For each window she had to decide what share of her working time went to each of the six groups, and which stance to take on the window's question.

For the first window the question was how the diagnosis would be run, and three courses were on the table.

The first was to begin with the cabinet and leave any independent review for later. The cabinet expected to be heard before anyone else, and Renata Coralles, the chief operating officer, had built much of the way the company operated. This course avoided early conflict with the executives she would depend on to run the business. Its cost was that her picture of the business would reach her through the people who ran it.

The second was to ask the board's audit committee to commission an independent review — a *shadow audit* by an outside firm reporting to the committee rather than to her — and to keep her time with the cabinet to a minimum. The board had not asked for one; the committee could commission it without the cabinet. This course gave her an account of the business that did not pass through the people responsible for it. Its cost was that the cabinet would hear less from her while outsiders examined its work.

The third was to run both from Day 1: the full cabinet listening cadence and the audit-committee review in parallel. The review would be added to Tomás's cabinet conversations rather than replace them. This course gave her the cabinet's account and an outside one from the first day. Its cost was the heaviest call on her time, and an early finding from the outsiders could harden the cabinet against her before she had heard it out.

Tomás's draft split of her time for Days 1–10 gave the largest shares to the cabinet and to the chair and board (see Exhibit 2). Coralles, whose cooperation every operating decision would need, held that the cabinet knew the business better than anyone below it, and that a chief executive who went around it in her first weeks would find it harder to lead when decisions came due. Beatriz Solís, the chief human resources officer, held that people one layer down and on the plant floors and sales routes knew things the cabinet did not, and would say them only to someone who came to ask. Manuel expected regular time for himself and the board, whose backing she would need for whatever the diagnosis concluded. Tomás would redraft the split for each window; she was free to depart from it.

## The Company and its Setting

Helio Verde S.A. was a branded consumer-foods company headquartered in Escazú and publicly listed. It made its products in plants in Costa Rica and Guatemala, La Sabana outside San José among them, and sold them through neighbourhood shops, route-sales teams and supermarket chains across Central America; a few large retail customers were its anchor accounts.

The cabinet was inherited. Tomás, a former management consultant, had served her predecessor. Coralles had spent most of her career at Helio Verde and had run its largest plant before becoming chief operating officer. The controller was covering the finance seat as interim.

## The Situation

The figures she inherited were few (see Exhibit 1). Organic revenue growth sat well short of the stretch target her predecessor had set, and that target still stood as the company's stated ambition. A multi-year systems transformation, launched before she arrived, was the project several cabinet members raised first when she asked what mattered most. Analysts had been asking since her appointment when she would set out a strategy.

Tomás and the chair had agreed on six readings for the hundred days, each on a scale of 0 to 100. *Diagnostic quality*, starting at 50, reflected how sound her reading of the business was becoming. *Chair confidence*, at 50, reflected Manuel's trust in the way she was running the period. *Analyst pressure*, at 60, reflected how hard analysts and investors were pressing for a statement of strategy; on this reading, lower was better. *Cabinet trust*, at 70, reflected the executive committee's confidence in her. *Organizational truth*, at 30, reflected how much of what people below the cabinet knew had reached her. A *composite score*, at 52, combined the five.

After each window Tomás would log her split, a *balance* reading out of 100 of how well it fitted that window's demands, a *posture* reading out of 100 of how her stance held up, the composite, and a count of listening touchpoints by group. At the end, the run of composites would be summarised in an overall assessment.

If she changed nothing — kept the routine her predecessor had left and took no position — the plans she had inherited would stand as they were, the finance seat would stay empty, and the analysts would keep asking.

## What Arenas-Beltrán Controlled

In each window she set six shares of her time, summing to 100%: the cabinet; the layer below it; the frontline in plants, route sales and operations; the anchor-account customers; the chair and board; and the external ecosystem of regulators, industry peers and analysts. She also chose her stance on the window's question from the courses her team set out. She did not control when the questions arrived or who raised them.

## Day 9

Arenas-Beltrán took a legal pad from the side table and wrote the six groups down the left margin, leaving a column beside them for the percentages. Below the list she wrote Doña Carmen's third question from memory. The first window closed the next evening.
