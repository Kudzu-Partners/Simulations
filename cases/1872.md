# The Competition Commission of India: Merger Under Scrutiny

**Case Code:** RSK-2026-1872 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1872 — *Merger Under Scrutiny — The CCI Antitrust Economist* · v1.0 · August 2026

---

On the morning of 3 August 2026, the thirty-day clock on Meenal Deshpande's desk ran out. The Competition Commission of India had spent that window deciding whether a notified merger between two of the country's largest packaged-edible-oil sellers deserved a second look, and the answer was yes: the Commission had formed the preliminary view that the deal risked an Appreciable Adverse Effect on Competition. The case moved that day from a first look to a full investigation, and the file landed with Deshpande and the rest of the Combination Division's economics cell.

Deshpande had to produce the competitive-effects assessment the Commission would rely on to decide the case, and defend it if either side appealed to the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, the NCLAT. She had 210 working days from that morning to get an order in front of the Commission, and the two companies were pressing for speed: their financing for the deal lapsed if approval slipped past that limit.

## The Protagonist

Deshpande was forty-one and had spent six years in the Combination Division's economics cell, the group inside the CCI that turns a notified merger into a number the Commission can act on. She had come to the Commission from a research role in competition economics and had carried three merger reviews through to an order, one of them contested to the appellate tribunal and upheld. Colleagues who had worked a case with her knew her for insisting that every share and every price estimate in a filing be checked against the underlying data before it went into a draft order; the parties' counsel on this file already knew it too. Her standing inside the Division rose or fell with whether her orders held up on appeal, and this file was the biggest she had led.

## The Decision

The review ran in four stages, each built on the last (see Exhibit 2). First, how widely or narrowly to draw the market the deal would be judged against, a choice that would set every share and every concentration number that followed. Second, how concentrated that market would become, and what the concentration numbers actually proved. Third, how much prices would likely rise, and how much of the acquirer's claimed cost savings were real and would reach shoppers rather than the acquirer's own shareholders. Fourth, a recommendation, clear the deal, block it, or clear it with conditions, defensible to the Commission's bench under the clock. Each stage's finding would sit permanently on the case file the Commission ultimately read.

## The Company and its Setting

The merger paired Aarav Foods Ltd., which held about 27 percent of the national branded edible-oils market, with Sundara Consumer Brands, which held about 18 percent; the market leader held 31 percent. Aarav had offered ₹14,200 crore, all cash, for Sundara. The two companies sold overlapping products, refined sunflower and soyabean oil, and retailers who dealt with both told investigators that shoppers treated an Aarav bottle and a Sundara bottle as near-interchangeable, switching between them mainly on price and promotion. That closeness was exactly what made a regulator look twice at a merger between rivals. Aarav's own submission argued the opposite case: combining the two companies' procurement and refining would generate ₹900 crore a year in synergies that would lower prices, not raise them. Whether that claim survived scrutiny was one of the four questions in front of Deshpande.

## The Situation

The data room gave Deshpande a starting picture, not a finished one (see Exhibit 1). In the national market for branded sunflower oil, the standard measure of market concentration stood at roughly 1,950 before the merger and was estimated to rise to roughly 2,920 after it, a jump of about 970 points against review thresholds of 100 to 150. A preliminary pricing model put the likely price rise on the affected products at 6 to 11 percent, translating to an estimated ₹1,400 to ₹2,600 crore a year in higher costs to shoppers if the deal's terms went unchanged. Against that sat Aarav's ₹900 crore synergy claim.

The Commission tracked Deshpande's file against four measures. One asked whether the eventual order protected shoppers of a staple household good, and stood at 40 out of 100 before she filed a single finding. A second asked whether her findings rested on tested evidence rather than assertion, and stood at 25. A third asked whether the order would hold up on appeal to the NCLAT, and stood at 30. A fourth measured her exposure to getting the call wrong in either direction, wrongly clearing a harmful deal or wrongly blocking a benign one, and stood at 50, where lower was better. The 210-day clock read zero. If she filed nothing and the case simply ran on the companies' own account of the market, the merger would clear on a filing nobody at the Commission had independently tested.

## What Deshpande Controlled

First, she set the market's boundaries. She could accept a market as broad as all edible oils together, mustard, palm, sunflower and soyabean combined, under which the merged company's share came to roughly 22 percent and the concentration numbers barely moved; draw it narrowly around just the branded sunflower and soyabean oil where Aarav and Sundara competed head-to-head; or draw it narrower still, around Aarav's own product line alone. Geographically, she could treat the whole country as one market, treat the three states where combined share exceeded 55 percent as markets of their own alongside the national one, or treat pricing as a function of a global commodity market traded on world exchanges. She also had to decide how she reached those lines: by accepting the boundary the two companies proposed in their own filing, by reusing a market definition an earlier order had used for a similar product without re-checking whether it still fit, or by testing the boundary herself against the switching-survey and diversion data on file.

Second, she decided what the concentration numbers meant: treat the jump itself as proof of harm and stop there, set the numbers aside on the view that market share alone never proves higher prices, or treat the breach as a signal shifting the burden onto the two companies to justify the deal. She also had to decide whether to build her case around the merged company alone finding it profitable to raise prices, around the shrunken field of rivals finding it easier to move prices together, or around both, and how many of the four candidate markets she would classify as presumptively harmful, from none of them to all four.

Third, she picked a diversion ratio, the share of sales one brand would lose to the other on a price rise, anywhere from 5 to 60 percent; switching surveys and retailer testimony on file put the real figure around 35 to 45 percent. On the synergy claim, she could credit the full ₹900 crore as filed, apply a test that counted only the portion specific to this merger, independently verifiable, and shown to reach shoppers rather than stopping at the acquirer's margin, or decline to credit any of it. Whatever she credited, she still had to decide what share of it, from zero to 100 percent, would reach the shelf price rather than the shareholder.

Fourth, she settled on a recommendation: clear the deal outright, block it outright, or clear it subject to conditions addressing the harm directly. If conditions, a multi-year cap on prices and supply commitments running across thousands of individual products, or a requirement that the two companies sell the overlapping sunflower-oil brand and a refinery in the affected states to an independent buyer. She also had to decide which mistake she would rather risk when evidence left room for doubt: clearing a harmful deal, blocking a benign one, or holding the order strictly to the evidence regardless of which risk that implied. And she had to state, as a percentage, how much conviction she could put behind defending the order to the bench on time, on the evidence.

## Closing

Deshpande opened a blank finding sheet at 9:15 a.m. Four stages, thirteen separate calls, and 210 working days that had just started counting down. The parties' lawyers were already drafting their submission on why the broad market was the right one. She had not yet written a word.
