# Lakeshore Executive Education: Past the Wish List

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1824 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1824 — *Custom Build* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 5 October 2026, Renata Voss, Executive Director of custom programmes at Lakeshore Executive Education, opened a request for proposal from Aurora Health Networks, a $9,000,000,000 hospital system, and started a clock she could not stop. Aurora's newly appointed chief executive had told Lakeshore, in a discovery call days earlier, that her 180 senior clinical and operational leaders "manage budgets but cannot lead change." The RFP itself asked for something broader — training in strategy, finance, innovation, and resilience, for all 180 leaders at once — and gave Voss three weeks to answer, against two rival schools.

Somewhere between the CEO's one sentence and the RFP's four demands sat the design Lakeshore actually had to propose, funded inside a fixed $1,200,000, launched within four months, and expected to earn a three-year, $4,000,000 renewal on nothing but what its first year proved.

## The Protagonist

Voss, 49, had run Lakeshore's custom-programmes practice for four years, promoted out of the unit's academic-partnerships team after an earlier career advising hospital systems on operations. She had built her name on client retention: several of her largest relationships had stayed with her more than five years. But the practice's numbers told a specific story: clients who signed with Lakeshore rated it a Net Promoter Score of 71, comfortably ahead of the executive-education field, yet Lakeshore won only 38% of the competitive proposals it entered. Clients loved the delivery. Too many chose someone else before they found that out. Aurora was a flagship name in the sector Lakeshore wanted to grow, and the outcome of this bid would be read as a referendum on Voss's own record this year.

## The Decision

Voss had to submit, inside three weeks, a design for developing Aurora's 180 leaders — one that had to clear four separate tests before anyone could call it a win. It had to answer the CEO's actual problem rather than the RFP's topic list. It had to fit inside $1,200,000 without hollowing out the parts of the design that changed behaviour. It had to launch within four months and run nine, in cohorts small enough that Aurora's wards stayed staffed. And it had to promise evidence, not a satisfaction score, that Aurora's CEO could take to her own board before renewing. Every dollar Lakeshore would commit, and everything it would tell Aurora to expect, ran through decisions Voss had not yet made.

## The Company and its Setting

Lakeshore Executive Education, based in Ann Arbor, Michigan, was the custom-programmes arm of a top-ranked North American business school: about $38,000,000 in annual revenue, a roster of 90 faculty spanning tenured professors, professors of practice, and vetted outside practitioners. Its signature product was the immersive corporate residency — a multi-day, multi-modal experience blending faculty teaching with live projects, coaching, and structured reflection — which was why its NPS ran ahead of the field (see **Exhibit 1**).

Aurora Health Networks was a $9,000,000,000 hospital system whose new chief executive had inherited 180 senior clinical and operational leaders who could run a budget but, in her own words, could not lead change through the people who reported to them. She had put a custom leadership programme out to competitive bid, invited Lakeshore alongside two rival schools, and given all three three weeks to respond.

## The Situation

The numbers bounding Lakeshore's proposal were fixed before a single module was written. Aurora would pay $1,200,000 total — a sum that, at Lakeshore's cost structure, funded roughly 18 days of faculty time and two weeks of residency, not an open-ended curriculum. The programme had to launch within four months of signature and finish within nine, ahead of the CEO's first full strategy cycle. The 180 leaders could not all leave their hospitals at once, so the design had to move them through in stages rather than a single sitting. And the CEO had said plainly that she would renew — a three-year relationship worth $4,000,000 — only on what the first year actually proved, not on how the launch felt (see **Exhibit 1**).

Two failures sat on either side of the design. A proposal that simply mirrored the RFP's four topics would likely price out above what $1,200,000 could fund and lose to a rival that read the brief more sharply, extending a win rate that already lost most of what it entered. A proposal that won the bid but spent past what the budget, the calendar, or the faculty roster could sustain would face the opposite failure: a signed contract, an exhausted faculty bench, and a first year that gave Aurora's CEO no reason to spend $4,000,000 more.

## What Voss Controlled

Before any budget was spent, Voss had to decide what to name as the real challenge. One reading of the discovery call and the RFP together was that Aurora's leaders shared a single behavioural gap: competent operators who had never had to lead change through other people. A second reading took the RFP's four headings at face value and built a content track for each — strategy, finance, innovation, resilience. A third located the problem one level down, in a technical shortfall in financial and strategic knowledge that better teaching could close. A fourth read the complaint as motivational: leaders who had the skills but not the drive. Attached to that was who, among the 180, actually needed developing: all of them together as one identical population, all of them segmented into a shared core tailored by clinical and operational role, or only the twenty most senior, leaving the rest untouched. Underneath both sat a third question: how much time to spend testing the RFP's framing against the discovery call before designing at all.

The design that followed had to become an architecture. Voss would set how much of the nine months leaned on experience — a live project, a simulation, coaching — against faculty teaching; whether the learning journey was built backward from the change Aurora needed to see, assembled forward from the RFP's own topic menu, or front-loaded with faculty content and a project added later; whether the 180 moved through four cohorts of roughly forty-five, two of about ninety, or one wave of all 180 together; and how many distinct modules the nine months would carry.

Then the budget itself (see **Exhibit 2**). Eighteen faculty-days had to split between a marquee tenured strategist, priced at $22,000 a day, and professors of practice at $9,000 a day. A change-leadership simulation built for Aurora cost $60,000; an off-the-shelf equivalent cost $20,000; or the simulation could be dropped in favour of more faculty teaching. An executive-coaching pool billed at $450 an hour had to be sized per leader across all 180. And a separate line had to fund operations — logistics, design staff, materials — all of it inside $1,200,000 and above the 30% gross margin the practice needed to keep its faculty roster available for other clients.

Only then came the pitch. Voss had to decide what Lakeshore would contract to measure — pre/post change-readiness plus a completed improvement project per leader, a mix of satisfaction and self-reported confidence, or an end-of-programme satisfaction score. She had to set what Net Promoter Score to promise the CEO: a number at Lakeshore's own benchmark of 71, a higher number meant to beat the rivals outright, or no number at all, describing it in words instead. She had to decide how to frame the design when the CEO pushed back — around the diagnosis and the evidence it would produce, around a promise to transform leadership across all four topics, or around the school's faculty names and brand. And she had to decide how hard to hold that frame under direct challenge.

## Closing

Voss closed the RFP document, opened a blank one-page scoping template, and looked at the three weeks left on her calendar. Aurora's CEO had already told her, almost in so many words, what she believed was wrong with her 180 leaders. The RFP she had actually issued asked for something else. Before her team wrote a single module, Voss had to decide which of the two documents she was designing against.
