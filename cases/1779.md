# Maison Lumière: Twenty-One Days to Pitch the Impossible

**Case Code:** ENT-2026-1779 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1779 — *Create the Improbable* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 September 2026, Camille Auriol watched Maison Lumière's board close the folder on "Cocotte 2027" in under four minutes. The proposal her twenty-two-person innovation studio had spent three months preparing — a lighter lid and a new colour range for the company's flagship cast-iron pot — was set aside without a vote. The chief executive, who had created the studio in 2023 and could just as easily close it, gave Auriol a different assignment instead: bring back something the market would call impossible, and prove the studio could actually ship it, or there would be no more budget cycles to argue about. Auriol had twenty-one days until a twelve-minute pitch to the board and two outside investors, a fixed working budget of EUR 180,000 to get there, and the studio's EUR 3.2 million annual budget riding on what she brought into the room.

## The Protagonist

Auriol, 41, had led the studio since its founding, recruited from a Lyon design consultancy for never having worked in cookware. In three years she had shipped two modest line extensions and one abandoned packaging redesign, none of which had required the company to give anything up. Her studio's twenty-two salaries were a rounding error against Maison Lumière's EUR 410 million in revenue, which was exactly why the board could close it in an afternoon if the next pitch failed to justify it. Auriol reported to the chief executive directly, but the studio's real approval sat with the board chair, a member of the founding family who had said publicly that Maison Lumière had never discontinued a single product in 134 years and did not intend to start now.

## The Decision

Auriol had four decisions ahead of her, spread across three weeks, each closing off what the next could be: which question the studio would actually answer, rather than which better pan to design; which single disruptive exercise to run with part of the EUR 180,000, and how to judge the ideas it produced; which part of Maison Lumière's existing business — a product, a rule, or a margin — the new idea would have to displace, and how openly to say so; and who to bring onside before the twelve minutes in front of people who could end the studio.

## The Company and its Setting

Maison Lumière S.A., founded in 1892 and headquartered in Lyon, sold enamelled cast-iron cookware in 38 countries and posted EUR 410 million in 2025 revenue at a 9% EBIT margin, with 1,850 employees (see Exhibit 1). Its Cocotte Classique Dutch oven had once generated 31% of group revenue; unit sales had fallen 6% a year for four consecutive years as younger buyers moved to lighter, induction-friendly pans from newer, digital-native brands. At the current rate the Cocotte would fall below 20% of revenue within three years, taking roughly EUR 25 million of contribution with it. Two rival brands were rumoured to launch "smart" induction cookware within nine months, and retail buyers had warned that another cosmetic update would cost 15% of shelf facings. In 134 years, the company had never discontinued a single product.

## The Situation

Inside the studio, Auriol kept a private scorecard (see Exhibit 2). Against a target of 100, how far the team had changed the question — rather than just the pan — stood at 25. How convincingly it could prove an idea would ship stood at 30. Its standing with the people who would decide its fate stood at 40. She had EUR 180,000 to spend over three weeks, competing against the twenty-one days on the clock. If she walked in with a version of the brief the board had already rejected, the arithmetic did not change: the Cocotte's decline would continue at 6% a year, the rival launches would arrive on schedule, and the studio's EUR 3.2 million would not survive the next review.

## What Auriol Controlled

The first decision was which brief to pursue. Four options sat on the table: restating what cooking itself might become as a ritual in 2035; asking whether heat, rather than the pan, could become the product; producing a lighter Cocotte in a new range of colours; or tuning the Cocotte specifically for induction cooktops to compete with newer entrants. Attached to that choice was a second judgment, set on a hundred-point dial that opened at 55: how far the restated problem sat above "a nicer Cocotte." A third judgment set how defensible the resulting category would be — at one end, 134 years of heat-and-material expertise no rival could copy quickly; in the middle, a credible category two known rivals could still chase; at the other end, an idea any competitor with a factory could reproduce within a season.

The second decision committed part of the EUR 180,000 to one disruptive exercise. Inverting the brief and designing cookware built to disrupt the kitchen it entered cost EUR 45,000 over four days. Fusing cast iron with a category it had never touched cost EUR 55,000 over five days. Building a mocked-up 2035 museum label and reverse-engineering the object behind it cost EUR 70,000 over seven days. Running an ordinary cross-functional brainstorm with the whole team cost EUR 20,000 over two days. A second dial, opening at 60%, set how long the team would hold off judging the resulting ideas on feasibility. A third choice set the standard those ideas would be judged against: the reframed brief's long-run originality and value, whether they would sell next quarter, or whatever the room found easiest to agree on.

The third decision named what the new category would cost the old one: retiring the heritage Cocotte Classique line over the next twenty-four months; abandoning the internal rule that everything Maison Lumière made had to be cast iron; moving from a high-margin unit-sale model to a subscription model; or adding the new line beside the existing catalogue with nothing retired. Whichever she chose, finance's own estimate put a genuinely new category's first-year cost to Cocotte sales at EUR 8–12 million, and Auriol had to decide, between zero and EUR 25 million, what figure to book openly to the board. A separate decision set what the team would carry into the room as proof: a working prototype plus three commitments — a named supplier, a cost estimate, and a signed pilot retailer; a polished render and a short film, with no physical object behind them; or nothing at all, trusting the concept and the pitch itself to carry the room.

The fourth decision chose who to turn into a champion before the pitch: the chief executive, who had issued the challenge and could be briefed to defend it in the room; the board chair, whose family ties made her the loudest voice against retiring anything; the finance function, which could present the cannibalisation figure itself rather than leave it to land as a surprise; or no one, leaving the pitch to make its own case without any internal backing. A second choice set how to answer the chair when the displaced Cocotte sales came up: naming the figure first and then arguing the new category's value exceeded it; describing the new line as additive to existing sales and moving the conversation on quickly; or offering to keep the heritage line fully intact regardless of what the new category required. A third choice set the order of the twelve minutes: the reframe, the exercise, the displacement decision and the prototype in sequence, ending with the ask; the long-term vision first, saving the supporting proof for a later appendix; or the financial model and the size of the request, opened before anything else. A final dial, beginning at 70%, measured how composed the team stayed under the two challenges the board was known to raise.

## Closing

At eight the next morning, Auriol pinned the four decisions to the studio wall — the brief, the exercise, what would be destroyed, and who would carry her into the room — and looked at the two numbers underneath: EUR 180,000, and twenty-one days. Both were still whole. The board chair and the two investors would be sitting across from her in three weeks, and Auriol had not yet written down which brief the team would pursue.
