# Wasatch Analytics Group: Hold the Line

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1917 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1917 — *Boundary Work* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

At 6:40 on the evening of Monday, 1 June 2026, Priya Raman stood in her kitchen in Salt Lake City with two messages open on the same phone. The first came from the chief information officer of NorthPeak Retail, Wasatch Analytics Group's flagship client. The stalled analytics rollout was now a war-room sprint: fifteen working days to a board deadline three weeks out, a stand-up every morning at seven, and "real-time" replies from Raman personally in between. The second was from her child's daycare, closed for the next two weeks. Her partner read both over her shoulder and said, "I can't absorb another one of these."

Raman had until the first stand-up the following morning to decide how she would run the three weeks. The arithmetic she wrote on an envelope that night did not resolve. The sprint, her other engagements, her seven direct reports and the home load came to roughly seventy hours a week; she could sustain about fifty (**Exhibit 1**).

## The Protagonist

Priya Raman, 34, had been a Senior Engagement Manager at Wasatch for four months. Before the promotion she had been the firm's most reliable individual contributor on retail analytics: the person partners named when a client was difficult and a deadline short, and who had answered at eleven at night for six years because answering was what she was praised for. The new role gave her seven people, a flagship account and a title. It had not given her different habits. She was sleeping under six hours a night and had cancelled two personal commitments that week.

## The Decision

Raman had to decide how to meet a twenty-hour weekly shortfall that no rearrangement of her calendar could close: what kind of problem she was facing, which parts of the week she would change and how, what she would say to the client, to her skip-level boss and to her partner, and what rule she would follow when a message arrived outside whatever cadence she set.

## The Company and its Setting

Wasatch Analytics Group, founded in 2012 in Salt Lake City, sold consulting, data engineering and a fast-growing managed-analytics service. Revenue was US$58 million, growing 24% a year, across 340 employees. Billable consultants ran at 88% of capacity.

The firm had no written expectations about availability. Responsiveness was the unwritten culture: the managers who answered fastest got the accounts. An internal pulse survey that spring had put a number on the cost. Thirty-one per cent of managers reported "always-on" expectations and rising exhaustion, and voluntary turnover among high-performing managers had reached 17% against a firm average of 11%.

NorthPeak Retail was the firm's flagship client and Raman's account. Its CIO had escalated the rollout himself, and the cadence he asked for was addressed to her by name.

## The Situation

Wasatch's manager development programme kept four measures on every engagement manager, each on a scale of 0 to 100, and Raman's stood where six years of answering at eleven at night had left them (**Exhibit 2**). Well-being, tracking sleep, energy and exhaustion, stood at 22. Sprint Performance, covering delivery on her account and her standing with the client, stood at 50. Boundary Credibility, which recorded whether limits she stated turned out to be limits that held, stood at 15; she had never stated one. Team-Norm Health, which recorded whether her seven reports followed the example she set rather than the instructions she gave, stood at 25. Beneath the four sat a running total: the weekly demand-capacity gap, 20.0 hours.

The seven were the part Raman had not counted. Two had begun sending her analysis after ten at night, and neither had been asked to; they had watched her status stay active and taken it as the job's standard.

If she changed nothing, the position was arithmetic rather than drama: seventy hours of demand against fifty of capacity for fifteen working days, the daycare closed for ten of them, and the twenty-hour difference coming out of sleep already under six hours.

## What Raman Controlled

**How she named it.** Four readings were open to her, and the plan would follow whichever she chose: that being reachable was what she believed made her valuable, and that belief was colliding with being a present parent; that the week was a scheduling problem, to be met by rebuilding the calendar; that it was an effort problem, to be met by pushing harder for three weeks; or that it was a client problem, NorthPeak having asked for more than was reasonable and needing to be told so. Alongside it sat a question about herself: she had always liked weaving short bursts of work into the evening, and she could design the three weeks around that preference, prescribe a strict separation of the two domains regardless of it, or treat the question as immaterial. She also had to name what was at stake in her sense of herself — the belief that constant availability was the source of her value, a shortfall in the skills a four-month-old job demanded, or nothing beyond three hard weeks — and how candidly she would say it.

**What she changed.** Twenty hours a week had to come from somewhere, and there were four places to take them from, in whatever proportions she chose: handing the 7:00am stand-up to a deputy lead; blocking protected hours with no messages after eight in the evening; keeping a defined workspace and leaving the laptop in it; and renegotiating the cadence with the CIO and her own team. The conversation with the CIO had three shapes. She could put a cadence in writing — one brief at eight each morning, replies by noon the same day. She could accept "real-time" as asked. Or she could raise nothing and simply stop replying after seven in the evening. Then there was how firmly she would hold whatever she announced.

**What she said.** Raman needed one sentence about what she was worth to the firm: that her value was judgement, outcomes and running a team of seven; that it was being the most responsive manager at Wasatch; or that it was best protected by stepping back from NorthPeak. Her skip-level boss had told her that week, "You're our most responsive manager — that's why we trust you with NorthPeak," and a reply was owed: thanks, then NorthPeak's outcomes and a delivery plan without midnights; a flat refusal of the label and what it asked; or a grateful acceptance of it, proved through the sprint. At home a second conversation was owed: naming the daycare plan, the protected evenings and the renegotiated cadence; asking her partner to absorb three more weeks, promised as the last; or carrying it quietly and adding nothing to the tension. And one limit had to be nominated as the one she would hold when holding it cost her: the hour of her child's bedtime, every night of the sprint; both weekend days across the sprint; or none, leaving every hour negotiable.

**What she settled in advance.** Some of it Raman preferred to decide before it arrived. An urgent message from the CIO outside the agreed cadence had three answers: acknowledge it, confirm it would be handled at the morning brief, and leave it there; take it up at once and work it through the night; or not reply. She had to fix where a deliberate exception belonged — for a genuine risk to the board deadline only, named, time-boxed and announced as an exception; never, holding the line whatever it cost NorthPeak; or whenever refusing would be awkward. She had to decide whether what she settled for herself became the standard for the seven: async by default after hours with a rotating on-call, set as the team's rule and followed by her; her own arrangements only, the team's left to the team; or a rule announced to the team and not applied to herself. And she had to choose what she would watch — sleep and after-hours message volume now, with sprint milestones and turnover risk later; the next pulse survey and the exit interviews alone; or nothing in particular — and how firmly she would defend a sustainable pace against an always-on one.

## Closing

At 10:15 that night Raman left the envelope on the counter, the twenty circled between the seventy and the fifty. The first stand-up was in eight hours.
