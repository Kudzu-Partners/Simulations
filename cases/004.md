## Abstract

Nexus Corporation, a USD 4.2 billion diversified Latin American conglomerate with business units in financial services, retail, industrial manufacturing, logistics, and customer service, had committed USD 150 million over three years to an enterprise-wide AI program. In March 2026 the newly formed C-suite Steering Committee — chaired by Chief Strategy Officer Daniela Fuentes — had to ratify a Round 1 portfolio: which of five Lighthouse programs to fund, at what level, under which sourcing mode (Build, Buy, or Partner), and at what setting on three governance toggles — transparency, data-sharing, and automation pace. A known event table, compiled from legal, risk, and strategic advisors, listed twelve discrete events that could fire across the three-year horizon — from an INAI regulatory investigation in Mexico to a viral public AI failure. Each event punished specific portfolio shapes and specific governance settings. Fuentes had to present a single recommendation, supported by a quantitative model and a governance rationale, to a committee that did not yet agree.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate an AI capital-allocation portfolio at enterprise scale and articulate the concentration-versus-diversification trade-off.
2. Analyze the Build / Buy / Partner sourcing decision at the individual-initiative level and its aggregation to portfolio shape.
3. Design a governance regime that balances transparency, data-sharing, and automation pace against known adversarial events.
4. Recommend a first-round portfolio that preserves optionality for Rounds 2 and 3.
5. Defend the recommendation against a sceptical CFO, a cautious General Counsel, and a Business Unit CEO with a favorite initiative.

On the evening of **March 17, 2026**, **Daniela Fuentes** stood at the whiteboard of the Nexus Corporation strategy room on the thirtieth floor of the Torre Reforma in Mexico City and stared at five rectangles she had drawn that morning and erased twice. Each rectangle was a Lighthouse. Inside each she had written a dollar figure and a three-letter sourcing tag — BLD, BUY, PRT. At the bottom of the board she had written three words: *Transparency. Data-sharing. Pace.* The C-suite Steering Committee would meet at 7:30 a.m. on March 18 to ratify the portfolio. She had two versions on her laptop. Neither was ready.

---

## The Protagonist

Daniela Fuentes, 47, had been Chief Strategy Officer at Nexus since late 2023, after eight years as head of strategy at a Mexican bank and two years as a partner in a boutique strategy consultancy. Her brief, when Nexus had recruited her, had been explicit: "Build the AI function, but through strategy — not through IT, not through a business unit." In practice that meant she would chair the AI Steering Committee and be accountable for the program's three-year outcomes, without direct line authority over any of the five business units from which the Lighthouse sponsors were drawn. She reported to the CEO. Her compensation included a three-year AI-program performance vesting tranche, defined against revenue impact, adoption, and the absence of a Class-I reputational event.

---

## The Decision

Fuentes had to present, by 7:30 a.m. on March 18, a single Round 1 portfolio covering April 2026 through March 2027. The portfolio had three dimensions:

1. **Lighthouse allocations:** dollar amounts across the five Lighthouses (Fraud Detection — Financial Services; Demand Forecast — Retail; Predictive Maintenance — Industrial; Route Optimization — Logistics; GenAI Customer Service — Retail and FS), summing to a Round 1 ceiling of USD 60M (40% of the three-year commitment).
2. **Sourcing mode** per Lighthouse: Build internally (ROI multiplier 0.7, adoption multiplier 0.85, strategic-ownership +), Buy (1.0 / 1.0, +0.5 ownership), or Partner (ROI 0.9, adoption 1.15, strategic-ownership −).
3. **Governance toggles:** Transparency (low, medium, high), Data-sharing (restricted, selective, open), and Automation pace (paced, steady, aggressive).

The committee would vote the package up or down. A no-vote defaulted to a "study year" — no spend, no commitments — which the CEO had informally indicated he would not tolerate.

---

## Company Background

Nexus Corporation, founded in 1972 in Monterrey, had grown through forty years of acquisition and organic expansion into a diversified conglomerate with revenues of USD 4.2 billion in 2025 across five business units: Financial Services (USD 1.1B), Retail (USD 1.4B), Industrial Manufacturing (USD 800M), Logistics (USD 550M), and Customer Service / Business Process Outsourcing (USD 350M). The group was publicly listed on the Bolsa Mexicana de Valores, with a founder family holding 27% through a holding company, two major domestic pension funds holding a combined 22%, and a U.S. strategic holding 8%.

Nexus's historical operating model had been radical decentralization. Each business unit ran its own P&L, its own tech stack, and — until 2024 — its own data infrastructure. The Chief Data Officer role, created in 2024, had spent eighteen months building a central data lake against intense BU resistance. As of March 2026 the lake housed structured data for Financial Services and Retail, partial data for Logistics, and no data for Industrial or Customer Service. The AI program would be Nexus's first enterprise-wide technology effort in a decade.

## Industry and Market Context

Large Latin American conglomerates in 2026 faced three simultaneous AI-related pressures. First, capital commitments had escalated: regional peer conglomerates had announced multi-year AI programs ranging from USD 50M to USD 400M, with public disclosure creating analyst pressure. Second, the regulatory environment had tightened: Mexico's National Institute for Transparency, Access to Information and Personal Data Protection (INAI) had opened three investigations into algorithmic decision-making in consumer credit during 2025, and Brazil's Autoridade Nacional de Proteção de Dados had published AI-specific guidance in January 2026. Third, talent had become the binding constraint: median compensation for senior AI engineers in Mexico City had risen 64% in three years, and regional retention had weakened as remote roles with U.S. employers had multiplied.

Research from the region's corporate advisory firms had produced two recurring findings. First, the median Lighthouse program in Latin America returned roughly 70% of its promised business case, with adoption the single largest driver of the shortfall. Second, portfolio concentration above 50% on any single Lighthouse was associated with a higher incidence of public failure — either technical (a model error that reached customers) or reputational (a governance event that reached the press).

## The Situation

### How we got here

The Nexus board had approved the USD 150M AI commitment in October 2025 after a two-year debate. The commitment came with four conditions: a centralized steering committee (created in January 2026), a published governance framework (in draft), an external advisory panel (seated February 2026), and an annual public disclosure of AI-related incidents. Fuentes had been given the chair over an internal rival — the Chief Technology Officer, Marco Velázquez — who had proposed a BU-led model.

### What Fuentes knew

Advisors — a mix of strategy, law, and technology — had compiled a twelve-event risk table covering the three-year horizon. Each event had a triggering pillar (governance, concentration, adoption, partner dependency), a threshold, and a penalty. Three events were, in the advisors' view, above a 30% probability of occurring in Round 1: an INAI preliminary communication on consumer-credit AI; an activist-investor letter targeting governance; and a partner-dependency event triggered by any Lighthouse above 40% Partner sourcing.

### What Fuentes did not know

How aggressively INAI would act if the preliminary communication landed; whether Marco Velázquez, in the event of a portfolio setback, would be positioned to reopen the BU-led model; whether the U.S. strategic would support an aggressive pace or prefer a conservative ramp; and whether the CEO — supportive in private, non-committal in public — would defend the committee if an event fired in Round 1.

### What others were saying

**Marco Velázquez**, CTO, argued for concentration on GenAI Customer Service with Partner sourcing. "Highest visible ROI. Vendor maturity. Adoption multiplier 1.15. Ninety million on it, and we tell a story."

**Laura Serrano**, General Counsel, argued for maximum governance and low Partner dependency. "Transparency high. Automation paced. No Lighthouse above 25% Partner. The INAI letter will arrive. We must be able to point to what we already did."

**Ricardo Fuentes-Méndez** (no relation to Daniela), CFO, argued for a balanced portfolio with strict gating. "Five Lighthouses at USD 12M each, Round 2 funded only against measured Round 1 adoption. Governance at medium. No heroes."

---

## Options Under Consideration

Fuentes had narrowed the Round 1 portfolio to three coherent packages.

- **Option A — Concentrated Bet (the Velázquez plan):** GenAI Customer Service USD 28M (Partner), Fraud Detection USD 14M (Buy), Demand Forecast USD 10M (Buy), Predictive Maintenance USD 4M (Build), Route Optimization USD 4M (Build). Governance: Transparency medium, Data-sharing selective, Automation aggressive. Highest expected Round 1 revenue impact; highest exposure to the Partner-dependency event and the Public AI Failure event.

- **Option B — Risk-Adjusted Portfolio (the Fuentes-Méndez plan):** Five Lighthouses at USD 12M each (USD 60M total). Sourcing: Fraud Detection Buy, Demand Forecast Build, Predictive Maintenance Partner, Route Optimization Build, GenAI Customer Service Buy. Governance: Transparency high, Data-sharing selective, Automation steady. Expected Round 1 revenue impact roughly 80% of Option A; event exposure roughly 45%.

- **Option C — Governance-First (the Serrano plan):** Fraud Detection USD 20M (Buy), Demand Forecast USD 15M (Build), Predictive Maintenance USD 10M (Build), Route Optimization USD 10M (Build), GenAI Customer Service USD 5M (pilot only, Buy). Governance: Transparency high, Data-sharing restricted, Automation paced. Expected Round 1 revenue impact roughly 55% of Option A; event exposure low and bounded.

(See **Exhibit 3** for the modeled Round 1 revenue impact and event-exposure heat map by option, and **Exhibit 4** for the twelve-event risk table.)

---

Fuentes erased the whiteboard one last time, wrote the five Lighthouse names across the top, and drew three columns underneath — A, B, C — with space for a fourth. The CEO had told her, in a one-on-one the previous Thursday, that he would support whichever plan she brought "as long as I can read it in five minutes and defend it in thirty." The Steering Committee would meet at 7:30 a.m. She had the night to choose.
