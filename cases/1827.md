# Meridian Cardiac Systems: Scaling the Clan Without Killing It

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1827 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1827 · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Wednesday, 4 February 2026, Renata Sokol read two numbers her operations analyst had left on her desk and understood why the chief executive wanted to see her that afternoon. Clinical Operations, the 140-person unit she had led for six weeks, had cleared roughly 3,300 monitoring cases in January. The hospital-network contract Meridian Cardiac Systems had just signed would require about 10,000 a month by year's end, and the number of devices the unit supported would have to grow from 40,000 to 120,000 within eighteen months.

She had four weeks to bring the executive committee a plan for closing that gap without dismantling the collaborative, craft-driven way of working that had given the unit a customer-satisfaction score above 98% for eleven straight years — a run no other unit at Meridian could match. The same contract carried a 24-hour clinical-response commitment the unit had never operated under, backed by federally regulated complaint handling that turned a mishandled case into a compliance matter rather than a service one. Sokol had a completed staff survey, a four-week deadline, and a unit that had never scaled a process in its history.

## The Protagonist

Renata Sokol, 44, had spent eleven years inside Clinical Operations before her promotion to General Manager in December 2025 — first as a field clinical engineer, then as the unit's senior scheduling lead. She had helped build the informal, apprenticeship-style habits behind the unit's satisfaction record: pairing new analysts with veterans, negotiating deadlines directly with hospital contacts, and treating complex cases as craft problems rather than queue items. She had never run a unit through a mandated change of this size, and now had to ask the people who had trained her to work in ways the unit had never required before.

## The Decision

Sokol had to decide, at four connected moments over the mandate's life, how much results-and-process discipline to add and how much of the unit's relationship-and-craft identity to protect — and to keep making that call as new pressure arrived at each stage (Exhibit 2). The two were not separable: a move that bought throughput risked the trust of the two people who held the unit's deepest hospital relationships, and a move that protected the culture risked the contract's number instead.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Cardiac Systems, LLC designed, manufactured and serviced implantable cardiac-monitoring devices from a plant and clinical-support center in Plymouth, Minnesota. The company employed 1,150 people, generated roughly US$310 million in annual revenue, and ran at a 19% EBITDA margin. Clinical Operations sat inside that company as a 140-person group of device-monitoring analysts, field clinical engineers and customer-support specialists who supported cardiologists and hospital technicians. The unit's reputation was why corporate had handed it the new contract. That same unit had never published a performance metric, never operated under an externally imposed deadline, and never turned relationship judgment into a repeatable process.

## The Situation

The mandate was easy to state and hard to execute (Exhibit 1). At current productivity, closing the volume gap by hiring alone meant adding roughly 120 people — an 86% headcount increase — against a transition budget the executive committee had capped at US$2.0 million. A ninety-person internal survey, just completed, showed a unit weighted toward relationships and improvisation and light on results-tracking and standardized process — the opposite of what the new 24-hour clinical-response commitment demanded, each missed response costing US$25,000, capped at US$1.5 million a year. Two senior field engineers, holding 30% of the unit's top hospital relationships, had not yet been asked to change anything. On the four measures the committee would track — culture fit, readiness to hit the required throughput, how much trust the engineers and hospital customers placed in the unit, and how many different leadership behaviors the team could actually show under pressure — the unit opened at 22, 18, 40 and 15 out of 100. Left alone, the unit would keep clearing about 3,300 cases a month while the requirement rose toward 10,000, and the 24-hour commitment would start breaching from the first month that volume climbed.

## What Sokol Controlled

The first task was to interpret what the ninety-person survey said about how the unit worked. She could read it as imbalanced for the new mandate — heavy on relationships and improvisation, light on results-tracking and standardized process, needing more of both; as evidence that the relationship-driven approach was itself the problem, one the unit needed to retire and replace with standardized process; as proof that eleven years above 98% meant the unit's approach was already right and should continue unchanged; or as confirmation the unit already operated the new way, and the only gap was that no one had said so. She also had to name which capability the unit most needed to add: results-orientation and standardized process together, more relationship-and-craft capacity on top of what it already had, standardized process alone, or results-orientation alone. A third setting, a dial from zero to a hundred, fixed how openly her team would name out loud that the unit was becoming partly a different kind of culture, from full candor to quiet avoidance.

The second task was to commit resources to that position before the first 25,000 devices went live in 120 days. A hundred leadership-attention points had to split between control-and-results and relationships-and-craft, summing to exactly one hundred. Three further moves came with it. For added capacity: a tiered process that routed cases by complexity, conversion of the unit into a scripted, centralized intake operation, or hiring the roughly 120 additional people current productivity would require. For visibility: a published running dashboard of response times, continued case-by-case negotiation of deadlines, or the same metrics tracked privately and shared with staff later. And to protect what had built the unit's reputation: a dedicated lane for complex, relationship-heavy cases, no protected lane at all, or faster throughput bought by trimming back the complaint-handling checks.

The third task ran at the same time: which leadership behaviors her team would practice under three live pressures. The team could mix results-driving and relationship-nurturing behaviors, draw on the relationship-and-mentoring style, draw on results-and-direction-setting, or draw on process-monitoring and coordination. When the chief executive demanded the throughput number immediately, Sokol could commit to a staged trajectory with trackable indicators, promise the original number on the original date, or ask for eighteen more months. When the two engineers threatened to leave, she could enroll them as leaders of the protected lane and mentors to newer staff, hold the line that the changes applied to them as well, or exempt them from the changes entirely. When a hospital escalated a near-miss safety report, she could keep the complaint-handling check non-negotiable while flexing everything else, downgrade the report's priority to keep the faster process moving, or freeze the whole scale-up until every process was re-certified.

The fourth task arrived at the plan review, when a mid-course signal showed the satisfaction score slipping from 98% to 91% and throughput still 20% short. She could make a small adjustment and hold the rest of the transition steady, reverse the changes and return to the prior way of working, or tighten control further. She also had to sequence the ninety-day plan: the tiered process and protected lane first, the dashboard next, and the full service commitment only at first go-live; a single all-hands declaration, made once at the outset, that the whole unit was now results-driven; or switching the entire unit to the new operating model in a single day, with every team moving at the same time. She had to name which indicators she would keep watching afterward — all four measures together, throughput alone, or the satisfaction score alone — and set a final dial, zero to a hundred, for how firmly she would hold the rebalanced course rather than swinging back toward either extreme.

## Closing

At 8:15 that morning, Sokol pinned the survey results to the wall behind her desk next to a single sheet listing the contract's numbers: 3,300 against 10,000, forty thousand against a hundred twenty thousand, four weeks against eighteen months. The chief executive's assistant confirmed the afternoon meeting. Sokol drew two columns on the sheet — one for what the unit would add, one for what it would protect — picked up a marker, and started writing.
