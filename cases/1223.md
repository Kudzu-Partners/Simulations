# The Migration Trade-off: Costaria's Parliament and the Boravian Flow

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1223 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1223 — *The Migration Trade-off: Labour Markets and Borders* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Sunday, April 12, 2026, two of Costaria's national newspapers ran their lead editorials on the same question. One was headlined *"Costaria Needs These Workers — Open the Door."* The other asked *"Whose Country Is It Becoming?"* Both quoted economists, and both economists were reputable. On Monday morning Inés Arregui-Holm, rapporteur of the Costarian Parliament's cross-party Migration Policy Working Session, laid the two pages side by side on the committee-room table and opened the session's shared dashboard on the screen above them.

The proposal before the session would admit 50,000 additional Boravian workers a year — construction labourers, farm workers, hospital nurses and software engineers — under a flat quota filled by lottery. Arregui-Holm had to put three papers to the plenary: a statement of how heavily the session counted each group the flow affected; a revised statement with an integration budget, once the fiscal and community evidence was in; and a full migration framework of the session's own design. The first paper was due at 18:00 on Friday, April 17. The plenary had agreed to judge the session on the full record once the third paper was filed.

## The Protagonist

Arregui-Holm was fifty-one. She had spent twelve years at Costaria's National Statistics Institute running the labour-force survey before winning a seat in 2022 for the coastal industrial city, one of the two places where most Boravian arrivals settled. Her constituency office heard from hospital managers who could not staff night shifts and from parents whose children's schools were full. Both main blocs had accepted her as rapporteur in February because neither could name a vote she had cast on migration. She had never had to cast one.

## The Decision

Each paper carried a set of welfare weights: how much the session counted the gains and losses of four groups — Costarian employers, Costarian native workers, Boravian migrants, and the Boravian families who stayed at home. The weights were multiples of five, had to total 100, and could be restated in every paper. From the second paper on, she also set Costaria's annual integration budget. In the third she chose the framework itself and the size of the annual flow (see **Exhibit 2**).

## The Company and its Setting

Costaria was a small open economy of 12 million people with output of about CU 22,000 per head, in the currency units (CU) both countries' statistics offices used for joint tables. Its population was ageing; the median Costarian was 41. Boravia, its neighbour, had 14 million people, a median age of 27 and output of about CU 6,500 per head. Some 200,000 Boravians already lived and worked in Costaria and sent home about CU 1.4 billion a year. A Boravian construction labourer earning CU 2.20 an hour at home earned about CU 8 an hour in Costaria (see **Exhibit 1**).

The positions had hardened before the session met. Bruno Saldaña, president of the Costarian Construction Federation, arrived with a list of 42,000 unfilled vacancies and a warning about the pension system. Rosa Quintana-Lenz, general secretary of the construction workers' union, answered that real wages on building sites had been flat for six years and that the market should tighten before more labour arrived. *"We are not against the Boravians,"* she told the session. *"We are for the people already on the site."* Aurel Vescan, Boravia's Deputy Minister of Health, wrote that his country lost 40% of its newly trained nurses within five years of graduation, and that rural clinics were closing for lack of staff.

## The Situation

The dashboard showed the proposal as tabled. Costaria's output would rise by about CU 530 million a year in the short run and about CU 1.3 billion a year in the long run. Average native wages would barely move. For low-skill Costarians who did the same jobs as the arrivals, published estimates ranged from close to zero to a loss of 3–4%. The migrants themselves would gain about CU 600 million a year from earning Costarian rather than Boravian wages, and would send about CU 130 million a year home on top of the existing remittances.

A second layer of evidence was scheduled for the second paper. The Finance Committee's figures showed the public accounts in deficit on the new arrivals for their first five years, and in surplus over thirty, with a present value of about CU 4.2 billion. Two receiving cities would take 60% of arrivals; their rents had risen 28% in five years, and schools in three of their neighbourhoods ran at 115% of capacity. Vescan's ministry projected 23 rural clinic closures in Boravia under the central scenario. Research on diaspora effects suggested that young Boravians who trained in the hope of emigrating would make up 30–60% of that loss over a decade. Clinic closures, like the city-level costs, would enter the dashboard only with that second paper.

If the session changed nothing, each paper would go up with the drafts as written: equal weights of 25, an integration line of CU 200 million a year from the second paper on, and in the third an annual flow of 25,000 workers, half the tabled figure. The framework line was the one cell the drafts left blank.

## What Arregui-Holm Controlled

In the first paper she set only the four weights. The session's own reading was that the facts were largely settled within ranges. The weights said whose share of them counted, and by how much.

In the second paper she restated the weights and set annual integration spending on language courses, school places and labour-market access for arrivals, anywhere from nothing to CU 600 million in steps of CU 50 million.

In the third she chose one of eight frameworks. Open borders admitted anyone who passed a criminal-record check and a health screening. A skills-based points system scored applicants on language, education, age and occupation. Employer-sponsored visas tied each worker to a named employer and lapsed when the job did. A refugee and humanitarian pathway admitted people fleeing persecution or disaster. Family reunification let migrants already in Costaria sponsor immediate relatives. Cap-and-quota admitted qualified applicants by lottery up to a fixed number. A rotating-worker scheme issued multi-year permits with no path to residence. A free-movement compact with Boravia let citizens of both countries work on either side. Alongside the framework she set the annual flow, from 5,000 to 100,000 workers in steps of 5,000, and restated the weights and the integration budget.

After each paper the dashboard reported five lines: Costaria's output gain, the wage effect on close-substitute low-skill natives in percentage points, the migrants' welfare gain, remittances from the new flow, and modelled Boravian clinic closures. It also scored each of the four groups from 0 to 100 and combined those scores, through the session's own weights, into a consensus score out of 100. A round history kept each paper's framework, flow, output gain, wage effect, migrant gain, clinic closures and consensus. The plenary would read the consensus score on the third paper, with a performance band, and would then ask the rapporteur to defend the framework before the group it served worst.

## Closing

At 17:05 on Friday, April 17, Arregui-Holm had four cells open in the first paper. Saldaña's office had called twice. Quintana-Lenz had sent a one-line message asking whom the session was counting. Vescan's letter lay on the table beside the two Sunday editorials, and all four weights still read 25.
