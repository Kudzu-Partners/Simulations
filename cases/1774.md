# Lumora Software: Disconnect to Deliver

On Tuesday, 5 May 2026, Elena Vidal opened three documents that had landed on her desk that hour. The first was the Q1 engagement survey: pride and commitment scored well, but work-family conflict sat in the bottom quartile of the company, and seventy-one percent of engineers reported answering messages after nine at night. The second was a resignation letter from a senior engineer, a parent of two, who wrote that there had never been a real boundary between her work and her home. The third was an email from the project manager at Lumora's largest client, sent at eleven the night before, asking whether the platform release due in three weeks was still on schedule.

Vidal had three weeks to answer that question, and four sessions to decide what connected the documents on her desk.

## The Protagonist

Elena Vidal, 39, had led Lumora's engineering team for three years, promoted from a senior backend role after the company decided the group needed a manager who had written the code. Her bonus tracked the release calendar: dates hit, dates missed, nothing else moved the number. Her performance review carried the team's engagement score and attrition rate. Both measures now pointed in different directions, and both belonged to her.

## The Decision

Across four sessions, Vidal had to decide what was driving the exhaustion the survey had surfaced; what rule the team would follow about after-hours messages, and what happened when that rule met a client keeping none of those hours; how to answer one engineer's request for a different schedule during the release's three weeks, and who would cover her work if granted; and what she would tell the VP of Engineering about how delivery and wellbeing related, including what she would measure afterward and how she herself would keep whatever rule she set.

## The Company and its Setting

Lumora Software S.L., founded in Madrid in 2014, sold a logistics-optimization platform to retailers and carriers across Spain, Portugal and Latin America. Revenue had reached €96 million, growing 24% a year, and the company employed 540 people, 210 in engineering and product. The team worked hybrid, two anchor days in the office, and its clients spanned enough time zones that after-hours messages had become ordinary.

## The Situation

The numbers on Vidal's desk were specific. Seventy-one percent of engineers answered messages after 21:00, and the work-family conflict score sat in the bottom quartile of the survey (Exhibit 1). Replacing the engineer who had just resigned would cost an estimated €65,000. The client whose project manager had written at eleven the night before represented €4.2 million in annual revenue, with a release due in three weeks; missing it risked a penalty and the renewal. If Vidal changed nothing — left the survey, the messaging pattern, and the client relationship as they were — next quarter's survey would likely look the same or worse, and the €65,000 already spent once would likely be spent again. Lumora's own dashboards already scored the team on four measures out of 100 (Exhibit 2).

## What Vidal Controlled

In the first session, Vidal had to name what was actually driving the exhaustion, because whatever she designed afterward depended on that answer. It might be eleven individuals with poor personal time management, a problem addressed through resilience training on disconnecting in the evening. It might be a structural norm the team had built without noticing, where fast replies read as commitment and client time zones normalized midnight messages. It might be plain overload, simply more delivery work sitting on the team than its current headcount could reasonably absorb. Or it might be a compliance gap, addressed by publishing more visibly the disconnection policy Spanish law already required. Alongside that call, she had to decide whether the real problem was the absence of any shared rule about when a message was allowed to arrive, or the after-hours messages themselves, showing up on people's phones every single night regardless of urgency — and how seriously to weigh what continuing to do nothing was already costing.

In the second session, with the client already emailing at odd hours, Vidal had to set the team's rule. A defined escalation protocol could specify exactly what counted as a genuine emergency, routing everything else into the next working day. A hard cutoff could close all contact after 19:30 each evening, without exception, for every engineer on the team. A rotating handful of engineers could take responsibility for the client's messages on a rota, freeing everyone else to disconnect completely. The team could keep things as they were, trusting each engineer to manage their own limits without any shared rule. Or an async-first rhythm could make deferred replies the default, so a reply by the next morning counted as normal. Whichever rule she chose, she also had to define the escalation path for a genuine emergency — her own judgment applied case by case, decided in the moment, recorded nowhere; a single named channel with written criteria, logged each time and reviewed afterward by her; or no exception at all, with the rule holding absolutely regardless of circumstance. And she had to put a figure on what the team would stop doing outright: the share of after-hours messages she was committing to eliminate, not merely slow down.

In the third session, one request was already sitting on her desk. An engineer had asked to work a compressed schedule for exactly the three weeks of the release, to cover a family-care need that could not wait. Vidal could deny it, on the ground that the release was too critical to lose anyone from it now; defer it, asking her to raise the same request again once the release had shipped; grant it alongside a coverage plan built so the extra work did not land on the people already carrying the most; or grant it and, without a specific plan, hand her tasks to the two teammates who happened to be available. Whatever she decided about the individual case, she also had to set the wider flexibility policy the team would work under afterward: scheduling judged by the output delivered rather than the hours logged; compressed four-day weeks offered to everyone on the same published terms; requests handled privately, one at a time, as they arrived; or a fixed core-hours window with the remaining time left flexible. And she had to decide how visible whatever coverage resulted would be — left for the team to absorb without any particular arrangement at all; kept quiet, because naming it would only invite resentment; or named openly, bounded in time, and explicitly reciprocated once the release was over.

In the fourth session, release week, Vidal had to present her plan to the VP of Engineering. She could describe delivery and wellbeing as sequential, pushing hard for the three weeks that remained and addressing wellbeing once the release was out the door. Or she could describe them as complementary, protecting the team's boundaries and shipping the release as the same outcome, not two competing ones. She could also describe them as a trade-off, in which output had to come first now and the cost to the team was one to absorb later. She had to name what she would actually measure at a ninety-day checkpoint — a combined view of conflict, delivery, retention and wellbeing together; delivery alone, whether the date had been hit; or wellbeing alone, whether the survey score had recovered. And she had to say, in a specific and checkable way, how consistently she herself intended to keep the same boundary she was asking eleven other people to keep, and whether she would set the policy once and consider the matter closed, or commit to a scheduled review in ninety days to re-measure and adjust.

## Closing

At 8:15 that morning, Vidal printed the survey summary, the resignation letter, and the client's email, and laid them side by side. The release was three weeks out; the engineer who wanted a different schedule for those weeks was waiting for an answer. Vidal had four sessions to decide what she believed was true, and to write it where the VP of Engineering — and everyone reporting to her — could read it.
