# Costaria National Productivity Council: The Number for Cabinet

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1320 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1320 — *Panel Data Workshop — Fixed Effects vs Random Effects in Action* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

On Tuesday, April 28, 2026, Renata Valdeolmos-Ibarne, lead econometrician in the Research Department of the Costaria National Productivity Council, read a two-paragraph memo for the third time. It had come from the office of the Minister of Economy, marked *Pre-Decisional — Not for Distribution*, and the Council's Chief Economist had forwarded it to her with one line: "Yours. Three weeks." The Treasury wanted to know whether the government's management-training program for small firms had raised their productivity. The minister had been told, informally, that the effect was "around 10–15 percent." She wanted a defensible number for the cabinet meeting of Tuesday, May 19.

The data already existed. The question was what Valdeolmos-Ibarne would put in front of the minister, and how she would say it. Before the memo left the building she would take the analysis to the Chief Economist four times, and at each pass she would have to settle the same four choices again.

## The Protagonist

Valdeolmos-Ibarne was thirty-four and had led the Council's econometric work for two years. She had joined in 2021 after a doctorate in applied economics and three years at a regional development lender, and she knew the Costarian SME Panel better than anyone in the building except the analyst who cleaned it. She had written evaluation memos before. She had never written one whose answer a minister had already been given.

## The Decision

She had to decide, pass by pass, how to estimate the program's effect, how to report its uncertainty, which single extra check to run, and how to frame the result for a reader who wanted one number. The Chief Economist, Tomás Arrieche-Lindo, would read every pass and return it with comments (see **Exhibit 2**). Whatever she sent after the fourth pass would be the Council's answer.

## The Company and its Setting

The Costaria National Productivity Council was a technocratic agency created in 2017, reporting jointly to the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Economy. Its mandate was to produce evidence on what raised Costarian productivity and to recommend interventions. It employed about 80 people, 22 of them in the Research Department.

In 2020 the government launched the Programa Nacional de Capacitación Gerencial (PNCG), which paid for consulting hours to help small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) improve their management practices. Firms applied to join; none was assigned. By 2026 the program cost about CRP 180 million (Costarian pesos) a year, and it was the budget line the Treasury had asked about (see **Exhibit 1**).

The Research Department maintained the Costarian SME Panel (CSP), a survey of the same 1,500 firms every year from 2018 to 2024: 10,500 firm-year observations, stratified by six provinces, by sector, and by three size bands between 10 and 249 employees. About 420 of those firms had taken part in the PNCG at some point between 2020 and 2023; about 1,080 never had. The Council measured productivity as value added per hour worked, in logs.

## The Situation

Germán Olaizola-Paredes, two years at the Council and the analyst who cleaned the panel each year, had run the obvious regression before the memo reached her desk: productivity on a participation flag, with all 10,500 observations pooled together. It returned an effect of +12.0%, with a standard error of 0.018 and a p-value below 0.001. The figure sat in the middle of the minister's range.

He had also built three charts, and they were open on Valdeolmos-Ibarne's second screen. In 2018, two years before the program existed, the firms that would later join were already 8.3% more productive than the firms that never would. Between 2018 and 2019, still before any training, their productivity had grown 2.1%, against 0.4% for the others. And a model predicting participation from firm characteristics (size, capital intensity, region, export orientation) explained a substantial share of who joined, with a pseudo-R² of 0.34.

If nothing changed, the memo was already written. The pooled figure was precise, it agreed with what the minister had heard, and it could be in her office by Friday. Olaizola-Paredes had said as much. A smaller number with a wide interval might read in cabinet as the Council not knowing, and the program's next budget line would be settled on May 19 either way. Arrieche-Lindo had offered no view. He had asked only to see each pass as it was run.

He kept a running record of the work. Each pass went into a run history: the estimator, the type of standard error, the estimated effect, its standard error, the t-statistic, the p-value, and the credibility the pass had earned. Two tallies started at zero and accumulated across the passes: a credibility score for the specification work, and a memo-defensibility score for how the result was framed for the minister. A third figure, selection bias remaining, started at 100% and showed how much of the selection problem the latest specification still left unaddressed. Beside them sat the last estimated effect, in percent of productivity. At the end, the review would place the file in one of four grades, from naive analyst to a standard fit for the Chief Economist's own chair.

## What Valdeolmos-Ibarne Controlled

At every pass she settled four things.

The **estimator**. She could pool all firm-years as if each were an independent observation; use firm fixed effects, which compared each firm only with itself over time; use two-way fixed effects, which also absorbed what every firm lived through in a given year, such as the 2020 pandemic or the 2022 commodity-price spike; or use random effects, which drew on both the differences between firms and the changes within them.

The **standard errors**. Conventional ones, which treated the 10,500 observations as independent of each other; or cluster-robust ones at the firm level, which allowed each firm's seven years to move together.

One **additional diagnostic**, or none. A Hausman test comparing the fixed-effects and random-effects estimates; a parallel-trends event study, which traced participants and non-participants year by year around each firm's training year; or a heterogeneity breakdown, splitting the effect by sector (manufacturing or services) and by size band. Each pass had room for one.

The **memo framing**. A single point estimate with no caveat; a point estimate with a cluster-robust confidence interval; a best estimate under an explicitly stated assumption; or a range with an explicit caveat about the limits of the method.

The minister would read the framing. The Chief Economist would read everything.

## Closing

At 17:40 Valdeolmos-Ibarne opened a blank document and typed the heading the minister's office had asked for: *Estimated productivity effect of the PNCG*. Below it the cursor waited for a number. The 12.0% was in the run log, the three charts were on her second screen, and the first pass with Arrieche-Lindo was on Thursday morning.
