# Aubène 2028: The Fourth Programme

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1786 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1786 — *Execute the Change* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On 2 March 2026, in Nantes, Nathalie Vasseur received the board's signed mandate for a programme it had named Aubène 2028: sixty million euros and eighteen months to digitize claims handling, consolidate the back office, and turn regional branches from paperwork counters into advice centers. The strategy itself was not in question — the board, the executive committee and an outside consultancy had already signed off on it. What was in question, and what Vasseur had been hired to answer, was whether Aubène could actually change.

She had eighteen months to land it, with the board reviewing progress at the nine-month mark and able to pause the remaining budget. She also had history working against her: three transformation programmes at Aubène had been funded, launched and quietly buried in the decade before hers, each time without an open revolt — just polite agreement, missed deadlines, and a slow drift back to how things had always been done. A survey commissioned before her arrival found that 78 percent of employees supported Aubène 2028. Her first task was to decide how much that number was worth.

## The Protagonist

Nathalie Vasseur, 51, joined Aubène in January 2026, recruited from outside after its third transformation attempt collapsed eight months into a planned two-year programme. She had spent the previous eleven years running operating-model change inside two other mid-sized European insurers, neither with a record like Aubène's. She took the job knowing the mandate carried more scrutiny than cover: the executive committee had approved Aubène 2028 unanimously, but two of its members had also approved the three programmes that failed, and neither had explained why in public. Her contract ran to the end of the transformation; a fourth quiet failure would not be followed by a fifth attempt for her to lead.

## The Decision

Vasseur's mandate unfolded as a sequence of judgment calls rather than one choice. Before designing anything, she had to read how ready Aubène actually was to change, and identify what really held it back beneath the survey number. She then had to work out what was driving the covert opposition she suspected was forming, and where it was concentrated. With a fixed amount of goodwill to spend, she had to decide where to intervene and how to handle the most powerful person whose cooperation execution required. She had to sequence eighteen months of change against the organization's capacity to absorb it, and decide how much of the sixty million euros to commit by the halfway mark. And if the programme wobbled at the nine-month checkpoint, as the three before it effectively had, she would need to diagnose why in front of the people who controlled what remained of the budget.

## The Company and its Setting

Aubène Assurances SA was a personal-lines insurer founded in Nantes in 1968, with 3,200 employees and €1.4 billion in gross written premiums (Exhibit 1). Its combined ratio stood at 97 percent — profitable, with little room for inefficiency. The company had built its reputation on long tenure, close regional relationships, and branch managers who had run their territories, in some cases, for twenty years or more. That informal authority was itself what Aubène 2028 proposed to redistribute: the new operating model shifted decisions and data away from regional discretion toward centralized, digital-first processes.

Aubène's competitive problem was not a crisis so much as a slow leak. Direct insurers with lower-cost digital operations were taking roughly two points of market share a year — not enough to bankrupt Aubène in any single year, but enough, left unaddressed, to hollow out its position within a decade. The board had funded Aubène 2028 to close that gap, not to fund a fourth attempt at the same result.

## The Situation

Vasseur inherited four scores the board would use to track her (Exhibit 1). Changeability, zero to a hundred, reflected the organization's actual capacity to change rather than its stated willingness, and stood at 34. Hidden Resistance, also zero to a hundred, measured how much covert opposition remained — lower was better — and stood at 72. Political Capital, her own room to act without provoking a rupture, stood at 60. Board Confidence, which needed to stay above 50 for the funding to survive the nine-month checkpoint, stood at 65.

Underneath the aggregate numbers sat the fact she found hardest to set aside: the 78 percent figure predated her arrival, and the regional branch managers — the group with the most informal power to lose under the new model — had returned positive answers to that survey. None had said anything to her directly that contradicted it. Each of the three previous programmes had followed the same arc: visible early progress, a quiet stall before the finish line, and a board that eventually stopped asking why.

## What Vasseur Controlled

Her first decision (Exhibit 2) was how to characterize Aubène's real readiness beneath the 78 percent figure — substantially lower than stated, genuinely mixed with pockets of resistance, or high enough to take at face value — and which of three dimensions, formal structures like reporting lines, everyday habits and incentives, or unconscious anxieties beneath the surface, was the binding constraint. She also had to put a number, zero to sixty points, on the stated-actual gap.

Her second decision concerned the source and location of covert resistance: whether it came from a fear of losing informal status and control built over two decades, from a private worry about leading in a digital operating model, or from a belief the resistance was mostly noise, not a real signal. She had to choose which group to map first as most worth attention — the regional branch managers, the works-council representatives who spoke up in meetings, or the newly hired digital specialists — and set a percentage, zero to a hundred, for how far she would treat resistance as information, not obstruction to override.

Third, with time and goodwill limited, she had to decide what to do about the powerful division head whose branch network execution required: give her visible ownership of a workstream that protected something she valued, remove her from the role — judging a clean break fastest — or route execution around her through new digital channels, betting she wouldn't be missed. She also had to select exactly three of six possible moves to fund that round: building a coalition of front-line staff, redesigning incentives so new behaviors actually paid off, engineering early, visible wins that built credibility, addressing the barons' concern by defining their new role, running a company-wide town-hall and communications campaign, or publicly naming the people she judged were undermining execution.

Fourth, she had to set the pace of execution — concentrating on one workstream before widening, launching claims digitization, back-office consolidation and branch redesign together, or moving cautiously to avoid provoking the barons — and decide what went first: a small, contained win in a willing region, the branch redesign itself, or claims digitization everywhere at once. She also had to commit up to sixty million euros to spend by the ninth month.

Finally, should the nine-month checkpoint arrive with claims digitization stalled and a board member questioning the spend, she would have to diagnose the stall — under-treated resistance, a flawed strategy needing redesign, or a capability problem calling for new managers. Her next move toward the barons would be to offer them a credible, status-preserving role in the new model, run another round of communications and town halls, or impose compliance through mandates, deadlines and consequences for delay. She would then decide what to tell the board chair in three minutes — that execution had under-treated resistance and she knew how to rebuild it, that the strategy needed rethinking since she'd already spotted its flaw, or that matters were essentially fine since she'd seen this settle before — and state, as a percentage, how firmly she held it.

## Closing

On the morning of 2 March, Vasseur closed the funding letter, opened the 78 percent survey, and set beside it the exit interviews from the three programmes before hers. The board's clock was running. She had eighteen months, sixty million euros, and a survey number she had to decide, right now, how much to believe.
