# NairaReach Financial Technologies: Banking the Unbanked

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1867 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1867 — *Banking the Unbanked* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 9 March 2026, Chidinma Okoye stood in front of NairaReach Financial Technologies' board and watched them reject the plan she had spent six weeks building. The plan asked for the next tranche of NGN 4.5 billion to acquire more of the smartphone-owning, salaried Lagos and Abuja users who already made up most of the app's base. The board's lead director tabled a number first: roughly 38 million Nigerian adults, about 36% of the population, had no meaningful access to financial services. Then he tabled a second number that stopped the meeting: 92% of NairaReach's own active users already held a bank account before they ever opened the app. The board withheld the tranche and gave Okoye twelve months to reach a segment of Nigerians who were genuinely excluded, and to prove that serving them did not lose money.

Okoye now had to decide which underserved population NairaReach would build for, how the company would physically reach them, what it would charge them, and how she would defend the results to the same board — all while a go-to-market decision had to be locked by 30 June 2026, so three quarters of real traction data existed before the next tranche review, with the fourteen-month runway still running down.

## The Protagonist

Okoye, 38, had co-founded NairaReach in 2023 after eight years in digital-channels roles at two Lagos banks, most recently heading mobile product for one lender's retail arm. She had built the company's first product — peer-to-peer transfers for salaried urban users — into a business processing about NGN 38 billion a year in transaction value, with 1,400,000 registered users and 410,000 monthly active accounts, operating under a Central Bank of Nigeria Mobile Money Operator licence held through a banking partner. NairaReach was not yet profitable, burning about NGN 180 million a quarter against a runway of fourteen months. Okoye's standing with her own board now rested on a mandate she had not designed.

## The Decision

Okoye first had to pick one primary segment to build for, from five that had been profiled for the board (Exhibit 2): university students, already holding bank accounts, based on or near urban campuses; Lagos and Abuja gig-economy delivery riders, young, smartphone owners, mostly already banked; market women in secondary-city markets, cash-based, keeping their savings at home; Lagos informal traders moving high cash volumes through neighborhood mobile-money agents already; or rural smallholder farmers in the north, seasonal cash income, reachable only by basic phone. Alongside the segment, she had to name the one job that population most needed done — meeting recurring obligations such as school fees and supplier bills, saving cash somewhere safer than keeping it at home, borrowing working capital before building any savings at all, or sending money to family across distance quickly and reliably — and the single barrier her design would have to dissolve: living too far from a branch with only a basic phone, missing the identification documents a formal account would require, any fixed fee being unaffordable against an irregular income, or the trust needed to hand cash over to a stranger.

Reaching that segment meant a channel choice — a polished smartphone app on its own, matching rival products; a phone-menu system usable on any handset, paired with agents; or a smartphone app paired with agents to cover everyone else — and an agent-model choice: a specialist super-agent partner recruiting and managing a network on the company's behalf; the kiosk owners, POS merchants and mobile-money agents already operating in these communities; or NairaReach recruiting, training and managing its own network of agents directly. Whichever she picked, she had to set a per-transaction commission between NGN 15 and NGN 60 to keep the network active, and fit it all inside a NGN 1.2 billion launch budget split across daily agent cash float, agent recruitment and density, the channel build itself, and customer education.

Pricing came next, and the agent had to be paid out of whatever single fee Okoye set: a headline price anywhere from zero to NGN 200 per transaction that, if it sat below the commission she had promised the network, would leave the network earning nothing. The options included a flat naira charge applied every time money moved through the account; a fixed monthly subscription billed regardless of how often the account was used; a percentage of each transaction's value, capped so larger sums were not overcharged; or free transfers with revenue instead coming from float, airtime and bill-payment margins. She also had to default new customers into one of the central bank's three identity-verification tiers — full bank-verification-number and utility-bill documentation, unlocking the highest transaction limits; only a phone number at signup, capped at the lowest transaction limits; or partial identification documents, unlocking transaction limits somewhere in between — and state how many transactions a month, between one and thirty, she expected an active customer to make.

## The Company and its Setting

NairaReach was headquartered in Yaba, on the Lagos mainland. Three years old, it had built its reputation and its revenue — NGN 2.9 billion in the most recent year — on serving the urban, banked, smartphone-owning customers a Lagos fintech found easiest to reach — the strength the board had just called the problem. The company's fully loaded cost to acquire and serve a user ran to about NGN 2,100 a year, a cost base built with no feature-phone-only, cash-based customer in mind.

## The Situation

Entering the mandate, the board tracked four numbers it would use to judge whether the tranche was released (Exhibit 1). Financial Inclusion Reach — the share of new users previously unbanked — stood at 8%, making the 92%-already-banked complaint concrete. Net contribution per active user, revenue minus everything it cost to serve someone, opened at roughly minus NGN 1,200 a year, short of the NGN 2,100 cost-to-serve it needed to clear. An internal trust index for the agent network and the communities it served opened at 25 out of 100. Active users reached under the new plan started at zero.

Two facts bounded every option. Most of the genuinely excluded population earned NGN 30,000 to 80,000 a month, had never paid a bank fee, and would reject anything resembling a fixed charge. Within that population, only about 40% owned a smartphone — the rest reachable only by basic-phone menu or in person — and roughly 55% lived more than five kilometres from a bank branch, though most lived within about a kilometre of an informal shop or kiosk. If NairaReach changed nothing, the arithmetic pointed to the same outcome as before: more of the customers it already had, an inclusion number the board would reject on sight, and a runway that expired before the review arrived.

## What Okoye Controlled

Okoye would be measured on those four numbers as they moved over the twelve months of the mandate. As the plan ran, she would have to respond to whatever disrupted it — a sudden tightening of the regulator's account balance caps, a reversion to the urban-growth strategy the board had shelved, an agent-liquidity shortage or a fraud scare inside the network, or a rival undercutting NairaReach's fees to win the same customers — without abandoning the mandate. She would have to choose how to prove the plan's trajectory to the board: showing the underlying cohort economics improving quarter over quarter, arguing the social case for the reach on its own terms, or leading with a large, fast-growing headline user count instead. And if the board itself offered to loosen the mandate for faster growth, she would have to decide whether to hold the line and defend the mandate outright, blend it by keeping a supporting revenue base alongside the mandate, or take the trade and pursue faster growth now — stating a conviction level on the three-quarter plan she was putting in front of them.

## Closing

By the time the board meeting ended, Okoye had twelve months, a NGN 1.2 billion launch budget she had not yet touched, and a runway that would not stretch twice. She went back to her desk and pulled up the five segment profiles the board had reviewed with her.
