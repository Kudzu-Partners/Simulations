## Abstract

The Director General for Consumer Goods Markets at the case-universe Madrid-headquartered national consumer-protection authority had been asked, in the December 2025 ministerial mandate, to recommend a regulatory intervention package on the domestic used-car market in response to a 2024–2025 trade-volume collapse and consumer-complaint escalation. The baseline cycle had recorded an indexed trade-volume collapse from 100 in Q1 2024 to 32 by Q1 2025 across 16,400 transactions, a 71% welfare drop, a documented exit pattern of high-quality sellers, and a 218% year-on-year rise in formal complaints. The Q1 2026 ministerial brief asked the Director General to model four candidate interventions — Spence-style warranty signaling, Rothschild-Stiglitz inspection screening, mandatory audited disclosure, and any combination — across ten quarterly intervention periods on a stylised 100-transaction-per-period base, and to recommend the package that recovered the most welfare at the lowest regulatory cost. The case opens at 7:58 a.m. on Wednesday, April 22, 2026, at the authority's Madrid head office as **Carlota Iribarne-Sokolova**, a 51-year-old Director General, prepares the ten-period LemonMarket commitment.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the Akerlof market-for-lemons baseline (information asymmetry between informed seller and uninformed buyer; uninformed buyer's reservation price collapses to expected average quality; high-quality sellers exit; average quality falls; reservation price falls again; equilibrium unravels) and articulate why a no-intervention market with material asymmetry produces a documented trade-volume collapse rather than a stable mediocre-quality equilibrium.
2. Analyse the Spence signaling mechanism (sellers acquire a costly signal whose cost is decreasing in seller type; high-quality sellers buy the signal because the cost is low for them; low-quality sellers do not because the cost is high; buyers pay more for signaled units; separating equilibrium is sustained without regulatory cost) and apply it to the warranty mechanism in the used-car context.
3. Diagnose the Rothschild-Stiglitz screening mechanism (the uninformed party offers a menu that induces self-selection by the informed party; in the used-car context, buyers paying for inspection acquire information that high-quality cars pass cheaply and low-quality cars fail visibly) and articulate why screening is initiated by the uninformed side, in contrast to signaling.
4. Respond to the mandatory-audited-disclosure mechanism (full-information equilibrium at the cost of an enforcement apparatus including auditors, penalties and whistleblower protection) and articulate the welfare-versus-regulatory-cost trade-off that makes mandatory disclosure expensive in practice even when it is effective in theory.
5. Design the mixed-mechanism intervention (warranty + targeted inspection subsidy) that recovers 84% of welfare at a regulatory cost index of 22 — articulating why a two-tool design typically dominates a single-tool design when one tool is incentive-based (warranty) and the other is selectively-subsidised information acquisition (inspection).

## The Protagonist

Iribarne-Sokolova, 51, had been the Director General for Consumer Goods Markets since 2022, had spent twenty-two years across the European Commission's Directorate-General for Competition and the Spanish authority, and had supervised approximately a dozen market-intervention engagements ranging from retail-energy price-transparency to second-hand consumer-electronics disclosure. In 2018–2021 she led the design of the Spanish second-hand consumer-electronics disclosure framework that reduced documented information asymmetry by ~28 percentage points, and was subsequently invited to brief the European Commission's Adverse Selection Working Group. In 2024 she co-authored with the Chief Economist *Two Tools, One Market*, which informed the Q3 2025 ministerial mandate. The Minister assigned her on three considerations: her discipline of benchmarking pure mechanisms before recommending mixed designs; her command of the Spence-Rothschild-Stiglitz literature; and her willingness to recommend a lighter-touch package that did not exhaust the authority's enforcement budget.

---

## The Decision

Iribarne-Sokolova had to commit, by the 5:00 p.m. ministerial pre-brief, the ten-period intervention envelope and the recommended mechanism mix.

1. **Periods 1–2 — No-intervention baseline.** Trade volume falls from 100 to 64 in Period 1 and to 48 in Period 2. Welfare falls correspondingly. Establishes the unraveling pattern as the counterfactual.
2. **Period 3 — Mechanism 1: Spence-style warranty signaling.** Sellers may acquire a third-party warranty whose cost is low for high-quality cars and high for low-quality cars. High-quality sellers buy the warranty; low-quality sellers do not. Buyers pay a premium for warranted units. Separating equilibrium is sustained at zero direct regulatory cost. Trade volume recovers to 71 in Period 3 and stabilises at 73 by Period 4.
3. **Period 4 — Mechanism 2: Rothschild-Stiglitz inspection screening.** Buyers pay for an inspection; the regulator may subsidise the inspection cost to encourage adoption. High-quality cars pass cheaply; low-quality cars fail visibly. Adoption depends on the subsidy level. At a 50% subsidy, trade volume recovers to 68 by Period 5.
4. **Period 5 — Mechanism 3: Mandatory audited disclosure.** Every seller must post an audited quality rating. Trade volume recovers to 86 by Period 6 — the highest of the three pure mechanisms — but the regulatory cost index is approximately 78 because the apparatus requires accredited auditors, penalties and whistleblower protection.
5. **Periods 7–10 — Mixed-mechanism design.** Warranty (low regulatory cost) + targeted inspection subsidy (modest regulatory cost). Trade volume climbs to 78 by Period 10. Welfare recovers to 84% of baseline. Regulatory cost index is approximately 22 — a fraction of mandatory disclosure.

On Iribarne-Sokolova's working model, the ten-period envelope landed at a recommended mixed-mechanism design (Periods 7–10) with a Welfare reading of 84% of baseline and a Regulatory Cost Index of 22, against a counterfactual mandatory-disclosure design with a Welfare reading of 86% and a Regulatory Cost Index of 78. The mixed-mechanism design beat mandatory disclosure on cost-adjusted welfare by approximately three-and-a-half times, and beat warranty-only and inspection-only on welfare recovery while remaining inside the authority's annual sectoral-enforcement budget.

## Sectoral Background

The Spanish used-car market had, in the case universe, totalled approximately 1.84 million transactions in 2025 with a gross transaction value of approximately EUR 22 billion, had been characterised by a fragmented seller base (approximately 64% private sellers, 36% dealer sellers), and had carried a documented information asymmetry that had been amplified by the 2024 enforcement-policy gap when the previous certification regime had been allowed to lapse without a replacement. The 2024–2025 cycle had produced three named milestones: the December 2024 lapse of the prior certification regime; the Q3 2025 trade-volume collapse to 32 of indexed baseline; and the December 2025 ministerial mandate. The Q2 2026 context had compounded three pressures: a parliamentary inquiry on the consumer-complaint escalation, an industry-association lobbying campaign for a light-touch warranty-only mechanism, and a consumer-association lobbying campaign for a mandatory-audited-disclosure mechanism. The Director General had to propose a package both lobbies could accept and the ministry could fund.

## Industry and Market Context

The 1970–2025 information-economics literature produced three findings the package had to respect. First, no-intervention adverse-selection markets unravelled to 24–41% of baseline trade volume in 78% of documented cases, with the median collapse in the 32–38% band that the Spanish used-car market had reproduced. Second, Spence-style signaling sustained separating equilibria in 71% of cases when the cost differential between high-quality and low-quality signal acquisition was at least 2.5x. Third, Rothschild-Stiglitz screening sustained separating equilibria in 64% of cases when uninformed-side adoption exceeded ~38% — with subsidies in the 30–60% range typically sufficient to clear the threshold.

The post-1990 mixed-mechanism literature added two findings. Two-tool designs combining one signaling instrument and one screening instrument outperformed single-tool designs on cost-adjusted welfare in 68% of documented cases. And mandatory-audited-disclosure regimes recovered welfare 6–11 percentage points higher than the best mixed-mechanism designs but absorbed a regulatory cost index 3.2–4.4x higher — a trade-off the Spanish authority's annual sectoral-enforcement budget did not permit.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The December 2025 ministerial mandate asked for a recommendation by Q2 2026. The baseline-collapse model, the three pure-mechanism comparison and the industry/consumer-association lobbying-position summaries had all landed by April 8. The Minister had asked Iribarne-Sokolova to bring the ten-period envelope to the April 22 pre-brief.

### What Iribarne-Sokolova knew

No-intervention adverse-selection markets unravel to roughly a third of baseline trade volume; warranty-style signaling sustains separating equilibria at zero direct regulatory cost; inspection-style screening sustains separating equilibria with a subsidy in the 30–60% range; mandatory-audited-disclosure recovers the most welfare but at the highest regulatory cost; and a two-tool design combining warranty and targeted inspection subsidy typically dominates single-tool designs on cost-adjusted welfare. The authority's annual sectoral-enforcement budget would not absorb a mandatory-audited-disclosure design.

### What she did not know

Whether the parliamentary inquiry would absorb the mixed-mechanism recommendation cleanly or push for mandatory disclosure under consumer-association pressure; whether the industry association would absorb the targeted inspection subsidy as a reasonable price for avoiding the mandatory-disclosure regime; whether the warranty-cost differential between high-quality and low-quality cars in the Spanish market matched the 2.5x assumption from the literature; and whether the Q4 2026 evaluation cycle would surface a fourth mechanism (e.g., a centralised reputation registry) that warranted reopening the design.

### What others were saying

**The Chief Economist**: *"No-intervention is thirty-two percent of baseline. Mandatory disclosure is eighty-six. The mixed mechanism is eighty-four — at a quarter of the cost."*

**The Senior Policy Adviser**: *"Warranty is signaling. Inspection is screening. The two-tool design works because the tools attack the asymmetry from opposite sides."*

**The Industry-Affairs Lead**: *"The dealers will accept the warranty. The targeted inspection subsidy is a price they prefer to a mandatory rating."*

**The Consumer-Affairs Lead**: *"The associations want the audited rating. The cost-adjusted argument has to land or they will lobby the ministry directly."*

## Options Under Consideration

Iribarne-Sokolova had narrowed the space to three envelopes.

- **Option A — Mixed-mechanism design (warranty + targeted inspection subsidy) / Welfare 84% / Regulatory Cost Index 22:** the recommended envelope. Cost-adjusted welfare beats every alternative; both lobbies tolerable. Recommended.
- **Option B — Mandatory audited disclosure / Welfare 86% / Regulatory Cost Index 78:** the consumer-association preference. Rejected — exceeds the authority's annual sectoral-enforcement budget; the two-percentage-point welfare uplift does not justify the four-times-higher regulatory cost.
- **Option C — Warranty-only / Welfare 71% / Regulatory Cost Index 4:** the industry-association preference. Rejected — leaves thirteen welfare points on the table relative to the mixed-mechanism design at modest incremental regulatory cost.

Her private view was that Option A was the right envelope and that the period most correlated with the eventual ministerial decision was Period 7 — the launch of the targeted inspection subsidy on top of the warranty regime — because the inspection subsidy was the policy lever that the consumer associations could read as substantive consumer protection while the industry associations could read as a price for avoiding mandatory disclosure.
