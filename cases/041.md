## Abstract

Meridia Corporation, a USD 4.2B Mexican diversified conglomerate listed on the Bolsa Mexicana de Valores, scheduled four ordinary board meetings between March and December 2026. Patricia Solórzano, an independent director and chair of the audit committee, faced a Meeting 1 agenda built around an annual CEO performance review. Diego Castellanos, CEO since 2021, had hit every financial target. Employee engagement was 67 against a Board target of 75; the ESG roadmap was a year behind; and the succession plan, formally requested in 2024, was missing. On the Friday before Meeting 1, the audit committee received an anonymous whistleblower complaint via the company's third-party hotline alleging revenue-recognition manipulation in the Commercial Real Estate division. The case opens at 7:00 a.m. on Sunday, March 8, 2026, as Solórzano prepared the audit committee briefing for the Tuesday board meeting. The Meeting 1 decisions on the CEO rating and the whistleblower response would compound across three further meetings: a USD 1.8B acquisition (Construtora Horizonte), an activist campaign by Cascade Capital (9% holder), and — by Meeting 4 — a CNBV formal investigation, an unplanned CEO departure, and a 72-hour ultimatum on the Chair's undisclosed related-party transactions.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Evaluate the trade-offs in a CEO performance review when financial KPIs are met but governance, talent, and succession indicators are below target.
2. Diagnose the appropriate audit-committee response to an anonymous whistleblower complaint received in the same week as the CEO review.
3. Analyze the compound effect of Meeting 1 governance decisions on subsequent decisions about acquisitions, activist engagement, and regulatory disclosure.
4. Apply a structured framework — independent investigation, scope, counsel selection, board reporting — to a revenue-recognition allegation in a publicly listed company.
5. Recommend a Meeting 1 vote on CEO rating, LTIP modifier, and whistleblower handling, and articulate the framing the audit chair will use to anchor the three meetings that follow.

On the morning of **Sunday, March 8, 2026**, **Patricia Solórzano** sat at her dining-room table in Polanco with the Meridia Corporation board pack open at section seven — *Annual CEO Performance Review* — and a separate envelope, sealed, that the company secretary had delivered by motorcycle at 5:42 p.m. Friday. The envelope contained a four-page memo from the Speak Up vendor: a complaint filed at 3:18 p.m. Friday by an unnamed employee alleging that the Commercial Real Estate division's Q4 2025 revenue had been overstated by approximately MXN 1.6B through advance recognition of multi-year lease incentives. The board would convene at 9:00 a.m. Tuesday. Solórzano had two days to decide how the complaint would, or would not, enter the agenda.

---

## The Protagonist

Patricia Solórzano, 56, had served on the Meridia board since June 2022 as an independent director and had chaired the audit committee since the 2024 annual general meeting. A chemical engineer by training (UNAM, 1991) with an MBA from IPADE, she had spent twenty-two years at a multinational chemicals company — finishing as Chief Financial Officer for the Latin American region — before transitioning to a portfolio of three independent directorships in 2020. She held independent seats at Meridia, at a regulated banking subsidiary, and at a mid-cap industrial. The Meridia seat was, by her own private assessment, the seat with the highest reputational concentration in her portfolio: Meridia was a household name, the founding family retained 32% of the shares and the chairmanship, and the three independent directors — herself among them — sat on the legal exposure that the family did not.

---

## The Decision

Solórzano had to decide, by 9:00 a.m. Tuesday, March 10, three things:

1. **Her vote on the CEO rating** for fiscal 2025 — *Exceeds, Meets, Partially Meets, Below* — and the corresponding LTIP modifier.
2. **The audit committee's response** to the whistleblower complaint — independent investigation by external counsel, internal investigation by the General Counsel's office, deferral to Q2 with monitoring, or referral to management for triage.
3. **The framing** she would offer Roberto Ipiranga, the Chairman, for how the four-meeting calendar should be sequenced if the whistleblower complaint were validated — including whether the Meeting 2 acquisition discussion would proceed as planned.

Three further meetings, already calendared and partially briefed, would follow. Meeting 2 (June 11) was scheduled to consider the USD 1.8B acquisition of Construtora Horizonte, which would push leverage from 2.7x to 4.2x and trigger a probable Moody's downgrade from BBB to BB. Meeting 3 (September 17) would face Cascade Capital, a 9% activist shareholder that had, on March 4, filed a Schedule 13D and indicated an intent to seek two board seats, an Independent Chair, a strategic review, and a USD 500M capital return. Meeting 4 (December 9) was, on the calendar, an ordinary year-end review.

---

## Company Background

Meridia Corporation had been founded in 1968 as a regional construction firm by Augusto Ipiranga and had, across two generations, expanded into four reporting segments: Industrial Manufacturing (38% of 2025 revenue), Commercial Real Estate (24%), Consumer Brands (22%), and Financial Services (16%). Fiscal 2025 revenue was MXN 84.2B (approximately USD 4.2B at the December 31 spot rate), with reported EBITDA of 15% and net income of MXN 7.1B. The founding Ipiranga family held 32% of the equity and the chairmanship; the remaining 68% was held by institutional investors (43%), retail (16%), and Cascade Capital's recently disclosed 9%.

The board comprised eleven directors: the Chair (Roberto Ipiranga, the founder's grandson), three further family or family-aligned directors, four independent directors (including Solórzano), the CEO, and two directors representing the largest pension-fund holders. The audit committee comprised three independent directors — Solórzano as chair, plus two — and was supported by the internal audit function (which reported administratively to the CFO and functionally to the audit committee) and the external auditor (KPMG since 2019).

Diego Castellanos, the CEO, had been recruited from a peer Mexican conglomerate in 2021 with a five-year mandate to "professionalize the operating model and prepare the next ten years of growth." His 2025 financial results were unambiguously strong: revenue +9.4% year over year, EBITDA margin +180 basis points, return on capital employed of 14.2%. The non-financial scorecard was less unambiguous (see Exhibit 1).

## Industry and Market Context

Mexican conglomerate governance had, in 2024–2025, entered a more activist phase. The CNBV (Comisión Nacional Bancaria y de Valores) had issued in October 2024 a guidance circular tightening expectations on whistleblower-handling timelines and audit-committee documentation, with first enforcement actions in two peer cases in late 2025. International proxy advisers — ISS and Glass Lewis — had begun, in their 2026 voting recommendations, to apply governance scoring to BMV-listed conglomerates that had historically escaped that scrutiny. Activist investors, both domestic and U.S.-based, had filed five Schedule 13Ds against BMV-listed conglomerates in the prior eighteen months; in three of the five, the campaign had ended with at least one board seat for the activist within nine months of the filing.

Empirically, the academic literature on whistleblower handling at listed companies suggested that complaints validated through external-counsel-led investigations produced 60% smaller eventual restatement penalties than complaints handled internally and later re-investigated; and that boards that received an activist 13D within ninety days of an unresolved whistleblower complaint faced a 2.4x higher probability of regulatory action within twelve months.

## The Situation

### How we got here

The CEO performance review had been scheduled in October 2025 as a routine Meeting 1 agenda item. The whistleblower complaint had landed Friday afternoon, March 6. The Speak Up vendor's protocol routed the memo to the audit-committee chair within ninety minutes; Solórzano had received it at 5:42 p.m. The CFO and General Counsel had been informed by the vendor in parallel. The Chairman had not.

### What Solórzano knew

The complaint specified four allegations: (a) two specific lease contracts in the Commercial Real Estate division, with counterparty names, signed in November 2025, in which incentives that should have been amortized over the lease life had been recognized in Q4; (b) involvement of the divisional CFO and the divisional Head of Lease Origination; (c) approximate magnitude MXN 1.6B (roughly 4.2% of segment revenue, 1.9% of group revenue); (d) the complainant identified themselves as a member of the divisional finance team and stated they had attempted to escalate internally on January 14, 2026, without response. The complaint was specific, internally consistent, and named verifiable contracts.

Castellanos's 2025 financial scorecard had been delivered and signed off by the divisional CFOs on January 31, 2026 — seventeen days after the alleged internal escalation.

### What she did not know

Whether the alleged misstatement, if confirmed, was confined to two contracts or reflected a divisional pattern; whether the divisional CFO had acted alone, with the divisional president, or with knowledge at corporate level; whether Castellanos was aware on January 31 when he signed the consolidated results; whether the external auditor had identified the lease-incentive accounting in their interim work; and whether Cascade Capital, whose Schedule 13D had named "concerns about segment-level reporting transparency," was acting on independent information or on a tip from the same complainant.

### What others were saying

**Roberto Ipiranga**, Chairman, had, in their Friday evening phone call, suggested keeping the whistleblower memo out of Meeting 1 and adding it as a Meeting 2 item once "facts were established": *"The Tuesday meeting is about Diego. Let's not contaminate that conversation."*

**Inés Cárdenas**, General Counsel, recommended an **immediate independent investigation by external counsel**: *"This is an audit-committee matter, not a management matter. The company secretary cannot be the one who decides what the audit committee sees. Engage external counsel by Monday morning."*

**Tomás Riva**, the second independent director on the audit committee, recommended a **scoped internal investigation** under the General Counsel: *"External counsel signals to the market we already believe it. Internal first, escalate if confirmed."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Solórzano had narrowed the audit-committee response to three options.

- **Option A — Immediate Independent Investigation by External Counsel:** Engage a U.S. firm and a Mexican firm jointly by Monday 9:00 a.m. Brief the full board at the Tuesday meeting under privilege. CEO rating delivered as planned; the rating is independent of the investigation. Public disclosure deferred pending materiality assessment. Audit-committee documentation strengthened.

- **Option B — Scoped Internal Investigation under General Counsel:** Inés Cárdenas leads the work, with KPMG observing. Audit committee meets weekly to monitor. CEO rating delivered. Decision on external counsel deferred to a 30-day milestone. Lower upfront signal; higher risk that the investigation is later perceived as conflicted if material issues are confirmed.

- **Option C — Defer to Q2 with Enhanced Monitoring:** Note the complaint in the audit committee minutes; instruct internal audit to expand the scope of its 2026 plan to include the Commercial Real Estate revenue-recognition population; reconvene in 60 days. Lowest disruption to the Meeting 1 agenda; highest exposure if the complaint is later found to have been received and not acted upon.

The CEO rating itself was a separate but linked decision. The financial results supported *Exceeds*. The non-financial scorecard supported *Partially Meets*. The whistleblower complaint, regardless of investigation choice, made *Exceeds* indefensible at any reasonable governance review. The question was whether *Meets* or *Partially Meets* — with a corresponding LTIP modifier of 100% or 80% — best signaled the board's posture without prejudging an investigation that had not yet started.

---

Solórzano closed the board pack at 8:14 p.m. Sunday, sent a one-line email to the company secretary requesting that the Tuesday agenda include a privileged executive session of the audit committee at the start of the meeting, and went to bed. Roberto Ipiranga had not yet replied. The Tuesday meeting was thirty-six hours away. Cascade Capital's first letter to the board was, according to her best information, drafted and timed for Wednesday.
