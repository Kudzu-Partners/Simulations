# Cascadia Precision Components: The First Layoff in Thirty-Five Years

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1923 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1923 — *The Just Leader* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, March 24, 2026, Priya Sandhu read the letter twice, walked the length of the machine shop, and came back to her office without saying anything to anyone. It was from the rail-car assembler that bought 31% of everything Cascadia Precision Components Ltd. made — CAD 24 million of the Burnaby plant's CAD 78 million in annual revenue — and it was moving the work to a captive supplier of its own.

By six that evening the board had authorised what the finance director's model required: CAD 4.2 million of annual labour cost removed, 38 positions out of 240, communicated within 14 days. The number was fixed and not Sandhu's to argue. What she had until April 7 to settle was everything else — the criteria, the explanation, and the conduct of it.

## The Protagonist

Priya Sandhu, 46, had run the Burnaby plant since 2019. She had grown up four kilometres from the gate, and sixty people on the roster had been hired on her signature. Her pay moved with plant operating profit and an engagement score. Her standing with the bargaining unit rested on a record she had inherited rather than made: in thirty-five years Cascadia had never laid anyone off.

## The Decision

Sandhu had to decide how a fixed reduction would be made and delivered, and what happened to the 202 who would still be at their machines in May. The board expected the announcement inside 14 days and a report 90 days later.

## The Company and its Setting

Cascadia Precision Components Ltd. machined tight-tolerance aluminium parts for aerospace and rail customers from a single plant in Burnaby, British Columbia, founded in 1991 by two tool-and-die machinists. Revenue in 2025 was CAD 78 million at a 9% operating margin, or CAD 7.0 million. Roughly 55% of the 240 employees were covered by a single bargaining unit, and average shop-floor tenure was 11 years, most people having arrived through a relative on the payroll. Cascadia had come through three downturns on attrition, shortened weeks and owner pay cuts; the record appeared in its recruiting material and in the surviving founder's letter each May.

## The Situation

The finance director's arithmetic was not in dispute: without the rail-car volume Cascadia could not hold a positive cash position, and CAD 4.2 million of annual labour cost, or 38 of 240 positions, closed the gap (see **Exhibit 1**).

Everything else was in dispute. The draft criteria from head office weighted seniority, published no rationale and offered no route of appeal. Ted Mackey, the bargaining unit's business agent, had told Sandhu that a list built that way would be grieved as arbitrary and aimed at the longest-serving, highest-paid people on the floor. An unresolved grievance reached arbitration in 21 days, at a cost counsel put at CAD 120,000 to 250,000.

The plant carried instruments for most of what was now at stake (see **Exhibit 2**). A quarterly pulse survey put trust in management at 78 out of 100; below roughly 55, industry data and Cascadia's own history agreed, retained high performers began to leave. A fairness index kept by the HR business partner, then at 50, read four things at once: who was selected and why, how the criteria were set, how people were treated when they were told, and how honestly the reason was explained. Output per retained employee was indexed at 100; comparable plants had lost 8% to 15% of it for two to three quarters where the process was judged unfair, and under 5% where it was not. The fourth was Sandhu's own: a composure rating at 60, scored by her executive coach, of how she held up under pressure.

A regional manufacturing forum with 4,000 members already carried the rumour. Six CNC programmers — CAD 95,000 fully loaded each, 16 weeks to replace — read it as information about their own position.

If Sandhu executed the draft as it stood — the seniority-weighted list, the wording finance had prepared, conversations on the Friday, nothing after — the CAD 4.2 million came out on schedule and the exposure was everything else: a grievance running to arbitration, output among the 202 in the unfair range rather than the fair one, and six programmers drawing their own conclusions.

## What Sandhu Controlled

The first decision was a diagnosis. She had to settle what the test actually was: removing CAD 4.2 million and 38 positions cleanly, getting the announcement out inside the 14 days, keeping the criteria narrowly defensible against the grievance, or the design of the process itself. She had to name which feature of the draft would create the most unfairness: that correcting it cost money the target did not include, that the unit would object whatever she did, that seniority weighting was standard practice and no defect at all, or that a list with no rationale and no appeal read as picking off the longest-serving. And she had to divide her attention, tracked as a percentage, between the arithmetic and the process.

The second was the criteria and the communication. Three drafts of the selection method sat on her desk: keep the seniority weighting, simple and already understood by the unit; rank by loaded cost, reaching CAD 4.2 million through the fewest people and protecting the timeline; or build a skills-and-criticality matrix with published weights, an appeal mechanism and redeployment offers, costing time and money the mandate did not include. She had to decide how much of the commercial reason to state: call it a restructuring for the future and avoid the word layoff, confirm the job losses while withholding the customer loss and the cash position, or state all three. She had to decide whom the plan was built for — the 38 leaving, the board and the press, or leavers and the 202 together — and to set the budget for appeal, redeployment, retraining and exit support at CAD 0, CAD 0.3 million or CAD 0.6 million, charged against the CAD 4.2 million.

The third was the conversations and the grievance. Emile Gauthier, a machinist with 14 years on the floor, was on the Friday list. If he raised his voice she could answer in kind and close the discussion, signal that the number might still move, or take thirty seconds to steady herself before answering. In the conversation itself she could keep it short and transactional and be out of the room quickly; present it as a performance-adjacent decision and keep the company out of it; or name the loss, thank him for the 14 years, give the real reason and let him be heard, at the price of the longer, angrier conversation. On the grievance she could reduce the 38 to 22 and end it, leaving CAD 1.8 million of the target unremoved; go to arbitration and let the panel decide; or mediate, giving the unit a genuine say on the criteria and the appeal route while holding the 38.

The fourth was the 90 days after. She could tell the 202 nothing specific and let them get back to work; assure them that everyone remaining was safe and leave it there; or set out the reason, the criteria, why they were not on the list and what would change, putting those criteria on the record to be tested. For the six programmers she could do nothing and trust the pay to hold them; offer a retention bonus and buy the loyalty with cash; or commit visibly to them and show them how the leavers had been handled, which bound her to both. She could close the file and invite no more questions; hold a town hall and issue a memo about values, showing concern without committing to anything; or fund the redeployment and retraining she had promised and publish two trust indicators she would then be measured against. And she had to record, on the nought-to-hundred scale she used with the board, how firmly she held that the explicit route beat the vague one.

## Closing

Sandhu wrote April 7 on the whiteboard, and under it 38, and under that 202. Mackey wanted a meeting on Thursday. The founder's letter was due in May, and someone would have to tell him what to put in it.
