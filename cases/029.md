## Abstract

NEXUS Servicios, a pre-launch two-sided marketplace for professional home and personal services (cleaning, repair, tutoring, wellness, light maintenance) across three Latin American capital cities, entered Period 1 on March 1, 2026, with USD 2.4M of seed capital, zero providers, zero clients, and zero liquidity. A twelve-period runway was organized into four phases: Launch & Seeding (P1–P3), Liquidity & Retention (P4–P6), Monetization (P7–P9), and Scale & Defense (P10–P12). Five sliders — Marketing Budget (USD 0–200K/period), Supply/Demand Split (0–100), Take Rate (5–25%), Quality Investment (USD 0–100K/period), and Geographic Expansion (USD 0–150K/period) — would be set each period. The Round-1 decision — Phase-1 settings — would determine whether NEXUS crossed the 20% liquidity threshold by end-P3, the minimum for Phase-2 graduation. The case places the NEXUS CEO, Camila Restrepo, on the eve of the Period-1 launch.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Diagnose the cold-start problem in a two-sided marketplace and evaluate the strategies that empirically break it.
2. Analyze the interaction between Supply/Demand split and Take Rate during the seeding phase.
3. Evaluate the trade-off between early liquidity (aggressive marketing and low take rate) and unit-economics defensibility (higher take rate, quality investment).
4. Design a Phase-1 allocation that respects the 20% liquidity threshold without spending down the runway.
5. Articulate the criteria under which a pre-launch marketplace should delay monetization beyond Phase 1.

On the evening of **Sunday, March 1, 2026**, **Camila Restrepo** sat at the kitchen table of her apartment in Bogotá and looked, for the seventh time, at the five sliders her Chief Growth Officer had drawn on a whiteboard photo and sent to her at 11:14 p.m. The sliders — Marketing, Supply/Demand Split, Take Rate, Quality, Expansion — all sat at zero. Below the whiteboard, in block letters in the photo, the phrase her co-founder had written: *"Whatever we put in these by Monday morning, twelve periods from now we will either have a marketplace or a story."* The first product release was Tuesday at 7:00 a.m.

---

## The Protagonist

Camila Restrepo, 35, was co-founder and CEO of NEXUS Servicios. A systems engineer from Universidad de los Andes, with a master's in Technology Management from a U.S. school, she had spent six years at a regional super-app as a product leader (growing a food-delivery vertical from 180 to 4,500 merchants), three years at a growth-stage vertical marketplace in the on-demand-cleaning category, and eighteen months as an entrepreneur-in-residence at a regional VC firm before founding NEXUS with two partners in September 2025. She had raised USD 2.4M of seed capital in November 2025 from a three-fund syndicate on a post-money valuation of USD 9.0M, against a twelve-period plan that would terminate in a Series-A conversation based on liquidity, GMV, and net-revenue KPIs.

---

## The Decision

Restrepo had to set, by 7:00 a.m. on Tuesday March 3, Period-1 slider values on the five marketplace dials that the simulation would treat as her Round 1 input:

1. **Marketing Budget (USD 0–200K)** — demand or supply-side acquisition spend.
2. **Supply/Demand Split (0–100)** — 0 means 100% of marketing spend to client (demand) acquisition; 100 means 100% to provider (supply) acquisition.
3. **Take Rate (5–25%)** — percentage of GMV captured as platform revenue.
4. **Quality Investment (USD 0–100K)** — background checks, training, verification, and moderation spend.
5. **Geographic Expansion (USD 0–150K)** — spend on activating the second and third cities beyond Bogotá (Medellín and Cali in the NEXUS plan).

Phase-1 (Periods 1–3) goals were precise: cross 200 active providers, cross 1,500 active clients, and reach a liquidity ratio (completed requests / initiated requests) of 20% or above. Failure to graduate from Phase 1 would force a plan revision and, plausibly, a bridge round on less favorable terms.

---

## Company Background

NEXUS Servicios had incorporated in September 2025 with a three-person founding team: Restrepo (CEO), Andrés Vargas (CPO, ex-product-lead at a LATAM classifieds platform), and Patricia Solís (CCO, former regional director at a gig-economy company). The product had been in closed beta through January and February with 40 hand-recruited providers and 180 invited clients across cleaning, repair, and tutoring verticals in Bogotá. Closed-beta metrics had been encouraging: 62% of requests had converted to completed jobs; NPS was 58; average job price was COP 180K (roughly USD 45).

The seed round had been structured around a 15-month runway. Fixed costs — team of 14, basic technology infrastructure, general and administrative — consumed USD 120K per period. That left USD 450K per period of discretionary spend across the five sliders, with a reserve of USD 180K per period to buffer against seasonal demand shocks and against the lumpy cost of regulatory-approval cycles in each new city. Summed across twelve periods, discretionary spend could total USD 5.4M; the seed capital was USD 2.4M; the delta implied that either growth would be slower than the plan contemplated or a Series-A would close before the end of Period 9.

## Industry and Market Context

Two-sided services marketplaces in Latin America had entered 2026 after a consolidation wave: three pan-regional incumbents (two horizontal, one vertical) held an estimated 65% of GMV in the urban on-demand services segment, but a fragmented 35% remained addressable by focused entrants. Three findings from the post-2020 marketplace literature were consequential for Restrepo's decision. First, cold-start marketplaces that seeded supply-first (Split > 60) for three periods and flipped to demand-first (Split < 40) in Period 4 achieved median liquidity at Period 6 roughly 2.4x higher than marketplaces that ran balanced splits throughout. Second, take rates above 15% in Phase 1 depressed provider retention by 20–30% regardless of quality investment; take rates held at 10% in Phase 1 and raised to 15–18% in Phase 3 preserved supply and delivered higher Period-9 net revenue. Third, geographic expansion during Phase 1 was empirically value-destructive for mid-sized marketplaces; expansion during Phase 2 or later produced the highest Period-12 defensibility score.

Multi-homing — providers listing simultaneously on two or three competing platforms — was the central threat. Providers stopped multi-homing when a single platform delivered them reliable demand (empirically, roughly three requests per week for on-demand services). Below that threshold, providers drifted to whichever platform happened to send them a request first.

## The Situation

### How we got here

NEXUS had pre-committed, in its seed pitch, to a twelve-period plan with a Phase-1 liquidity threshold of 20%. Two of the three seed investors had explicitly conditioned their follow-on in the Series-A on that Phase-1 number. The closed beta had demonstrated that the unit economics worked when liquidity worked. The open question was whether a cold public market, beginning Tuesday, would produce liquidity at the pace the plan required.

### What Restrepo knew

The internal growth-model had run four Phase-1 allocations. An aggressive supply-first allocation (Marketing USD 180K, Split 70, Take Rate 10%, Quality USD 40K, Expansion USD 0) projected 240 providers and 31% liquidity by end-P3. A balanced allocation (Marketing USD 150K, Split 50, Take Rate 12%, Quality USD 30K, Expansion USD 20K) projected 180 providers and 22% liquidity. A conservative allocation (Marketing USD 100K, Split 50, Take Rate 15%, Quality USD 20K, Expansion USD 0) projected 140 providers and 17% liquidity — below threshold. A demand-first allocation (Marketing USD 180K, Split 30, Take Rate 10%, Quality USD 20K, Expansion USD 0) projected 160 providers and 24% liquidity — above threshold but with visibly thin supply.

### What she did not know

Whether the two horizontal incumbents would respond to NEXUS's launch with targeted provider-retention bonuses (they had done so in two previous vertical entries). Whether the closed-beta NPS of 58 would hold in open market (the prior marketplace where Restrepo had worked had seen NPS drop from 64 to 41 upon open launch). Whether the Series-A lead — a regional growth-equity firm that had signalled preliminary interest — would condition on liquidity reaching 50% by Period 6 (an unconfirmed rumor from Friday).

### What others were saying

**Andrés Vargas**, Chief Product Officer and co-founder, argued for the **Aggressive Supply-First Allocation**: *"Load the cold side first. Two hundred forty providers by end of P3. Let the take rate be low. We raise it in Phase 3 when the flywheel is turning."*

**Patricia Solís**, Chief Commercial Officer and co-founder, argued for the **Demand-First Allocation**: *"If we have demand, providers come. If we have providers without demand, they leave. Flip the split. Spend on client acquisition. Let the data-room screenshot show repeat-client ratio, not provider count."*

**The Lead Seed Investor (Partner, Fund II)**, in a Friday call, argued for the **Balanced Allocation**: *"Do not optimize one metric. The Series-A will look at liquidity, provider count, GMV, NPS, and burn. A balanced Phase 1 gives the cleanest data-room. If you over-invest in supply, the GMV looks small. If you over-invest in demand, the quality score drops."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Restrepo had narrowed the space to three options.

- **Option A — Supply-First, Aggressive (Vargas plan):** Marketing USD 180K, Split 70, Take Rate 10%, Quality USD 40K, Expansion USD 0. Projected end-P3: 240 providers, 1,280 clients, 31% liquidity. P1–P3 spend USD 660K (before fixed costs); P6 runway = 7.5 periods remaining.

- **Option B — Demand-First, Thin Supply (Solís plan):** Marketing USD 180K, Split 30, Take Rate 10%, Quality USD 20K, Expansion USD 0. Projected end-P3: 160 providers, 1,820 clients, 24% liquidity. P1–P3 spend USD 600K; P6 runway = 7.7 periods remaining.

- **Option C — Balanced, Series-A-Legible (Lead investor plan):** Marketing USD 150K, Split 50, Take Rate 12%, Quality USD 30K, Expansion USD 0. Projected end-P3: 180 providers, 1,540 clients, 22% liquidity. P1–P3 spend USD 540K; P6 runway = 7.9 periods remaining.

Restrepo suspected Option A dominated in the twelve-period composite (liquidity is the multiplier) and lost in the Series-A optics (thin GMV). Option C gave the cleanest data-room and the lowest Phase-1 risk of missing threshold. Option B won demand but depended on a provider-retention assumption the incumbents' response could invalidate. At 11:42 p.m. she saved the slider values she would run with into the admin tool.
