# Alpina Domus: Nine Million Euros of Green Innovation

**Case Code:** SUS-2026-1971 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1971 — *Green Edge* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 4 March 2026, two notices reached Maja Zupan's desk within four hours of each other. The first was the European Commission's confirmation that Ecodesign and Energy Labelling requirements for wet appliances would tighten, effective in twenty-four months. The second was one line from the tender desk downstairs: the three-year, €19 million white-label contract Alpina Domus d.o.o. had held for six years had gone to a competitor whose machines carried an energy label one class higher.

Zupan, the company's Strategy Director, had one planning cycle to bring the supervisory board a plan for the €9.0 million of green-innovation spend it had authorised — the whole sum, fixed, with nothing behind it. The chair, Andrej Lah, had told her what the board would not accept: a plan that cleared the new thresholds and stopped there.

## The Protagonist

Maja Zupan, 44, had been Strategy Director since 2022. She had joined Alpina Domus in 2011 as a product manager for dishwashers and spent four years running the white-label account team. She had proposed a green product programme in 2023 and again in 2024, and had been turned down twice on payback grounds. The €9.0 million was the first budget of its kind the board had granted, and Lah had granted it on the condition that she report against the same four numbers throughout the cycle.

## The Decision

Zupan had to decide how to divide the €9.0 million between the two kinds of green investment open to her, what posture the company would take behind that division, and what would keep the result from being copied by two rivals who spent three times as much on research.

## The Company and its Setting

Alpina Domus d.o.o., founded in Velenje in 1991, made washing machines, dishwashers and built-in ovens in one plant with a small research centre attached. It recorded €218 million of revenue in 2025 at a 7.2% EBIT margin and employed 1,180 people. Roughly half the business was its own brand, sold across the European Union on a mid-market reputation for durability; the rest was white-label manufacturing for two larger groups, Nordhaus of Germany and Verel of France, which together took 46% of volume at thinner margins.

Two competitors led the segment. Brenner and Solvane both earned EBIT margins of 10–12% and both sat one full energy-label class above Alpina Domus across the comparable range. Alpina Domus had no recognised environmental position of any kind, and 71% of the electricity its plant consumed came off the grid rather than from generation on site.

## The Situation

The tightening confirmed on 4 March put a date on something the company had described for two years as a trend. Roughly 38% of Alpina Domus's stock-keeping units, carrying €83 million of 2025 revenue, would fail the new thresholds within twenty-four months and could not be sold in the European Union without redesign (see **Exhibit 1**). Replacing the lost €19 million of tender volume on the open market, the commercial team estimated, would take price concessions of 4–6%. Both Nordhaus and Verel had said that without a credible environmental strategy they would take a second source within eighteen months, putting a further €41 million of volume in play.

Two decisions outside the company remained open, and both would be settled before Zupan had to release the money. A national subsidy covering up to 30% of clean-manufacturing capital expenditure was expected but not enacted. An EU carbon-border adjustment on imported components was under consideration; if confirmed, it would move input costs by about €2.4 million a year.

Lah had set the reporting format. The board would follow four numbers through the cycle (see **Exhibit 2**): a differentiation index scoring the customer-visible green position from 0 to 100, on which Alpina Domus stood at 40; a unit-cost index set to 100 at the current cost base, where a lower reading meant cheaper production; the reduction in emissions from purchased electricity — what the plant's engineers called Scope 2 — standing at 0% against a 2025 baseline; and the company's share of the EU appliance market, then 12.0%. A fifth line asked what protected the position. It was blank.

On the current path the arithmetic was not in dispute. The €83 million of at-risk revenue would leave the market on schedule, the two partners would dual-source, and Brenner and Solvane would still be a class ahead on a research budget Alpina Domus could not match.

## What Zupan Controlled

The diagnosis came first. Four readings of the position were available: that the constraint was brand perception, the machines being competitive and only the green story missing; that it was product performance and manufacturing emissions together, a full label class behind and built on 71% grid power; that it was manufacturing emissions alone, the customer following once the plant's electricity was clean; or that there was no structural constraint, the tender turning on one buyer's specification rather than the range's standing. The portfolio review could be taken three ways: the range accepted as sound, the scheduled model refresh expected to reach the thresholds on its own timetable; the exposure sized at a top line of roughly a third; or an audit family by family, each marked compliant, at risk or obsolete with its energy-class evidence attached. The company could be placed as reactive, treating environmental spend as a cost and doing the minimum the regulation forced; as uncommitted, the posture to be set once the subsidy and carbon-border decisions landed; or as deliberately proactive in one chosen segment, leading where the lead could be held and complying elsewhere. And the white-label channel could carry anywhere from none of the diagnosis's weight to all of it.

Then the money. The €9.0 million could be split in any proportion between product work — redesigning at-risk models to climb energy classes and raise recycled content, visible to the customer, slow to pay back — and process work — on-site solar, heat recovery and waste reduction, lower unit cost and lower purchased-electricity emissions, largely invisible to the customer. Behind the split sat a stance: reactive compliance, spending only to clear the thresholds; selective proactivity, leading in one segment and complying in the rest; or full environmental leadership across the range at once. And a thesis for why customers would choose Alpina Domus: a differentiation premium, a cost advantage, the two reinforcing each other, or on-time compliance itself.

Once the subsidy and carbon-border decisions were known, the process tranche had to be accelerated in full, built as a first stage with the rest conditioned on the subsidy, or held until the policy settled. Zupan would state on the record how much of the case had rested on the subsidy: all of it, some of it, or none. The imported components could be left exposed, absorbed into margin if the levy landed, or re-sourced and localised. The redesigned own-brand line could be cut 4–6% to buy back volume, held, or priced at a modest premium.

The cycle ended in front of Lah and a buyer from Nordhaus. There Zupan would name what protected the position: that it was the first move — the green position taken before either rival had taken one; that it was speed — the new classes reached before Brenner and Solvane could answer; or that it was an integrated capability — product and process know-how, contracted recycled-material supply, and an operating low-carbon plant. She would answer the dual-sourcing notice by concentrating on the own brand, by offering the partners the new platform on contract, or by leaving white label altogether. She would lead her three minutes with compliance achieved, with the single best of the four numbers, or with all four together and an account of why the position would hold. And she would say, as a number, how firmly she held her own test of what counts as an edge: that it must be valuable, rare, and hard for a rival to copy.

## Closing

At 8:20 on the morning of 4 March, Zupan wrote two column headings on the whiteboard in her office — *product* and *process* — and, under them, a single figure: 9.0. The Commission's clock had twenty-four months on it. The partners' had eighteen. The board met at the end of the cycle.
