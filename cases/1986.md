# Daloy Mobility: The Ninety-Day Mandate

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1986 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1986 — *Decide with Data* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Tuesday, May 12, 2026, Marivic Ocampo froze the operations wall in Daloy Mobility's Ortigas office on a single figure. Of the trips booked across Metro Manila in the previous seven days, 14% had ended in a cancellation. Beside the screen hung the dispatch team's whiteboard, carrying the rules the company actually ran on: surge bands set by hand each morning, driver repositioning by district, a reminder message to riders whose driver was running late.

At four o'clock that afternoon the board would hand Ocampo, Daloy's first Chief Analytics Officer, one machine-learning initiative, PHP 90 million, and one quarter — ninety days, about thirteen weeks — in which to move a number on that wall. The resolution set the bar at two points of improvement on whichever operating metric she nominated, which the finance committee valued at roughly PHP 70 million a year. It also said what would follow a quarter with nothing running in production: analytics would be contracted out and the in-house team dissolved.

## The Protagonist

Marivic Ocampo, 41, had joined Daloy in February 2026 after six years running pricing analytics at a Southeast Asian e-commerce marketplace and three as a transport modeller in Manila. She had been hired on the strength of an argument she made in her final interview: that Daloy's constraint was not a shortage of data but a shortage of decisions that used any. She reported to the chief executive and was named in the resolution as the accountable executive. Her team was four: two modellers, a data engineer, an analyst on loan from finance.

## The Decision

Ocampo had to settle three things inside the same ninety days, and each narrowed the next: which single prediction Daloy would build; what the model would cost and how long it would take to reach production; and at what point a prediction stopped being a number and became an action on a live trip.

## The Company and its Setting

Daloy Mobility, founded in 2017 and headquartered in Metro Manila, ran a ride-hailing and parcel-delivery platform across the capital region and three secondary Philippine cities. It coordinated about 46,000 active driver-partners and completed roughly 1.9 million trips a week. Annual gross bookings were PHP 24 billion, on which Daloy took a commission of about 18%, giving net revenue of PHP 4.3 billion. It sat near break-even, and its investors wanted profitability rather than growth.

Metro Manila's congestion was both Daloy's market and its operating problem: travel times were unpredictable and driver supply rarely met rider demand in the same district at the same hour.

Against that, Daloy held one of the richest urban-mobility datasets in the country — billions of location pings, complete trip records, years of demand signal. Almost none of it entered a decision. The whiteboard was not a stopgap; it was the system.

In January a venture-funded rival had launched in Metro Manila, quoting arrival times riders found more reliable than Daloy's.

## The Situation

Ocampo's readiness review, run in the fortnight before the board met, put three candidate predictions in front of her. The board would fund one.

The first was demand forecasting: predicting trips by zone and hour so drivers could be positioned before demand arrived. It targeted driver idle time. Its features already sat in the warehouse, and the driver-positioning tool it fed already existed.

The second was arrival-time prediction: predicting travel time trip by trip, feeding the estimate quoted to the rider at booking and the reliability comparison the rival was winning. Its data was the cleanest of the three and needed no new labelling.

The third was cancellation prediction: predicting which booked trips would be abandoned, so a dispatcher could reassign a driver, message the rider, or hold a surge band. It targeted the 14% rate directly. Its labels were messier, and the link into the dispatch system that would carry the intervention did not yet exist.

Three definitions of success had been proposed, each by someone who would have to live with it. Her lead modeller wanted a prediction-quality score the team could be measured on. The chief financial officer wanted a movement in an operating number, in points and in pesos. The head of product analytics wanted weekly manager usage of the analytics dashboard, arguing that nothing else would survive contact with the organisation.

The problem statement sat on a whiteboard in three drafts: one beginning from the prediction and asking for it to be as accurate as possible; one beginning from the decision, naming the prediction, the action it enabled and the metric it should move; and one committing Daloy to becoming data-driven, without naming a target metric.

Before scoring the candidates she also had to fix how heavily she weighted expected business value against data readiness; the two rankings did not agree.

The economics of the intervention were not in dispute. Daloy's operations team had costed both ways the system could be wrong (see **Exhibit 1**). A cancellation the model failed to flag cost about PHP 180 once driver dead time and the discount needed to win the rider back were counted. An intervention on a trip that would have completed anyway cost about PHP 40 in wasted driver capacity and rider irritation.

If nothing changed, cancellations would hold near 14%, the rival would keep its advantage on quoted arrival times, and on day 90 the board would receive a presentation with no production system behind it.

## What Ocampo Controlled

Ocampo committed her decisions in four instalments across the quarter.

In the framing round she named the prediction problem, the way success would be stated, the wording of the problem statement, and the weight she gave business value against data readiness.

In the build round she chose among three routes to a working model (see **Exhibit 2**): an in-house gradient-boosted model on warehouse features, an in-house deep spatio-temporal network on raw location pings, or a vendor scoring interface bought off the shelf. Separately she chose whether features came from the warehouse or were engineered from raw pings; how the result would be measured against a baseline — a live holdout comparison, an offline test on historical data, or no formal baseline; and how hard to push engineering against the ninety-day clock.

In the deployment round she set what the intervention point should maximise — overall correctness, cost-weighted value, or nothing yet, deferring the decision rule to a later release — then set the number: the predicted probability, from 10% to 90%, above which Daloy would act. She fixed which predictions ran unaided and which a dispatcher reviewed: all automatic, all reviewed, or high-confidence cases automatic with borderline ones routed to a person. And she chose what would be watched after launch — nothing, an offline accuracy re-check each quarter, or a live indicator with a stated trigger for rolling the model back.

In the final round she chose how to open three minutes in front of the board — with the model's prediction quality, with the architecture her team had built, or with the movement in the operating metric and its peso value. She took one of three live shocks — traffic patterns shifting, the rival improving again, or a regulator querying Daloy's use of granular location data — chose her response, and stated how firmly she held that a model in production beat a better one that was not.

The board's dashboard carried four figures. *Decision lift* was the movement, in points, of the operating metric she had nominated; two points was the bar. *Net annual value* was that movement converted to pesos a year, less what the build had cost. *Model accuracy* was the share of predictions the model got right. *Deployment status* said whether anything was running. A fifth line tracked how much of the PHP 90 million she had committed, and a round-by-round record logged each move against those four figures.

## Closing

At 3:40 p.m. Ocampo printed the three candidate predictions onto one sheet and wrote the ninety-day calendar underneath, marking the week each build route reached production. The board sat at four. She had one slot, PHP 90 million, and thirteen weeks.
