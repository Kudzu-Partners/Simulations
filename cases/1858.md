# The Custom Bid

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1858 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1858 — *The Custom Bid* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 8 June 2026, Marisol Ferreira found the Helvetia-Pacific request for proposal in her inbox and read the number twice before she read anything else: a two-year leadership program for 240 senior leaders across the Americas, EMEA and Asia-Pacific, capped at four point two million dollars, all in. Proposals were due 3 July. The client would decide by 24 July. Meridian Global School of Management's Executive Education unit had made the shortlist of four, and Ferreira, its Deputy Dean and Executive-Education Lead, had three weeks to decide how her school would win a room that already had a cheaper number in it.

## The Protagonist

Ferreira, 51, had run Meridian's custom-program practice for six years and had been Deputy Dean since 2023, after fourteen years on the design and delivery side of the business. She had built her reputation scoping programs that clients renewed, not programs that merely won. Meridian's Executive Education unit had delivered 71 custom engagements in 2025 on thirty-eight million dollars of revenue at a twenty-eight percent blended margin, and every bid Ferreira signed her name to had to hold that discipline. She was, by her own account and everyone else's, the most expensive person in most rooms she walked into — a fact she had learned to treat as a question to answer rather than an apology to make.

## The Decision

Across the bid cycle Ferreira would have to decide, in sequence, how Meridian would frame its case to win against three named rivals, how it would scope and price the two-year program inside the client's cap, how it would staff three regions from a faculty roster that could not cover all of them at once, and how it would answer a selection committee that would, eventually, ask her directly why Meridian cost more.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Global School of Management, headquartered in Phoenix, Arizona, was the executive-education arm of a globally ranked American business school, built in 1994 around a specialization in global management and cross-border leadership. Its Executive Education unit employed 44 program and business-development staff and drew on a faculty roster of 130 — tenured professors, executive fellows and practitioners who had run businesses on four continents. The unit's standing bet was that clients would pay more for programs that could be shown, afterward, to have changed what leaders actually did. That bet meant Meridian rarely won on price. It meant every proposal had to answer, in writing, what the extra money bought.

## The Situation

Helvetia-Pacific Bank, a global financial-services group resetting its strategy across three regions, wanted its senior leaders developed inside a single coherent program rather than three regional ones — roughly seventeen thousand five hundred dollars per leader across two years, all in, against the four point two million dollar cap. The RFP made one requirement non-negotiable: bidders had to commit to measuring the program's impact on leadership behavior and business results, not just collecting feedback forms. Meridian was one of four shortlisted providers, alongside two peer business schools and a global consulting firm expected to bid around three point four million dollars — roughly twenty percent below Meridian's likely number — on a leaner, more standardized design (**Exhibit 2**). Ferreira's tracking model put Meridian's win probability at thirty percent that morning, climbing toward roughly thirty-five percent if she bid the full cap and changed nothing else about the proposal. The price math cut two ways: every tenth of a million she bid above the rival's expected number was likely to cost win probability, and every tenth she bid below it was likely to cost margin. Finance had one hard rule of its own: no bid below a twenty-five percent contribution margin, worth one million and fifty thousand dollars in real money on a four point two million dollar bid. Delivery had another constraint: only about eighteen faculty on the global roster were credentialed to teach at the standard this RFP demanded, and flying one of them internationally to teach cost roughly nine thousand five hundred dollars a trip. A renewal pipeline worth some twelve million dollars over five years, tied to Helvetia-Pacific's broader leadership academy, rode on how this first program went (**Exhibit 1**).

## What Ferreira Controlled

The first decision was the win theme: whether Meridian would frame its bid around the long relationship her school had with the bank, around measured impact and global reach, around matching the consulting firm's price outright, or around fielding the richest program of the four bidders. Attached to it was a second choice — where to plant Meridian's sharpest, most defensible edge over a cheaper rival: the depth of Meridian's global faculty, the school's brand and international ranking, the impact-measurement requirement the RFP had made mandatory, or no single edge at all — competing evenly everywhere. Ferreira also had to set a target win probability, a number between zero and one hundred that would anchor everything she priced afterward.

The second decision, once the strategy was set, was the program itself. She could scope it as the richest possible build regardless of cost, as a calibrated design sized to the cap, or as a lean, stripped-down core meant to undercut on price. Whatever she chose, she then had to set an actual bid number between three million and the four point two million dollar ceiling, and decide how to present that number to the committee: as the full cap with the brand left to speak for itself, as a defensive number signaling Meridian would flex hard to win, or as a calibrated premium tied to a specific value gap.

The third decision was staffing and measurement together. Three regions, two years, and a roster of roughly eighteen qualified faculty meant Ferreira had to choose a delivery model: a blended mix — flagship anchors, regional fellows and local adjuncts, co-teaching together; flagship faculty alone, flown to all three regions; or local adjuncts alone, with almost no international travel. She also had to design the impact methodology the RFP demanded outright — a visual analytics dashboard tracking engagement and satisfaction metrics; a baseline-to-end-line framework tied to the bank's strategy goals; or a satisfaction-survey approach built on participant feedback. And she had to commit a specific number of international teaching trips, at roughly nine thousand five hundred dollars each, somewhere between zero and forty — enough to anchor three regions with flagship presence, but not so many that airfare ate the delivery budget.

The fourth decision came in front of the committee itself, once the consulting firm's actual number was on the table. Ferreira could defend the price with evidence — outcomes, reach, faculty and accountability; assert that Meridian was the better choice and let reputation carry it; or cut the price on the spot to match the competition. She also had to state, in writing, a walk-away price below which Meridian should not win the work at all, somewhere between two point five million and the cap, and a conviction level — how firmly she would stand behind the claim that the premium bought measurably better outcomes.

How the committee would ultimately score the bid — on price discipline, program substance, and the fit between what Meridian offered and what the RFP actually asked for — was something Ferreira would only learn after each decision had already been made.

## Closing

Ferreira closed the RFP document, opened a blank page, and wrote the date at the top: 8 June. Three weeks to 3 July, three weeks after that to a decision, eighteen faculty names on a spreadsheet beside her, and a consulting firm somewhere already pricing its own number.
