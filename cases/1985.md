# Liwanag Consumer Group: Innovating for Impact

**Case Code:** STR-2026-1985 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1985 — *Innovate for Impact* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 5 May 2026, eleven floors above Cebu City, Beatriz Villaflor was handed two documents she had not asked for. The first was a procurement note: 22% of the coffee volume Liwanag Consumer Group had budgeted for the year ahead was unsecured, and a competitor was signing the company's own farmers onto exclusive contracts. The second was a letter from Liwanag's largest modern-trade customer — 15% of group revenue, ₱7.8 billion a year — requiring audited evidence of inclusive sourcing within twelve months, or it would de-list the company's core lines.

The board gave Villaflor ninety days. She was to return on 3 August with a strategy that put social investment inside the operating business rather than beside it, an allocation for ₱1.2 billion over three years, and a return that cleared the group's 18% hurdle rate. The chair attached a condition: if the plan read as philanthropy with a strategy deck attached, it would be defunded and Liwanag would buy the missing coffee at spot.

## The Protagonist

Beatriz Villaflor, 46, had been Liwanag's Chief Strategy Officer since 2023. She joined the group in 2014 to run the coffee and beverage category, after nine years in trade marketing in Manila, and rebuilt the route to market for the neighbourhood-store channel. She had never run the foundation nor sat on its giving committee. Her targets were group margin and category growth.

## The Decision

Villaflor had to decide what Liwanag's social investment was for, then put ₱1.2 billion behind the answer in four stages.

The first stage was a diagnosis of why three decades of foundation spending had produced goodwill and little else. Three readings were on the table: that the foundation was under-resourced and would convert to advantage at twice the budget; that it was structurally separate from the business, so goodwill never reached supply, cost or price; or that it was a communications failure a better-told scholarship story would fix. She had also to say where a social outcome and a commercial outcome could be produced by one and the same act — in relief and scholarships, where need was most visible; in the smallholder base that grew the crops; or in plant operations at Cebu, in energy and packaging efficiency. And she had to fix what "institutionalise" meant: a bigger, better-governed foundation with a clearer giving policy; the work written into strategy, structure, incentives and the operating model; or an annual report and a board committee.

The second stage was the redesign. Four versions had been costed. One expanded the foundation — more scholarships, a larger relief fund, a communications programme around both. One reopened procurement — renegotiated farmgate prices and shorter contracts, releasing cash inside the current year. One rebuilt sourcing around long-term farmer agreements — multi-year offtake, input supply, agronomy and a floor price beneath the market. One built a digital service for those same farmers — extension advice, credit access and daily market prices on their handsets, with sourcing running through it. She had then to place the social component: as its own budget line, reported separately; as the identical mechanism to the sourcing, so neither could be removed without the other; or as a communications layer over sourcing otherwise unchanged. And she had to name the source of any advantage: a traceability audit and certification mark; the lowest farmgate price of the season; or the farmer agreements and the data behind them.

## The Company and its Setting

Liwanag Consumer Group, founded in 1981 in Cebu City, made packaged foods, coffee and home-care products under several brands. It employed 6,500 people, turned over ₱52 billion in 2025 at an 11% EBIT margin — ₱5.7 billion — and reached consumers through 140,000 sari-sari stores as well as modern trade (see **Exhibit 1**). Coffee, coconut and corn came from roughly 28,000 smallholders across the Visayas and northern Mindanao.

Liwanag's largest cost and its largest single risk were the same population of farmers. For thirty years the company had managed that population through two separate offices: a procurement desk that negotiated a price each season, and a foundation, funded from group profit, that awarded scholarships and moved relief goods after typhoons. The two had never shared a plan, a budget or a target.

## The Situation

The supply position had deteriorated inside a single quarter. A poor harvest and the competitor's contracting drive left 78% of required coffee volume under contract, against a normally full book. The unsecured share would have to be bought at spot, at whatever the market asked (see **Exhibit 2**).

The farmer data was the part the board had not seen. The average supplying farmer earned about ₱78,000 a year, close to 60% of the rural living-income benchmark the group's analysts used; attrition from the base ran at 9% a year and the average supplier was 58.

Four numbers would be read back to Villaflor in August. The blended three-year return on the ₱1.2 billion, against the board's 18% hurdle: on the plan as it stood, the finance function projected 9%. An inclusive-growth index, kept on a hundred-point scale and moving with farmer income, resilience and retention: 28. The share of required coffee volume held under contract: 78%. And a hundred-point reading of how hard the resulting position would be for a competitor to reproduce: 22. Above the four, the board kept a one-line view of the strategy, which on 5 May read: philanthropy in disguise.

If nothing changed, the arithmetic was undramatic: Liwanag would buy the missing 22% at spot, the competitor would keep signing farmers off a base losing 9% of its members a year, and in twelve months the ₱7.8 billion customer would ask for evidence that did not exist.

## What Villaflor Controlled

At the third stage she would split the ₱1.2 billion. The versions circulating were ₱800 million to a commercial case and ₱400 million to a separate impact case, on two scorecards; ₱700 million into the foundation's relief and scholarship work with the balance to procurement; the whole envelope concentrated behind one mechanism; or the sum spread across every idea so nothing was dropped. She would say who held it — a standalone sustainability office, the foundation's giving committee, or a profit-and-loss owner in the core business — and which institutional supports to build: intent alone, a mission statement signed by the chief executive, or purpose and drive wired into structure and measurement. One number went on the scorecard: media impressions, input cost per kilo, or secured volume per peso of farmer-income lift.

At the fourth stage she would present. She could lead the board and the customer with the good the company did, with the price it had achieved, or with the mechanism itself. The board tested every plan against one live shock, and she was asked to nominate which: the competitor doubling its farmer-contract incentives, a typhoon damaging 30% of the supplying farms, or a non-governmental organisation publicly calling the programme fair-trade-washing. Her answer could fall back on relief and donations, fall back on lower farmgate prices and the spot market, or hold the plan as written. She would close by naming the number that proved the case: pesos donated and families helped, brand awareness, or secured volume per unit of income lift.

Four dials ran underneath: how much of the shock she booked as strategy rather than procurement; how far she treated supply security and farmer income as one activity or two; how hard she held the 18% line while moving the inclusive-growth index; and how much weight she put on integrating impact and return or reporting them apart.

## Closing

That evening Villaflor wrote two words at the top of a blank page — *supply* and *farmers* — and a third between them: *or*. The customer's clock had twelve months to run.
