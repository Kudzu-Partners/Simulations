# Meridian Pacific Group: Two Quarters to Align

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1773 · **Companion simulation:** Express — *Aligning the Top Team* (sim 1773), 4 rounds, EN · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On Monday, 2 March 2026, Priya Sundaram walked into her first full meeting with the seven executives she had inherited as newly appointed chief executive of Meridian Pacific Group — the first outsider to run the Singapore-headquartered technology-services group. The board's mandate: lift group growth from 3 percent to 9 percent within three years by moving SGD 180 million out of the legacy infrastructure business that still produced most of its revenue and into the cloud-and-AI division. All seven executives told her, in the room, that the plan made sense. Within the week she had heard, from more than one source, that at least two did not.

The lead independent director had already been direct about the clock: two quarters to show a top team genuinely behind the transformation and one credible result delivered before the next results, and the board — including the founding family holding 44 percent — would judge her on both. Sundaram would have to work out what the room's agreement meant, turn it into something real, decide in what order to move, and defend the outcome before a board that included the founder who had built Meridian.

## The Protagonist

Priya Sundaram, 52, had spent the previous decade running the Asia-Pacific cloud-services arm of a global technology group before Meridian's board recruited her, in early 2026, as its first chief executive from outside. She had no history with the seven executives now reporting to her, and no debts to the culture the retiring founder had built. Her mandate came with a clock attached: the board wanted the top team standing behind her, not merely nodding.

## The Decision

Sundaram's task divided into four decisions: a diagnosis of what was really driving the resistance in the room and how far to trust the "yes" from five of seven executives; a choice of method to move the team from stated agreement to real commitment; a sequence for the change message and the SGD 180 million capital shift the plan turned on; and a defence of the result before a board whose founder-chair would test whether the alignment was real or merely performed.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Pacific Group was a Singapore-headquartered technology-services company with SGD 3.1 billion in 2025 revenue, an 8 percent EBIT margin — SGD 248 million — and 11,000 employees (see Exhibit 1). Two businesses sat in tension: legacy infrastructure, 62 percent of revenue and in slow structural decline, and cloud-and-AI, 14 percent of revenue and growing more than 30 percent a year, but short of capital and people. A founding family held 44 percent alongside institutional and sovereign-fund investors, and the board's patience ran through that family as much as through the boardroom.

## The Situation

Of the seven executives Sundaram inherited, two had said, unprompted, that the transformation was right. Two others — including the Infrastructure President, who controlled 62 percent of group revenue — had told colleagues privately it would not survive contact with reality. The remaining three had said yes and nothing since (see Exhibit 2). The retiring founder, now non-executive chair, was formally out of daily management, but at least three executives still weighed their positions against what he would think.

Her first morning, Meridian's dashboard read: Genuine Alignment 38 of 100 (how real, not merely stated, the backing was), Transformation Momentum 30 of 100 (progress toward the growth target), Board Confidence 60 of 100 (trust in her pace and substance), and Derailment Risk 45 of 100 (the chance the team fractured or the cloud-and-AI division lost its people). Two senior leaders in that division were already being courted by rivals, and both had said staying turned on whether the alignment at the top was real.

If nothing changed, momentum would keep drifting near its start, the two quarters would run out, and the transformation would enter its second year with a board no longer certain it had backed the right chief executive.

## What Sundaram Controlled

Her first decision had three parts and a dial. On the Infrastructure President's resistance, she could call it pure capital logic, answerable with a stronger business case; raw power, a wish to keep control of the budget; identity and status, since the change recast his life's work as declining; or unfamiliarity with the technology the transformation depended on. On the room's "yes," she could read it as surface compliance masking private hedging beneath the nods; a working majority letting her outvote the two skeptics; or genuine alignment, ready to act on without further testing. On the founder, she could treat him as a retired figurehead and plan to work around him; ignore him, since the mandate was hers, not his; or treat him as still present and bring him along with her. Beneath all three sat a fourth: how far to push the diagnosis before acting — from a shallow read to surfacing every unconscious dynamic in the room.

Her second decision was which mechanism would move the team from stated agreement to something real. She could realign incentives and decision rights to match each executive's stake to the strategy; reshape the team by replacing its most open resister; convene a structured off-site to surface and hold the room's anxieties; co-create the roadmap with the team rather than hand it over finished; or build a shared diagnosis of why change was necessary, jointly with the team. A dial ran beneath: avoiding confrontation versus pressing hard. On the founder-chair, she could inform the full board only once the mechanism was already moving; brief him privately first and invite him to sponsor it; or bypass him entirely, acting first and explaining only afterward. On the two cloud leaders being courted by rivals, she could show them visible top-team alignment before asking either one to stay; wait, on the view that alignment should come before any conversation about their future; or offer both an immediate counter-offer of retention packages to slow any departure.

Her third decision was about order: move the full SGD 180 million reallocation immediately; lock in genuine top-team alignment before any other visible move; align the founder-chair and founding family before turning to the team; or deliver one credible early win first, to build momentum. The win itself was a separate choice — a legacy-unit cost cut to fund the pivot; a fast cloud-and-AI client win that did not threaten legacy; a large, headline capital shift away from legacy; or a single KPI the whole team would share — as was the timing of the SGD 180 million: the full amount now, to demonstrate resolve without delay; phased in gradually, starting with a modest first step now; or after alignment was real, giving the Infrastructure President a voice in it. A dial ran beneath it all: how fast to move under the board's clock, from full readiness to maximum speed.

Her fourth decision was how to present the result to the board. She could declare that the team was fully aligned and the win already delivered; give an honest account in which alignment was real but still partial, alongside a credible path forward; or frame the update around the board's own timeline and the constraints it created for her. Asked whether the team was really aligned or merely compliant, she could invite the Infrastructure President to answer the question himself, in front of the room; deflect the question to the metrics on the table and move on; or reassure the founder-chair directly that the whole team was on board. Her eighteen-month roadmap could lead with an immediate restructuring of the operating model, department by department; with the growth number and an aggressive capital timeline; or with alignment, momentum, and risk milestones, sequenced and conditional on each other. A last dial: how deliberately to hold the board's anxiety, from unaddressed to actively reassured.

## Closing

At 8:40 that morning, Sundaram asked her assistant to block two hours before the next executive-team meeting. The board's two quarters had started, and next door seven executives were waiting — two had told her the plan was right, and at least two had told someone else it was not.
