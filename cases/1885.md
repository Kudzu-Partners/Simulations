# Highveld School of Business: Accountable by Design

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1885 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1885 — *Accountable by Design* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On a Tuesday morning in February 2026, Dr. Refilwe Mahlangu read the external moderators' report twice before reacting to it. Highveld School of Business's flagship MBA course, "Managing for Results," had gone through its cyclical quality review with a finding attached: seventy percent of every student's grade came from one final group project, and the panel could not find, anywhere in that grade, evidence of what any one person had learned. Mahlangu, Academic Programme Director for the MBA, had eight weeks to bring a redesigned assessment back to the panel, or the course's accredited-credit standing would move to "under review" before the next intake enrolled.

She had written part of the scheme just flagged. Four years earlier, as lead convenor, she had built the current group project herself, reasoning that a management problem should be assessed the way it was actually solved — by a team. Now she led the redesign, inside a fixed pool of marking hours and a fixed rand budget, and the scheme carrying her name was the one under review.

## The Protagonist

Mahlangu, 44, had joined Highveld in 2014 to teach operations and organisational behaviour, taken over "Managing for Results" as lead convenor in 2019, and become Academic Programme Director for the MBA in 2024. She held a doctorate in management education and had argued for years that a management course should be assessed the way management was practised — in teams, on real problems. The moderators had not disputed that a manager's job was collaborative; they disputed that Highveld could point to any individual graduate and say she had personally learned to do it.

## The Decision

Mahlangu had to decide where individual accountability had actually gone missing from the current design; how to rebuild the grade so it evidenced one person's learning without discarding the team project that taught real collaboration; when a struggling student would first become visible rather than only at the final mark; and how to spend a fixed support budget while defending the finished redesign to the same panel.

## The Company and its Setting

Highveld School of Business was an accredited business school in Pretoria, Gauteng, founded in 2004, enrolling roughly 2,600 students across an MBA, postgraduate diplomas and an open-enrolment executive programme, on annual revenue of about ZAR 480 million, 40% of it from executive education (see **Exhibit 1**). Sixty-four full-time faculty and a panel of adjuncts taught it. "Managing for Results" sat at the centre of the MBA, taught to roughly 280 students a cohort across several sections and convenors, and its results were submitted as accreditation evidence for the whole degree.

Highveld marketed the MBA on one promise: graduates who could be held individually accountable for results — a promise the finding now undercut.

## The Situation

The headline numbers were not the problem, and that was itself part of the problem. Eighty-eight percent of students passed and ninety-two percent completed — figures the school used in its own marketing. But an anonymous survey found forty-one percent of students reporting that they had personally carried a teammate's share of the group project, or been carried themselves; and the peer-evaluation scores the course already collected tracked only about eight percent of the actual variation in what individual students knew, checked against independent markers. Highveld gathered that data every term and had never once used it in a grade.

A pilot section had already shown how a fix could make things worse: trying to answer the individual-learning question alone, it had moved sixty percent of its grade onto one final individual exam, and completion fell from ninety-two percent to seventy-four percent inside a term. Whatever Mahlangu built had to fit a budget of about six hundred marking-and-support hours per cohort and a support fund of ZAR 1.6 million, both fixed for the term. If she changed nothing, the course kept its current weighting, the finding stood unanswered, and the consequence was already named: the course's accredited-credit status would move to "under review."

## What Mahlangu Controlled

Diagnosis came first, and the numbers supported more than one reading. The healthy pass rate might mean the course already worked and the moderators were reading too much into a small survey. The missing piece might simply be the peer scores the school already collected but never graded — turn them on, and the gap closed. It might be a teaching problem rather than an assessment one, fixed by sharpening delivery, not the grade. Or individual outcomes might be tested only through a mark the whole team shared, so no version of that mark could show one person's learning. Whichever reading she chose, she also had to name which course outcome suffered most: the accountability promise the school marketed but never tested individually, the teamwork outcome the group project already tested well, or the possibility that no outcome was really damaged — and then decide how plainly to state that finding.

The grade had to be rebuilt from four pieces adding to a hundred percent (see **Exhibit 2**): an individual exam or oral defence, costly to mark but hard to fake; an individual applied or reflective assignment, cheaper per point of evidence given; the peer-evaluation scores already collected, useful as a supplement but not a substitute; and the group project, kept alive at reduced weight so the course still taught real collaboration. Every combination had to fit the six-hundred-hour budget, and Mahlangu had to choose how to hold it there: by leaving the hour count to be resolved later; by favouring the component that gave the most validity per marking hour; or by finding the extra hours inside the moderation process itself.

Timing was its own decision, with three answers: a single mid-term test around week seven, the same for every student in the cohort; an early diagnostic in week three, followed by a further mid-course individual checkpoint; or nothing individual at all, every mark counted only at the very end. Alongside that sat contribution inside the group, tracked one of three ways: peer ratings alone, gathered from teammates at the end of the project; no tracking of individual contribution inside the group at all; or logged individual contribution, tracked continuously and set alongside the weighted peer scores. And once a warning fired, someone had to own it — no one specifically, on the assumption tutors would notice informally; a named convenor who would follow up within the week; or the student advisory office, reviewing flags only once grades were in.

The last decision was where the ZAR 1.6 million would go: targeted tutoring for the bottom quartile and two weak competencies the data flagged; writing and quantitative support, open to every student regardless of risk level; or the early-warning system and the moderation time convenors needed to act on it. Mahlangu also had to decide how to defend whatever she built to the same panel: by leading with the evidence — mapped outcomes, validity per marking hour, and the support plan; by leading with the rigour of the new individual weighting, trusting that would be enough; or by pointing back at the old pass rate and arguing the course had not really been broken — and, whichever way she argued it, how hard to insist she had done both: raised rigour and protected completion, inside budget.

Four measures would judge her: individual validity, whether the grade could show what any one student had learned, starting at 25 of 100; accountability clarity, how visible ownership was during the term, starting at 20; completion, at the 92% baseline she could not afford to collapse; and the panel's own risk rating, starting at 60, where lower was safer.

## Closing

Mahlangu opened a blank redesign template at 7 a.m., eight weeks before the panel would reconvene. Four pieces of the grade had to add to a hundred percent, six hundred hours had to hold, and R1.6 million had to land somewhere in the plan. She had built the scheme the moderators had just flagged. She started typing.
