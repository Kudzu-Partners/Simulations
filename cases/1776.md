# Designing the Capstone

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1776 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1776 — *Learning by Doing* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 8 June 2026, Insa Vogel, lead learning designer at Spree Executive Academy in Berlin, read the message that had come in overnight from the chief learning officer of Nordwind AG, a logistics group with revenue of EUR 3,000,000,000 and the academy's largest custom client. The two-hour lecture Vogel's team had drafted for the capstone of Nordwind's "Leading Through Disruption" programme — the closing session for twenty-four senior managers — had been rejected. The chief learning officer called it exactly the kind of talking at people the academy was being paid not to do, and asked for a session that made the managers feel the decisions rather than hear about them. The rebuilt capstone had to run on 19 June: nine days away.

Vogel had nine days to decide, in sequence, what capability the capstone would build, which experiential format would carry it inside a hard twenty-minute limit, how the twenty-four managers would be cast into roles for the live exercise, and how the debrief and measurement that followed would be designed and defended to Nordwind's chief learning officer and an observing board member.

## The Protagonist

Insa Vogel, 43, had led design at Spree Executive Academy for six years, after eight years designing corporate training inside a Munich manufacturing group. She had joined the academy for its distinguishing commercial promise: unlike most providers, who reported participant satisfaction, Spree's contracts guaranteed a measured transfer score — a structured read on how much capability actually showed up in a participant's work sixty days after a programme — and tied part of the fee to hitting it. That guarantee was Vogel's daily work. It also meant a capstone that merely felt good in the room, without changing what a manager did back at their desk, was a design that had failed on its own terms.

## The Decision

Nordwind's contract was worth EUR 1,800,000 a year and was up for a three-year renewal, with 20% of the fee — EUR 360,000 — tied to a transfer score of at least 70, measured sixty days out; the current programme tracked at 58. Vogel's task broke into four linked design questions, one for each stage of the nine days remaining.

The first was diagnostic. Four capabilities could be named as the gap the capstone would close: a manager's ability to make fast, structured decisions under ambiguity and time pressure; a manager's command of the frameworks and models used in crisis leadership; a manager's general recognition that disruption events were difficult to lead through; or a manager's ability to deliver a polished script once a crisis had begun. Four instruments could then define "transfer" at sixty days: a line manager's rating of how far capability had moved from the baseline; an end-of-session survey asking participants directly how the day had felt; direct, on-the-job observation of whether managers made faster, structured calls under ambiguity; or a knowledge quiz administered to participants once the programme had ended. A further question was scope: the capstone could carry as few as one capability inside its twenty minutes, or as many as six.

The second question was the format itself, chosen from four options detailed in Exhibit 2, each using the twenty minutes differently. The minutes then had to be divided between exercise and debrief — the working assumption was twelve for the exercise and eight for the debrief, though the split was Vogel's to set — and a single biggest risk had to be named: the exercise itself running past the twenty-minute limit set for the session; no risk standing out enough to justify a specific plan; uneven participation, with louder voices dominating the room and quieter ones disengaging; or the technology becoming the focus instead of the thinking it was meant to provoke.

The third question was casting. The cohort included three senior executives who had said plainly that they did not do games, two quiet subject-matter experts who rarely spoke up, and one vice president known for taking over any discussion. Vogel had to decide who held the seat that made the exercise's central call — giving the seat to the vice president, rotating the seat through several different managers in turn, leaving the seat to whoever volunteered first, or giving the seat to one of the three skeptical executives — and separately how to handle the vice president: putting him in charge of the exercise outright, pairing him with one of the two quiet experts, who would hold a veto over the outcome, making no specific arrangement for him and letting the dynamic play out, or casting him as an observer who reported back to the group rather than deciding. She also had to set the crisis trigger — a scripted scenario built around one correct answer, a hard, injects-driven dilemma on a countdown with no clean answer, or an open discussion prompt with no time limit — and dial in how much felt time pressure the live run would carry, from calm deliberation to an urgent countdown.

The fourth question, facing the board, was the debrief and the measurement behind the guarantee. The structure could be a short recap of the exercise, inviting anyone who wanted to add a reflection; a staged sequence of three questions in order: what happened, what it meant, what would change; no separate debrief segment at all, with a summary sent to participants afterward; or a full four-stage walk from experience through reflection and general lessons to concrete application. Vogel had to decide how many minutes to protect for it, and how the sixty-day measurement behind the EUR 360,000 would work: the same end-of-session survey used elsewhere to gauge how the day had felt; manager-observed behaviour scored against the same 58-to-70 scale as the baseline; no formal measurement, trusting the design itself to show its own results; or a self-report from each participant on how much material they expected to apply. Whatever she chose, the twenty minutes would not stretch, so something had to give: nothing cut at all, fitting every element into the twenty minutes regardless; extra capabilities moved into a handout, keeping just one inside the session; the debrief shortened instead, since the exercise was the part people would remember; or the exercise trimmed back so the debrief was never squeezed for time.

## The Company and its Setting

Spree Executive Academy gGmbH, founded in 2011, ran roughly 120 programme-days a year for corporate clients across the DACH region, generating EUR 11,400,000 in 2025 revenue with a 16% surplus reinvested into faculty and design (see Exhibit 1). Fourteen full-time learning designers and ninety affiliated faculty delivered that work. Its differentiating claim in a crowded executive-education market was the transfer guarantee itself: where competitors sold satisfaction, Spree sold a number tied to what happened back at work.

## The Situation

Nordwind AG's crisis had been real: a supply-chain disruption exposing how unprepared its middle-management layer was for fast, ambiguous calls. "Leading Through Disruption" was the response, and the capstone was its closing module — the only piece of the twenty-four managers' training the observing board member would actually see. If the redesigned capstone underwhelmed that observation, Nordwind had signalled what would follow: it would move the three-year renewal to a rival provider, and word of a failed flagship capstone would reach Spree's other DACH clients within a business quarter. The twenty-minute limit was fixed by an agenda that placed board observation in the programme's final hour, and the transfer target was equally fixed: 58 today, 70 required, EUR 360,000 riding on the difference.

## What Vogel Controlled

Across the nine days, Vogel controlled the diagnosis of the capability gap and its measurement, the format and how its twenty minutes were split, the casting of every role in the live exercise, and the structure, length and measurement plan of the closing debrief — plus whatever she chose to cut to make the minutes hold. She would be judged on the projected transfer score against the 58-to-70 range, on how engaged the room of twenty-four managers appeared, on the strength of the debrief, and on whether the design stayed inside its limit.

## Closing

At 8:40 a.m., Vogel opened a blank page and wrote the date at the top: nine days to 19 June. Somewhere in Berlin a printer was already running off the rejected two-hour lecture deck, in case nothing better arrived in time.
