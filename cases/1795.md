# Leading the Turn: Transforming Meridian Freight Without Breaking Its People

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1795 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1795 — *Leading the Turn* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

---

On the morning of 14 March 2026, three weeks into her tenure as chief executive of Meridian Freight & Fulfilment plc, Naomi Okafor learned that Meridian's largest client considered the relationship over in all but name. The client, a fashion-marketplace retailer accounting for £154 million of revenue — 21% of the total — had told the board that its contract, due for renewal in eleven months, was "under strategic review," because Meridian could not yet offer the automated, data-integrated fulfilment its rivals already ran. Within days the same signal came from Meridian's grocery client, worth a further £186 million a year, which asked for a 9% cut in what it paid. The share price fell 18% on the two announcements together. The board's instruction to Okafor was direct: she had twelve months to show a transformation credible enough to keep both contracts, and the shape of that transformation was hers to decide.

## The Protagonist

Okafor, 49, had spent the previous decade running operational turnarounds at two European logistics groups before an executive search firm brought her to Meridian's board, hired from outside specifically to do what the previous leadership had not: modernise a business that had grown comfortable. She had no history yet with Meridian's people, its union or its clients. What she had instead was a mandate, a number and a deadline.

## The Decision

The mandate was to spend up to £60 million over three years — against £31 million of annual operating profit and £22 million of free cash flow — modernising Meridian's automation and data systems and building the capability behind them. Before she committed a pound of it, Okafor had to decide what kind of change she was actually running (see Exhibit 2). She could treat it as a fight to keep the business intact; as a cost-and-efficiency programme built around automating the depots to meet the grocery client's target; as a growth initiative aimed at winning new same-day contracts; or as the previously shelved robotics upgrade the old board had drawn up, now funded and run as business as usual. Tied to that choice was a second one: where the problem that would decide everything actually sat. It could be a leadership-and-culture problem — the latest staff survey pointed to low engagement and unclear direction — or it could be a strategy problem in the mix of markets and clients Meridian had chosen, a technology problem in an automation and data gap against competitors, or a cost problem calling for a hard restructuring of the base. Okafor also had to name the stakeholder she most needed to bring around, not simply manage: the unionised frontline; the board; the fashion client; or the analysts watching the stock. She also had to decide how much time her team spent studying the business's human and cultural side before costing anything — anywhere from a light read to an exhaustive one.

## The Company and its Setting

Meridian Freight & Fulfilment was a Coventry-based third-party logistics and e-commerce fulfilment operator, recently listed on London's AIM market, with annual revenue of £740 million and an operating margin of 4.2%, or roughly £31 million (see Exhibit 1). It employed 5,800 people, and the average employee had stayed eleven years. Automating the two flagship depots at the centre of the transformation would touch roughly 900 frontline roles.

## The Situation

Nothing about the crisis was subtle. The client review and the cost-down demand had arrived in the same week; the share price had already fallen 18%; and the board's twelve-month clock was running from the day Okafor started, not from any date she would choose herself. If nothing changed, and the automation and data gap persisted through the renewal date, the fashion client was expected to leave — and on Meridian's own reading of the risk, the grocery client would follow, putting some 46% of the company's revenue, and its recently listed shares, in play. Against that was the one asset a better-capitalised rival could not simply buy: eleven years of average tenure in a workforce that still trusted the place enough to stay — the very people automation would retrain or replace. Two things, above all, would tell the board whether the turn was real: whether the fashion client could see credible, visible progress before the renewal, and whether the board, the clients and the union still trusted where Okafor was taking them — watched alongside how many of the roughly 900 jobs she protected, and the pace at which she spent the £60 million she had been given.

## What Okafor Controlled

Beyond the diagnosis, Okafor had to build the story that explained the change to the people living through it, and decide the order in which it reached them. The narrative could be built around purpose — an account of the threat alongside the workforce's part in meeting it; around urgency alone, framed as transform-or-lose-your-job; around an inspiring destination with the threat left unsaid; or around a walkthrough of the margin numbers, the contract risk and the investment plan. She would choose whether to visit the depots and listen before saying anything formally, hold one all-hands meeting for everyone at once, secure the board's backing before speaking to anyone else, or reassure the fashion client before addressing her own workforce. Because the board, the union and the depot floor would each hear the same announcement and ask a different question of it, she would decide whether to answer each audience's own question from the same facts, give everyone an identical presentation, or concentrate mainly on the board and the analysts. And because the question of job losses could not be avoided forever, she would decide whether to name that tension and commit to redeploying and reskilling people first, to state publicly that there would be no redundancies, or to leave the question unaddressed for now.

Further out, Okafor would set the shape of the rollout: automate one depot first and prove the approach before scaling to the second, or automate both flagship depots at once. For the roughly 900 roles it touched, she could redeploy and reskill people into new positions, offer voluntary exits with no compulsory element, or make compulsory redundancies — estimated at some £9 million and, with 62% of operatives unionised, a real risk of industrial action. She would also decide how much of the £60 million to commit at each stage, how ambitious a client-visible milestone to promise before the renewal, and how much backfill and reskilling time to protect for a workforce absorbing all of it at once.

Finally, because no year-long plan runs exactly to script, Okafor had to decide in advance how she would lead if it ever wobbled: hold the pace and own any setback publicly, ease off to give the workforce room to absorb what had already changed, or push harder to protect the schedule. She had to decide, separately, how she would respond if difficult news reached people before she chose to share it — addressing her own workforce first, issuing a single formal statement, or saying nothing at all. And she had to decide what a future update to the board and clients should sound like if the plan slipped: acknowledging the delay while arguing the destination still held, confident and focused on the most favourable numbers, or a repetition of the original timeline. However she answered any of this in the first month felt like the standard she would be held to for the rest of the year.

## Closing

By the time Okafor's assistant knocked to say the board was assembling, none of these decisions were final. She had a survey showing 41% engagement, a client relationship under formal review, twelve months running from before she arrived, and 5,800 people who had stayed an average of eleven years for a company that had not yet told them why it needed to change. The number on the table was £60 million. The harder number — how much of their trust she would need to spend to get there, and how much she could afford to lose — had no line item at all.
