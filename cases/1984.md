# Maharlika Bank: The Promise and the Counter

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1984 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1984 — *Service That Sells* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of 10 March 2026, Maricel Ocampo read the first-quarter customer tracker in her office in Makati and found two numbers on the same page that could not both stand. Maharlika Bank had spent the quarter advertising fifteen-minute service. The tracker's field team, timing visits across the 280-branch network, had clocked an average of thirty-four minutes.

Ocampo had been the bank's Chief Marketing Officer for ten weeks. The board had given her ninety days to show that the service brand could still command a premium, four quarters to return customer satisfaction to 75%, and PHP 600 million to do it with. Below 65%, the board had already resolved, the budget would be cut and the bank would compete on price.

## The Protagonist

Maricel Ocampo, 44, had joined Maharlika Bank in January 2026 from a regional consumer-goods group, where she had run brand and customer insight for six years. She was the first outsider to hold the marketing seat at Maharlika. Her predecessor had built "Service from the heart," the campaign that had carried the bank's advertising for three years and was still running at PHP 220 million a year. Ocampo reported to the chief executive and presented to the board each quarter, alongside the chief financial officer, who had opposed the size of her budget in the December planning round.

## The Decision

Ocampo had to decide, quarter by quarter, what the bank told the market it would deliver, what it changed inside the organisation to deliver it, and how the PHP 600 million was divided — and to keep deciding for four quarters while the tracker printed.

## The Company and its Setting

Maharlika Bank was a universal retail bank headquartered in Makati, Metro Manila, serving 3.4 million retail customers through 280 branches and a growing digital channel. It employed 9,200 people, of whom roughly 5,600 were customer-facing: branch tellers, relationship officers, contact-centre agents and digital-support staff. Annual revenue was PHP 38 billion. For two decades the bank had competed on branch density and a warm, personal service reputation that had earned it the highest brand-trust scores among mid-tier Philippine banks.

That position had narrowed. Digital-first neobanks had entered with zero-fee accounts and heavy launch advertising, and the country's two largest banks had put money into their own service operations. Inside Maharlika, the front line was measured on sales and on satisfaction. The internal departments it depended on — information technology, operations, product and credit — were run and budgeted as cost centres.

## The Situation

The tracker released on 10 March recorded the first decline in eight years (see **Exhibit 1**). Top-two-box satisfaction — the share of surveyed customers choosing one of the two highest ratings — had fallen from 78% to 69%. The brand-equity index, the tracker's measure of what the Maharlika name added beyond the product itself, had fallen from 64 to 58. The service-quality gap, the distance between what customers said they expected and what they said they received, had widened to 1.8 points on the tracker's scale, where a lower reading was the better one. It was the widest gap the bank had recorded.

The tracker broke that gap across five measured dimensions: reliability, responsiveness, assurance, empathy, and the physical condition of the branches. A separate internal diagnostic ran alongside it (see **Exhibit 2**). Branch staff waited an average of 4.2 days for an information-technology ticket to be resolved and 6 days for a product or credit approval, against a one-day internal standard set three years earlier. Front-line engagement stood at 61%.

Four explanations were circulating in the building, and each had a sponsor. The branch network's own managers held that the counter experience had slipped and that tellers and agents needed to be faster and warmer. The operations diagnostic located the problem upstream, in the turnaround times the internal departments delivered to the front line. The agency argued that the PHP 220 million a year behind "Service from the heart" no longer bought share of voice against newer, louder competitors. The customer-insight team observed that expectations across the whole market had moved as digital entrants reset what a transaction was supposed to take, and read the 1.8-point gap as a rise in what customers expected rather than a fall in what they received.

The attrition arithmetic was not disputed. On its current trajectory the bank was losing roughly 190,000 customers a year, and each point of annual attrition represented about PHP 410 million in lost lifetime revenue. If nothing changed, the tracker would keep printing, the customers would keep leaving at that rate, and the 65% floor sat four points below where the bank already stood.

Operations had given Ocampo one further number. Thirty-four minutes was the current average. Twenty-two minutes was reachable by the third quarter with the investments available to her. Fifteen minutes was what the advertising said.

## What Ocampo Controlled

In the first quarter, Ocampo had to put a diagnosis in writing for the board: which of the four explanations she was acting on, which two of the five measured dimensions carried the gap, and how she framed the relationship between the satisfaction score and whatever sat behind it — as one thing to be fixed directly, as a symptom of the internal turnaround times, or as a brand-equity problem caused by insufficient advertising. She also chose how much of that first submission to spend quantifying the cost of doing nothing, against the competing demand to lead with the plan.

In the second quarter she set the internal terms. She could leave the internal departments without a service standard, issue a one-day standard as a target without additional capacity or recognition, or set a two-day standard funded with capacity, recognition and shared measurement. She decided how much authority a branch employee held to settle a complaint on the spot — none, a capped discretionary limit of the order of PHP 5,000 with a recovery playbook, or full authority with sign-off removed. She chose whether to move the internal departments by instruction, by treating the front line as their customer through communication, recognition and shared scorecards, or not to touch the question at all. And she committed a figure to that work, up to PHP 300 million of the PHP 600 million.

In the third quarter she divided the money and re-set the promise. External advertising took PHP 60 million, PHP 180 million or PHP 350 million. Customer-facing service investment — queue management, digital self-service, branch layout and training — took PHP 80 million, PHP 180 million or PHP 300 million. She could add a top-up of up to PHP 300 million to the internal work. The three slices plus her second-quarter commitment had to fit inside PHP 600 million. And the advertised standard became fifteen minutes, twenty-two minutes, or a "no wait, ever" claim of five minutes.

In the fourth quarter she presented to the board and absorbed whatever arrived: a viral complaint about a broken fifteen-minute promise, a neobank launching zero-fee accounts behind a PHP 300 million campaign, or a chief financial officer demanding a 20% mid-year cut. She chose what her defence led with — campaign reach and awareness, the chain from internal service through engagement to satisfaction and equity, or a commitment that 75% would arrive the following quarter. She named the one indicator she would watch monthly, choosing between advertising awareness and share of voice, internal turnaround times and front-line engagement, and the quarterly satisfaction score itself. And she stated how far she would hold the plan through a quarter in which the numbers might not yet move.

The board tracked four measures each quarter, and would judge her on them: satisfaction, the service-quality gap, the brand-equity index, and marketing return — the retained customer value produced for each peso of the PHP 600 million she committed.

## Closing

At 8:20 on the morning of 10 March, Ocampo wrote the two numbers at the top of a clean page — 15 and 34 — and beneath them the four quarters and the PHP 600 million. The board met in eleven days. The next tracker would print in June.
