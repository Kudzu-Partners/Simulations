# Siri Mattress: Nine Days from the Buyer

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1976 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1976 — *Proximity Play* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Wednesday, 1 April 2026, Anchalee Rojanasakul read SleepCo's termination letter for the third time and walked out into the finished-goods yard at Samut Prakan to look at the trucks. SleepCo was Siri Mattress' largest domestic retail partner — 18% of revenue, THB 576,000,000 a year — and the letter gave one reason. Siri quoted nine days from order to doorstep. A boxed-mattress company that had entered Thailand the previous year quoted two, and let the buyer choose firmness on a website before anything was built.

Twelve weeks later Rojanasakul would stand in front of the board with a redesign of where and how Siri made and moved its product. She had a one-time transformation budget of THB 180,000,000, a condition from the chief financial officer that every baht committed show a capital payback under 36 months, and a shortlist that cost more. Two further retail partners, worth a combined 14% of revenue — THB 448,000,000 — had told their account managers they would follow SleepCo out unless delivery came in under four days.

## The Protagonist

Rojanasakul, 44, had been Chief Innovation Officer since 2024. Before that she ran manufacturing engineering at the Samut Prakan plant, where her reputation was built on line balancing and yield — cost taken out of a factory that was already efficient, without new capital. The budget was hers because she had asked for it in November, in a paper arguing that Siri's delivery times were commercial, not logistical. The payback condition was Somchai Preechawit's price for signing it: the chief financial officer would fund a change in the business, he said, but not in the balance sheet.

## The Decision

Rojanasakul had to decide where Siri produced value and what it deployed to bring that value nearer the buyer, inside THB 180,000,000 and inside the payback window. The decision had a second half she could not defer: whatever she funded had to stand in front of real customers in the pilot the two wavering partners had offered — one peak weekend in June, on capacity that would not cover the demand it drew.

## The Company and its Setting

Siri Mattress Public Co. was a listed Thai maker of mattresses, pillows and bedding, with annual revenue of THB 3,200,000,000. Everything came out of one plant south of Bangkok, in Samut Prakan, and shipped from a single distribution hub to 140 domestic retail partners and to export customers across ASEAN. Make-to-stock and ship-from-one-place had worked for as long as customers were willing to wait.

Mattresses were bulky and low in density: a full-height queen filled most of a truck's cube and almost none of its weight limit, so a long-haul lane carried air as much as product. Siri spent THB 1,150 per unit on freight and handling, and its logistics team put roughly 22% of that against the space inside a finished mattress.

The entrant had built the opposite business: mattresses compressed, boxed and shipped by parcel carrier in two days, with firmness set online rather than by lying on a sample in a showroom. Its net promoter score in Thailand was +52; Siri's was +18.

## The Situation

Siri's own numbers described the gap rather than explaining it (see **Exhibit 1**). Order-to-delivery on stocked items ran at nine days; made-to-order units — the configured, non-standard sizes carrying the best margin — ran at twenty-one. The retail channel's research put conversion loss at roughly 6% for every delivery day beyond three, which left the twenty-one-day lane out of most decisions customers were making. Customer satisfaction, measured on a hundred-point scale from post-delivery surveys, stood at 78.

The innovation office had costed the shortlist that reached Rojanasakul — up to three regional finishing hubs and four proximity technologies (see **Exhibit 2**). She could not fund it in full; the question was which of it THB 180,000,000 would buy.

Nothing on the shortlist was free of an argument against it. Finishing hubs traded the plant's scale economics for shorter runs closer to demand. A compression line would change how the product reached customers who had bought Siri because it did not arrive in a box. The platform and the fitting technologies would capture what a buyer wanted with a precision Siri had never had, and would move no mattress closer to that buyer.

If Siri changed nothing, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The THB 576,000,000 that left with SleepCo would not return, the THB 448,000,000 attached to the two wavering partners would go within two quarters, freight would stay at THB 1,150 a unit, and the entrant would keep the customers who wanted a mattress this week.

## What Rojanasakul Controlled

She controlled four things, in sequence.

First, the reading of the problem. Three views were live in the building: that the money sat in the ASEAN export lane, where long-haul freight was heaviest; that the loss sat in the twenty-one-day made-to-order lane and in the customer who walked into a store on Saturday expecting delivery by Monday; or that nine days was competitive in the showroom trade Siri served and the entrant was selling to a different customer. On the THB 1,150 the split ran the same way — the plant's engineers pointed at handling and warehouse labour, the logistics team at the low density of finished goods on long lanes, the finance team at diesel and carrier rates. And on the kind of problem this was: a capacity problem answered by a second plant, a distance problem answered by moving where production finished, or a price problem answered by undercutting the entrant. She also had to state, as a number, how urgently Siri should act.

Second, the capital. Up to three regional micro-finishing hubs at THB 35,000,000 each, and four technologies to fund or decline: an on-demand compression-and-box line at THB 60,000,000, a digital made-to-order configuration platform at THB 45,000,000, 3D-scan firmness fitting at THB 30,000,000, and mobile pop-up fitting units at THB 25,000,000.

Third, the pilot. Where the new model went live first — all 140 partners at once, the region holding the two wavering partners, or the export lane. How to ration capacity that would not cover the demand it drew — take every order and let lead times stretch, cap intake at what the new model could serve well and route the rest through the existing channel, or discount to pull volume through the new line. What to do about the two events on the calendar, a lead-time slip at the compression supplier and platform sign-ups below plan — hold the plan and absorb them, re-allocate inside the budget by dual-sourcing the line and shifting adoption spend to in-store assistance, or go past the budget for a second line and a marketing push. And what share of eligible volume went onto the new model that weekend.

Fourth, the board. The answer to the question the chair had asked twice, why not simply build a second factory — concede that the capacity would help, undercut the entrant on price instead, or argue that a larger plant would make more of the same distance. The line she would defend the redesign with — that Siri had bought the best technology in the category, that value now arrived at the moment of demand, or that Siri now competed on price with anyone in Thailand. The plan for SleepCo — a renewal priced to win the volume back, the pilot's delivery record and a rollout co-designed for its stores, or replacing the revenue with partners the new model could serve. And, again as a number, how firmly she held whichever line she took.

She would be judged on four measures: time-to-demand, the days between an order and a delivery; delivered unit cost, the freight and handling carried by each mattress; customer satisfaction on the hundred-point scale; and the blended payback, in months, on the capital committed.

## Closing

At 7:15 p.m. Rojanasakul laid the shortlist on the table and wrote the budget across the top of the page: 180. The peak weekend was ten weeks out; the board met in twelve. SleepCo's letter had been signed by a buyer she had known for six years.
