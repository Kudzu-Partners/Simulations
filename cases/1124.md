# Calvessa Cold Chain: The First Ninety Seconds

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1124 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1124 — *PublicSpeakingNerves — The First 90 Seconds* · **v1.0 · October 2026**

**Time required: 80 minutes**

---

At 09:25 on Wednesday, May 20, 2026, Leonor Castilho-Brandt, head of quality at Calvessa Cold Chain, sat in a small meeting room on the fourth floor of the company's Porto office with her laptop open and her hands colder than the room could explain. At 09:40 the next morning she would walk onto the stage of the Calvessa Customer Forum in Lisbon and give a twenty-minute talk to about 420 people, most of them customers. The talk was written. She had rehearsed it aloud twice. What she kept replaying was the walk to the lectern, the silence, and the first sentence.

At 09:30 she had a video call with Maren Duquesne, the speaking coach the forum's organisers had hired for first-time speakers. Duquesne would take her through three passes: the open, the routine for the hours before walk-on, and a run of the first ninety seconds as if it were five minutes to go. By the end of the third pass Castilho-Brandt had to have settled how the talk would begin, what she would do with her body before it, and how the first ninety seconds would sound.

## The Protagonist

Castilho-Brandt was thirty-four. A chemical engineer, she had joined Calvessa in 2017 as a warehouse quality analyst and had run the quality function since 2023, with a team of 26. She presented every quarter to the operations committee, a room of twelve, and once, in 2025, to an internal review of about forty. Nobody in the company knew her material better. She had never spoken from a stage, and the largest audience she had faced was a tenth the size of Thursday's.

## The Decision

The talk itself was not in question; its slides had been signed off on May 8. What Castilho-Brandt had to decide was the first ninety seconds and everything that fed them: the first sentence and how much of the talk she would know by heart; the routine for her breathing, caffeine, activity and posture in the hours before; and the pace, pitch, pause and eye contact with which she would deliver the open.

Duquesne had said at the start that she would not try to make her feel calm, and that nothing on her sheet measured how calm she felt.

## The Company and its Setting

Calvessa Cold Chain, S.A., founded in 2008 and headquartered in Porto, ran refrigerated warehouses and trucks for food and pharmaceutical shippers in Portugal, Spain and Morocco. It employed about 1,900 people and reported €312 million in revenue for 2025. The Customer Forum, held every May since 2015, was its main event of the year: two days in a Lisbon conference hotel, a main hall of 450 seats, every session streamed to customers who could not attend and kept on the company's website afterwards.

The chief executive, Tomás Vilarinho-Kast, opened the second morning. Castilho-Brandt's slot followed his, from 09:40 to 10:00. He had asked her in April to present the programme she had led since 2024, which tracked the temperature of every pharmaceutical pallet in transit and alerted drivers before a load left its range. Temperature excursions on pharmaceutical loads had fallen from 2.1% of shipments in 2023 to 0.6% in 2025 (see Exhibit 1). Several of the customers in the hall had paid for that tracking; some had filed claims before it existed.

## The Situation

Advice had arrived from every direction. Vilarinho-Kast opened every all-hands meeting with a joke and told her the room would be friendly. Sunniva Halloran, the head of fleet, who had spoken at the 2025 forum, had learned all twenty minutes of her talk word for word and said it was the only way she had got through it. Dário Meireles-Antunes, the operations director and Castilho-Brandt's manager, told her not to script anything: rooms could tell when a speaker was reciting. A colleague in quality swore by a run the evening before any big meeting; another by a glass of wine before a wedding speech. Castilho-Brandt herself drank two espressos before every committee meeting.

If she changed nothing, the plan was the one she had rehearsed: thank the organisers, move to the first slide, and say the rest in her own words, as she did at committee meetings, after her two espressos. Duquesne's sheet would show where that left her. It carried four readings, each out of 100 and each at 50 before the first pass (see Exhibit 1). **Audience Verdict** was Duquesne's reading of how the hall would judge the open; she told every speaker that a hall made up its mind within about thirty seconds. **Body Protocol** read how well the routine before walk-on prepared the speaker's body. **Delivery Quality** read how the first ninety seconds would sound and look from the hall. **Stage Readiness** drew the three together. Each pass moved the reading it measured, and Duquesne wrote one line of feedback after it. At the end she would place the run in one of four bands: Beginner, Solid, Strong or Stage-Ready.

## What Castilho-Brandt Controlled

In the first pass she settled three things (see Exhibit 2). **When she would do the work**: the night before, about twenty-four hours out; the morning of the talk, about five hours out; or the five minutes before she walked on. **Her opening sentence**: a stake, eight to fifteen words on something the hall had reason to care about; a promise of what the audience would leave with; a concrete image of a place, a person and an action; a thank-you for having her; a line admitting she was a little nervous and asking the hall to bear with her; a joke; or a housekeeping note. And **how much to learn by heart**: the first ninety seconds word for word, with the rest of the talk in bullet points; the first ninety seconds and the last sixty word for word; the whole twenty minutes word for word; or nothing, speaking naturally.

In the second pass she settled the routine. **Her breathing**: box breathing, four counts in, four held, four out and four held, for ninety seconds; long exhales, six counts out and four in, for sixty seconds; holding her breath and counting to ten; fast, shallow breaths; or no breathing routine. **Caffeine**: none; half her usual; her usual; or extra, to be sharp. **What she did beforehand**: twenty to thirty minutes of cardio in the twenty-four hours before; an extra hour of sleep the night before; a power pose in the bathroom; pacing the green room; or a drink to take the edge off. And **her posture and voice**: humming for twenty seconds, rolling her shoulders and planting her feet; picking three friendly faces in the hall in advance; choosing one friendly face to lock onto; or walking straight on and sorting it out on stage.

In the third pass she settled the delivery. **Her pace**: 100 to 120 words a minute, deliberate; 130 to 150, which would feel slow to her; or 180 and above, the speed she reached when she rushed. **Her pitch on the first sentence**: dropped, to ground the voice; her normal range; or allowed to climb in the hope it would settle. **The pause after the first sentence**: two seconds; none, to keep momentum; or five seconds or more. And **where her eyes went**: three friendly faces, left, centre and right, in rotation; one friendly face, held; a rapid scan across the hall; or the slides and the floor.

## Closing

At 09:29 Castilho-Brandt read the first line of her notes aloud to the empty room. Her voice came out higher than she remembered it. The call link turned green. In twenty-four hours and eleven minutes Vilarinho-Kast would say her name and step away from the lectern.
