## Abstract

Helix Pharma Distribution, a USD 640M regional pharmaceutical distributor serving hospital networks, pharmacy chains, and independent pharmacies in a mid-income emerging market, entered Q1 2026 with a Balanced Scorecard composite of 41/100, an EBITDA margin of 4.2% (industry median 7.8%), a customer NPS of 22, and a customer-retention rate of 78%. A new CEO, Daniel Hirsch, had been appointed in January with a board mandate to move the composite above 65 within eight quarters. The transformation budget of USD 18M per quarter — approved for eight quarters — was to be allocated across four perspectives (Financial, Customer, Process, and Learning & Growth) that did not respond at the same speed. The causal chain ran Learning → Process → Customer → Financial with a two-to-four-quarter lag. The case puts Hirsch inside his office the evening before the first quarter's allocation is ratified and sets up Round 1 of the simulation.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this case discussion, participants will be able to:

1. Apply the Balanced Scorecard framework to a live allocation decision, distinguishing leading from lagging indicators.
2. Diagnose the causal chain that links Learning & Growth through Process and Customer into Financial performance, and articulate the lag structure.
3. Evaluate the trade-off between short-run composite lift (financial-heavy) and medium-run composite compounding (learning-heavy).
4. Recommend a quarterly allocation that preserves optionality for mid-course correction.
5. Design a dashboard that surfaces the causal chain to a board that reads only lagging indicators.

On the evening of **January 28, 2026**, **Daniel Hirsch** sat at the long oak table in Helix Pharma Distribution's executive-floor boardroom and looked, one more time, at the four sliders his head of strategy had printed as a mock-up on a single sheet of paper. The sliders — Financial, Customer, Process, Learning & Growth — summed to 100%. The four numbers written in the corners of the sheet were four different allocations from four different senior voices. The next morning's Executive Committee meeting was scheduled for 8:00 a.m.

---

## The Protagonist

Daniel Hirsch, 52, had taken over as CEO of Helix Pharma Distribution three weeks earlier. A chemical engineer by training and an EMBA from a leading school, he had spent seventeen years in pharmaceutical distribution — eight years as head of logistics at a regional competitor, five years as Chief Operating Officer of a specialty-pharma distributor in a neighboring country, and four years as President of the same company after leading its turnaround from a 3.8% to a 9.1% EBITDA margin. He had been hired by Helix's board on the strength of that turnaround and against the internal preference for his predecessor's successor. His contract carried an eight-quarter vesting gate tied to the BSC composite — a metric Hirsch himself had insisted on during his negotiation.

---

## The Decision

Hirsch had to ratify, by 8:00 a.m. on January 29, the Q1 transformation-budget allocation across four perspectives that the simulation would treat as his Round 1 input:

- **Financial (F)** — margin-recovery initiatives (working-capital programs, pricing analytics, logistics optimization).
- **Customer (C)** — NPS and retention (service-level agreements, customer-success staffing, loyalty programs).
- **Process (P)** — internal operating-excellence initiatives (warehouse automation, ERP upgrades, S&OP redesign).
- **Learning & Growth (L)** — capability investments (analytics hiring, training, management-development, culture).

The sliders had to sum to 100%. The total quarterly budget was USD 18M. The causal chain — documented in the November 2025 operating review that had preceded Hirsch's hire — ran L → P → C → F with empirical lags of two, three, and four quarters between perspectives. A Q1 allocation that starved Learning to fund Financial would lift the composite in Q1–Q2 and collapse it in Q5–Q6. An allocation that over-funded Learning would lift the composite in Q6–Q8 and drag it in Q1–Q3. The board's mandate was "move the composite to 65 by Q8." The mandate did not specify the path.

---

## Company Background

Helix Pharma Distribution had been founded in 1992 in the country's commercial capital as a hospital-focused pharmaceutical distributor and had expanded over three decades into pharmacy-chain and independent-pharmacy channels across five countries. 2025 revenue was USD 640M with an EBITDA margin of 4.2% (against an industry median of 7.8%). The company operated three distribution centers (DCs), served 2,200 customer sites with 18,000 SKUs, and employed 1,850 people. Customer concentration was moderate: top-ten customers 44%, top-fifty customers 71%.

Helix had been majority-owned (61%) since 2018 by a mid-market regional PE fund (Cordillera Partners, Fund III, 2018 vintage), which had approved the 2025–2027 value-creation plan with three headline targets: EBITDA margin to 8.5%, NPS to 45, and customer retention to 88%. The BSC composite of 41 at the start of 2026 reflected Financial 28, Customer 42, Process 48, and Learning & Growth 46 — meaning Financial was the weakest pillar, but the causal chain placed Learning & Growth upstream of Financial by three-to-four quarters.

## Industry and Market Context

The regional pharmaceutical-distribution sector had consolidated substantially in 2021–2025. Three findings from the operating-review literature were consequential for Hirsch's decision. First, distributors whose transformation programs front-loaded Financial and Process investments and back-loaded Learning & Growth showed average composite lifts of 12 points in Q1–Q4 followed by a median reversal of 7 points in Q5–Q8. Second, distributors whose programs front-loaded Learning & Growth and Process showed flat or negative Q1–Q4 composite movement followed by a median lift of 18 points in Q5–Q8. Third, balanced allocations (within ±10 points of even 25/25/25/25) showed the lowest variance and the highest median eight-quarter composite, but required board tolerance for an early-quarter composite that did not move.

The sector's analyst community had, since 2022, increasingly asked for leading-indicator disclosure — training hours, employee-engagement scores, process-KPI coverage — alongside lagging financial results, a development Cordillera Partners had signalled it supported in principle but did not yet enforce.

---

## The Situation

### How we got here

Helix's EBITDA margin had drifted from 6.1% (2021) to 4.2% (2025) on three mechanisms: (a) price compression from two large hospital-network customers renegotiating in 2023; (b) working-capital decay as the customer-pays-at-120-days norm crept into the independent-pharmacy channel; and (c) a visible gap in analytics and S&OP discipline relative to the regional market leader. The November 2025 operating review, commissioned by Cordillera, had diagnosed the decay as a "capability first, process second, customer third, financial fourth" problem — that is, the causal chain was explicit in the diagnosis and the recommendation was to fund it from the upstream end.

### What Hirsch knew

The internal analytics team had modeled six allocations across the eight-quarter horizon. A Financial-heavy allocation (40/25/20/15) delivered Q2 composite +8 and Q8 composite +14 (to 55). A Learning-heavy allocation (15/25/25/35) delivered Q2 composite +3 and Q8 composite +26 (to 67). A balanced allocation (25/25/25/25) delivered Q2 composite +5 and Q8 composite +22 (to 63). The board target was 65. The Learning-heavy allocation was the only one that exceeded the target in the median run; it was also the one that looked worst in Q1.

### What he did not know

Whether the board's "65 by Q8" mandate was hard or soft in practice — whether missing 65 on Q8 by two points would cost him his job. Whether the two largest hospital customers would renegotiate again in Q4 (the rumor from the commercial team) and, if they did, whether the Financial-perspective gains from Q1 spend would survive the renegotiation. Whether the Learning-heavy allocation would survive three quarters of a flat composite before Cordillera intervened.

### What others were saying

**Sara Klein**, CFO, argued for **Financial-Heavy (40/25/20/15)**: *"The composite is forty-one. The margin is four point two. Fund the Financial perspective in Q1. Hit fifty by Q3. Earn the room to do the longer work."*

**Tomás Aguirre**, Chief Operating Officer, argued for **Process-Heavy (20/20/40/20)**: *"The warehouse ERP upgrade pays back in seven months. Fund Process first. Customer lifts when service levels do."*

**Rocío Franco**, Head of Strategy, argued for **Learning-Heavy (15/25/25/35)**: *"The causal chain is the entire point. Front-loading Learning is the only allocation whose Q8 composite crosses sixty-five in the median run. Buy the board three quarters of patience. Sell the patience with the dashboard."*

---

## Options Under Consideration

Hirsch had narrowed the space to three options.

- **Option A — Financial-Heavy (Klein plan):** 40 F / 25 C / 20 P / 15 L. Projected Q2 composite 49; Q4 52; Q6 54; Q8 55. Earnings visible. Long-tail composite below target.

- **Option B — Balanced with Learning Tilt (Hirsch's draft):** 25 F / 25 C / 25 P / 25 L. Projected Q2 composite 46; Q4 55; Q6 60; Q8 63. Board tolerance test at Q2. Target narrowly missed.

- **Option C — Learning-Heavy (Franco plan):** 15 F / 25 C / 25 P / 35 L. Projected Q2 composite 44; Q4 52; Q6 62; Q8 67. Q2 will look worst in all three options. Q8 is the only one above target.

Hirsch suspected Option C was right on the eight-quarter arithmetic and wrong on the Cordillera-quarterly-call arithmetic; Option B was right on political tractability and narrowly lost the target; Option A was the option that had produced the 41 they were trying to move away from. At 9:14 p.m. he turned off the boardroom lights.
