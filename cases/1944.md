# Halden & Crew Engineering: The Pulse at 51

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1944 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1944 — *Feeling the Change* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday 9 March 2026, Imogen Farrar opened the weekly pulse report and found the score at 51. It had read 72 before the merger completed, five weeks earlier. Beneath the number sat the free-text extract her team coded every Friday: 58% of comments now used words the scheme classified as anger, anxiety or betrayal, against 20% a fortnight before.

Farrar had until the integration board of 15 May to bring regretted attrition among Halden & Crew Engineering's 210 senior engineers below 10% a year. It was running at 19%. If the figure was still above the line when the board met, the board had told her it would take the GBP 9 million of merger synergies out of headcount instead — compulsory redundancies at a firm that had promised for thirty years it would never make any.

## The Protagonist

Imogen Farrar, 44, had been Group People Director of Halden & Crew since 2021, after eight years running the firm's resourcing function. She had written the Day-1 message that went to all 1,770 employees on the morning the merger completed. It described a merger of equals and committed the group to no compulsory redundancies. Her name was on it.

## The Decision

Over the eight weeks before the board papers were due, Farrar had to settle four things in sequence: what she treated as the reason people were resigning; how she spent the one fund the integration committee had not frozen; what she permitted ninety people to do with roles that now existed twice; and what she said to the board and to the trade press. She had GBP 1.2 million and no authority to spend anything beyond it.

## The Company and its Setting

Halden & Crew Engineering, headquartered in Leeds, designed water and environmental infrastructure for United Kingdom utilities. Five weeks before, it had completed an all-share merger with Tarn Civil Partners, a 620-person rival based in Bristol, taking a 1,150-person firm to a group of 1,770. The logic was scale: framework contracts neither firm could win alone.

What Halden & Crew recruited on was continuity. The firm had been engineer-led since it was founded, and for three decades it had told candidates and staff that it did not make people redundant. Its billable core was 210 senior engineers who signed off designs, carried the client relationships and could not be replaced inside a quarter.

Three things reached the workforce in the same week at the end of February. The new organisation chart showed 90 roles that now existed twice across the two firms. A synergy slide prepared for the integration committee leaked, citing GBP 9 million of cost reduction "including approximately 6% of headcount." And the pay-data merge revealed that the two firms' bands differed by as much as 14% for the same grade.

## The Situation

The dashboard Farrar took to the integration committee carried four measures (see **Exhibit 1**). Regretted attrition tracked the annualised rate at which the 210 senior engineers resigned into jobs the firm wanted them to keep; a settled year ran at about 7%, and it now stood at 19%. Wellbeing was the weekly pulse score out of 100, down from 72 to 51. Perceived fairness was the share of staff agreeing that the integration was being handled fairly, indexed to 100: 29% on the Tarn side, 41% on the Halden side, a combined reading of 35. Change adoption measured how far people had engaged with the new structure at all — attending the briefings, using the new reporting lines, applying for the new roles — and sat at 30.

The attrition line was the one with a price on it: 19% of 210 engineers was around forty resignations a year, and the firm's recruitment records put replacement at roughly GBP 95,000 each, before the client projects that stalled while a signing engineer's seat was empty. Nothing about the rate suggested it would fall on its own: four engineers had resigned in five weeks, and the four exit conversations pointed in four directions. One had accepted a named increase from a competitor, having seen what a Tarn-side peer earned at her grade. One wrote that nobody had told her anything since the announcement. One wrote that his job now appeared twice on the chart and nobody would say whose it was. One gave notice the day after the synergy slide began circulating.

The integration committee had frozen discretionary spend across the group until the synergy plan was signed off, leaving Farrar a single ring-fenced fund of GBP 1.2 million that had to be committed in full. It had costed the available interventions before handing it over (see **Exhibit 2**). Reshaping roles — letting people alter the tasks, the working relationships and the remit of a job rather than being handed a new one — carried a separate target of GBP 150,000, on the argument that it was mostly manager time.

If the eight weeks passed without intervention, the arithmetic was not in dispute. Attrition at 19% would cost about GBP 3.8 million a year in replacement alone; the pulse and fairness readings had fallen in every week since the announcement; and on 15 May the board would take the synergy out of the 90 duplicated roles.

## What Farrar Controlled

She began with what she treated as the driver of the resignations, choosing between the leaked slide and its 6% headcount figure, the 14% pay-band gap at the same grade, the silence since Day 1 against a thirty-year promise, and the 90 duplicated roles on the new chart. She then decided how to read the evidence: map each event separately to the reaction and the group it reached, work from the single pulse score and the size of its fall, or work only from the sharpest movement of the past fortnight. She chose which group she answered first — all 1,770 employees at once with one message, the 90 duplicated-role holders and the Tarn-side engineers, or the Halden side of the merged firm — and how much weight she gave the free-text comments in the diagnosis.

She then split the GBP 1.2 million across the three costed headings, in any proportion she could defend, and decided what the 210 senior engineers would encounter first: a revised figure on the payslip, nothing addressed to them specifically, or a conversation about their role alongside a published process.

For the 90 duplicated-role holders she set the scope — none offered a conversation about reshaping their role, roughly forty, or all ninety — and the guardrails: none at all, every request through the integration committee, or compliance and quality-assurance duties assigned by name first and reshaping permitted inside what remained. She fixed what line managers could approve without escalating: nothing, everything, or changes to tasks and working relationships with grade and pay referred upward. And she set the enablement spend against the GBP 150,000 target.

For 15 May she chose the framing she took to the board — that fairness was the right thing to do, that the synergy would come from cutting the 90 duplicated roles, or that attrition among the engineers was the cash the GBP 9 million was leaking. She chose the response to the trade press, which had the slide: deny the headcount figure and re-run the one-team campaign, decline to comment, or restate the no-compulsory-redundancies commitment and set out the plan. She chose what she committed to: that the target could not be met, that sentiment would improve, or that attrition would be below 10% by 15 May with no compulsory redundancies. And she set how firmly, from 0 to 100, she would hold the position under challenge.

## Closing

At 7:50 that Monday morning Farrar wrote three figures on the back of the pulse report — 51, 35, 19 — and beside them the date the board met. She had eight weeks and one fund she could not add to.
