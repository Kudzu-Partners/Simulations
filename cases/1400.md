# Caldorra Industrial Services: The Lighthouse Price

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1400 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1400 — *B2B Value-Based Pricing — Sentinela LatAm Pipeline* · **v1.0 · September 2026**

**Time required: 90 minutes**

---

At 7:42 AM on Tuesday, March 31, 2026, Marina Quesada, commercial director of Caldorra Industrial Services, forwarded an email to her leadership team from the company's São Paulo office. It had arrived at 23:14 the night before from Eduardo Vargas, chief procurement officer of RefiCosta, a 250,000-barrel-a-day refinery in Costa Rica. Vargas would proceed with Sentinela, Caldorra's predictive-maintenance platform, at US$360,000 a year, 25% below the last offer. He had, he wrote, an indicative quote from a comparable provider at US$340,000. He wanted confirmation by May 15.

Quesada's note above the forwarded email ran to two lines. She believed the competitor quote was a bluff, but the discount demand was real. She did not want to defend US$360,000. She wanted to defend US$1.5 million, and the price she set at RefiCosta would be the reference for every other refinery in the pipeline.

## The Protagonist

Quesada had spent fifteen years selling industrial software across Latin America before joining Caldorra to build its commercial team. She had closed large deals and lost some to procurement departments that treated software like spare parts. She ran twelve open opportunities for the coming cycle and reported to a chief financial officer, Andrés Calderón, who asked for one slide on every price above the price book.

## The Decision

Quesada had to set the commercial terms for RefiCosta and then for the eleven deals behind it (see Exhibit 2). For each customer she would choose a pricing structure, set an annual list price and decide how to answer procurement's counter. RefiCosta was the lighthouse deal: whatever Caldorra charged there, the next customers would hear about.

## The Company and its Setting

Caldorra Industrial Services was the business-software arm of the Caldorra energy group. Sentinela read sensor data from rotating equipment such as compressors, pumps and turbines, detected anomalies, estimated remaining useful life and told maintenance teams which assets were about to fail. In Caldorra's flagship deployments it had cut unplanned downtime by 40% to 60%.

Delivering Sentinela cost Caldorra about US$200,000 per site per year in data engineering, model maintenance, customer success and cloud infrastructure. The price book, built on a cost-plus markup, listed it at US$480,000 a year. The sales team routinely closed at US$380,000 to US$420,000 after a "standard" discount of 15% to 20%.

The customers were refineries and petrochemical plants from Mexico to Uruguay. Some were private, with reliability engineers who ran their own performance indicators; others were state-owned and bought on procurement rules. Caldorra's commercial team sorted them into four segments by two traits: the size of the value Sentinela could create for them, and their readiness to buy on value rather than on cost. Segment 1 combined a large value pool with low readiness; Segment 2, a large pool with high readiness; Segment 3, a small pool with low readiness; Segment 4, a small pool with high readiness.

## The Situation

At a discovery workshop, RefiCosta's reliability-engineering manager, Patricia Solano, had put the refinery's problem in three numbers: 47 unplanned downtime events the previous year, 210 hours of lost throughput and about US$21 million of lost margin. From those and the plant data, Quesada's team had built the economic value Sentinela would create for RefiCosta each year. Avoided downtime was by far the largest line, followed by longer equipment life, labour productivity and an insurance and safety credit. Against those sat the customer's own costs of integration, training and continuing data-quality work. Net, the incremental value pool came to US$3.95 million a year (see Exhibit 1).

At the price book's US$480,000, Caldorra would capture about 12% of that pool. At Vargas's US$360,000, it would capture about 9%. If nothing changed, the pattern was clear enough: the sales team would discount from a cost-plus list, procurement would push for more, and each concession would become the next customer's opening position.

Inside Caldorra, nobody agreed on the answer. Diego Restrepo, head of customer success, wanted US$800,000, because RefiCosta would sign quickly at that price and Caldorra needed a reference customer. Carla Mendieta, head of product, wanted an outcome-based contract, because Caldorra had built the measurement infrastructure to support one and its competitors had not. Quesada's draft proposal carried a list price of US$1.8 million, with room to close near US$1.5 million. Calderón wanted a number he could defend to the executive committee, which met in six weeks, and one that would survive Vargas.

## What Quesada Controlled

For each of the twelve deals, the briefing on her screen gave the customer's name and country, its capacity in thousands of barrels per day, its segment, its net value pool in US$ thousands, its readiness to buy on value, and how aggressive its procurement team was. Against that, she settled three things.

The **pricing structure**: a **conservative fixed fee**; a **premium fixed fee**, a higher list price expected to be negotiated; or an **outcome-based** contract, a base fee plus a share of the measured downtime savings. Caldorra's pricing playbook aimed each structure at a different share of the customer's value pool, the conservative fee lowest and the outcome-based model highest.

The **annual list price**, in US$ thousands, anywhere from US$100,000 to US$10 million a year. The draft for RefiCosta stood at US$1.8 million.

The **response to procurement's counter**, which every buyer made in some form: **hold the price and concede on terms** such as contract length, payment schedule or scope; **discount 10%**; **discount 25%**; or **walk away** rather than go below policy.

Procurement would then either sign or not. Quesada would watch five numbers across the pipeline. **Cumulative net present value** summed the discounted contract revenue of every deal signed, at the 10% rate Caldorra's finance team used. **Average value capture** measured realized price as a share of the customers' value pools. **Deals closed** and **deals lost** counted signatures and refusals. **Pricing discipline**, an index from 0 to 100 that Calderón's office kept on how consistently the sales organisation held its prices, stood at 70 when the cycle began; each response to procurement moved it. For each deal, the pipeline log would record the customer, segment, structure, list price, closing price, whether the customer signed, the share of value captured and the deal's net present value. The executive committee would judge the cycle on value created for Caldorra, share of value captured, deals won and discipline left intact.

## Closing

At 8:15 AM Calderón replied to the thread with a single line: "Beautiful number. So what do we charge them?" Quesada opened the RefiCosta proposal, looked at the US$1.8 million in the price cell and the US$360,000 in Vargas's email, and started drafting the reply that the next eleven customers would eventually hear about.
