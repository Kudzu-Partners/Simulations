# Rheinwerk Automation: The Bid for Voltura

**Case Code:** FIN-2026-1906 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1906 — *The Premium Trap* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, March 9, 2026, Sabine Hartwig took the lift to the eleventh floor of Rheinwerk Automation AG's Frankfurt headquarters and found the meeting room already renamed. Someone had printed VOLTURA on a sheet and taped it to the glass. Inside, Lukas Brandt, Rheinwerk's Chief Executive Officer, stood at the whiteboard with a two-page memo laid on every chair. He waited until the room filled. "This is the one. Whatever the model says, we are not losing Voltura."

Hartwig, Chief Financial Officer since 2022, had twenty-one days. Voltura Robotics GmbH's bankers had set a binding-bid deadline of Monday, March 30, and would take one number, in writing, with no second round. In those three weeks Hartwig had to build a valuation, decide how much of the time to spend on process rather than analysis, and commit Rheinwerk to a price.

## The Protagonist

Hartwig, 44, had come from the leveraged-finance desk of a German commercial bank, where she spent nine years underwriting mid-cap industrial credits and watching several come back. She had financed every acquisition Brandt had led since she arrived, and had never opposed one at the board table. Her long-term compensation moved with Rheinwerk's share price, as did Brandt's. She chaired the capital committee, so whatever the bid did to leverage, she would explain it to the rating agency.

## The Decision

Hartwig had to decide what Voltura was worth to Rheinwerk, how much of the twenty-one days to spend building a process around that judgement, and what single number to sign on March 30 — with a rival bidding and the Chief Executive already on record.

## The Company and its Setting

Rheinwerk Automation AG, listed in Frankfurt, designed control systems, drive electronics and factory software for European automotive, packaging and logistics plants. In FY2025 it reported revenue of EUR 1.8 billion and EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation — of EUR 306 million. It held EUR 240 million of net cash and carried a BBB+ investment-grade rating.

Brandt had led every acquisition the group had made, and had never missed an integration target he had publicly committed to. The press wrote about the record, and analysts modelled Rheinwerk as a company whose share price already contained the next deal.

Voltura Robotics GmbH, of Munich, built collaborative warehouse robots. Brandt had chased it for three years; it would put Rheinwerk into autonomous intralogistics, the segment analysts said the group was missing. Voltura had put itself in play, and a United States automation major was in the process too.

## The Situation

Voltura's defensible standalone enterprise value was EUR 1.0 billion — last-twelve-months EBITDA of EUR 80 million at 12.5 times, the multiple the robotics comparables supported. The sell-side was guiding bidders toward EUR 1.5 billion, a 50% control premium against a European median of roughly 30% for comparable technology transactions (see **Exhibit 1**). The United States bidder had opened at EUR 1.35 billion. Voltura's bankers had told both sides that EUR 1.55 billion would win it. Every ten points of premium was about EUR 100 million of cash.

Jonas Wieland, head of corporate development, had built the synergy case. His central estimate was EUR 90 million of annual run-rate synergies, with a credible band from EUR 45 million to EUR 130 million and a schedule reaching the full run rate in the second year. A bid at the guided level would take Rheinwerk from roughly no net debt toward the 3.0 times leverage covenant, and put the BBB+ rating in front of a committee.

Brandt's memo made five claims. It opened by stating that EUR 1.5 billion was fair. It set out the evidence supporting the acquisition. It described Voltura as strategically essential — a phrase he had used with two board members and a journalist. It noted that he had never missed a target he had committed to. And it carried Wieland's schedule reaching full run rate in year two. Dr. Helene Straub, the Lead Independent Director, had asked Hartwig which of the five she intended to treat as evidence.

Four measures would follow the deal into the board pack. **Acquisition return** was the value created per Rheinwerk share against the price paid, in euros per share. **Premium paid** was the percentage over Voltura's EUR 1.0 billion standalone value. **Synergy capture** was the share of Wieland's EUR 90 million base case actually realised, from 0% to 100%. A **decision-bias score** recorded how far the deal file showed conviction running ahead of evidence. The board secretariat kept it on a 0-to-100 scale where lower was better, and it stood at 70.

If Hartwig worked the memo forward, the arithmetic was not in doubt. The bid would be the guided EUR 1.5 billion, roughly EUR 500 million above standalone value. That EUR 500 million would be recovered, or not, out of the synergies in Wieland's case. The rival would still be bidding, and the number would be signed with all twenty-one days spent on analysis and none on how the analysis got approved.

## What Hartwig Controlled

Her first choice was where the valuation began. She could start at Voltura's EUR 1.0 billion standalone value and add synergies as a weighted range. She could start at the European median premium of about 30% and reason toward a defensible figure. Or she could start at the sell-side's EUR 1.5 billion and identify the synergy set that would support it.

Her second choice was what to do with Wieland's band. She could carry the whole EUR 45 million to EUR 130 million range and capitalise a probability-weighted figure, capitalise the EUR 90 million central estimate, or capitalise the EUR 130 million upper case. Her third was how many of the memo's five claims to record on Straub's register as conviction rather than evidence — none, all five, or any number between.

Nine of the twenty-one days were available for process rather than analysis, and five things could be bought with them (see **Exhibit 2**): an independent valuation built from completed comparable transactions rather than from Voltura's own numbers; a session in which the team assumed the deal had failed by 2028 and wrote down why; a maximum price ratified in advance by Straub; a group assigned to argue the synergy case down; and a change in the approval route so that the executive championing the deal was not the one approving it. Each cost days, and each cost standing with Brandt. She could defend the days to the board as time bought, take only the steps Brandt would tolerate, or keep the days for the valuation and go straight to the number.

On March 30 she would set the premium itself, anywhere from zero to seventy points over standalone, in steps of five. She would hold whatever ceiling had been ratified, move it up to stay in the auction, or set it aside to clear the rival. She would also structure the consideration: an earn-out, a contingent value right and part stock, leaving Voltura's owners with some of the synergy risk; a modest earn-out with the balance in cash; or all cash at closing, on a clean price the sellers could bank.

What followed the signing was hers as well. She would run the first hundred days on the standard playbook at the pace the line could absorb; on a detailed plan of named owners, tracked milestones and a paced ramp; or on an accelerated plan that took the full run rate to the market inside year one. She would reconcile the premium to realised synergies and put write-down risk on the record; restate the strategic case and hold the synergy numbers until the ramp was further on; or report the acquisition closed and the integration running to the plan the board had approved. And she would decide how much of the original analysis she would concede had been optimistic.

## Closing

Brandt capped the marker and left the memo on the whiteboard ledge. Hartwig took a copy back to her office, wrote **1.0** and **1.5** at opposite ends of a blank page, and looked at the twenty-one days on the calendar. Nine of them could be spent on how the decision was made. The bankers wanted one number on March 30, and the American bidder was already at EUR 1.35 billion.
