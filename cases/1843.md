# Marmara Beyaz Eşya: Twelve Months to Converge

On the morning of 9 March 2026, at the first full post-acquisition operating review inside Marmara Beyaz Eşya A.Ş.'s plant in Gebze, Elif Demir listened to the Chief Operating Officer of NordHaus Group summarise eighteen months of integration work in one sentence: almost nothing had transferred. Demir, Managing Director of the plant, had one budget cycle — the next twelve months — to show measurable movement toward NordHaus's operating standards, or NordHaus would fly in an expatriate team from Stuttgart to replace the entire local leadership.

The ultimatum was not a request for more effort. It asked Demir and her team to decide, across four working sessions over the year, how a fixed €2.4 million transfer budget and a scarce pool of seconded engineers would be spent, how far the plant would bend toward German standards versus keep its own practices, and how she would prove to the COO that a plan — rather than a promise — would close the gap.

## The Protagonist

Demir, 44, had spent seventeen years at Marmara, rising from a supply-chain analyst role into plant management before the board named her Managing Director in 2021. She was not part of the founding family that still shaped the company's culture from the boardroom, but she had their confidence, and NordHaus had kept her in place through the acquisition on the understanding that she would deliver the integration the deal's logic required. Her position, and those of the directors who reported to her, were themselves named in the ultimatum: if the year produced no visible convergence, hers was the seat NordHaus's replacement plan filled first.

## The Decision

Demir's task unfolded in four stages across the year.

The first was diagnosis. Eighteen months of manuals and a shared intranet had produced almost no measurable change, and the team had to decide why: the decisive skills — balancing assembly lines, engineering supplier quality — might live in engineers' hands, moving only person to person; the manuals themselves might be poorly organized and simply need trimming and re-translation into Turkish; supervisors might not have studied the material closely enough and need enforced, ongoing study time; or local supervisors might be resisting German methods and need replacing with new hires. A second call located the barrier itself: in supervisors who had never worked inside NordHaus's systems, facing a gap in language and business practice; in seconded engineers who were unwilling to share what they actually knew; in a channel problem that a better intranet and e-learning platform could fix; or in no real barrier at all, just an existing playbook that needed pushing harder. A third call named which capability — line-balancing and supplier quality, after-sales analytics, or an even split across all the gaps — would anchor the year's plan, and a fourth set, as a percentage, how candidly the team would name the problem as one of moving know-how between people rather than moving paper.

The second stage was resourcing. Demir split a €2.4 million transfer budget across four mechanisms: sending a chosen number of high-potential staff to NordHaus's Stuttgart operations for six to twelve months to learn by working there directly; running joint problem-solving teams that put Marmara and NordHaus staff on the line together; producing manuals, standard procedures and e-learning modules; and building a shared digital platform modelled on the Stuttgart plant. She also committed a number of engineer-days, from a pool of 600, to the people-based share of that mix — a plan assuming engineer time that did not exist would not be funded — and chose how many high-potential staff, from none up to six, to pull off the line and send to Stuttgart, each option trading current output against the size of the channel it built.

The third stage was alignment. NordHaus wanted its standards adopted wholesale; the founding family wanted most things kept local. Demir drew that line — fixing safety and quality gates while letting products and suppliers flex to Turkish and MENA markets, adopting every Stuttgart standard wholesale regardless of local fit or cost, or leaving the family to keep almost everything at the plant as it was — set a budget of up to €0.8 million for building supervisors' ability to take in and apply the new methods and for a regular bilingual routine between the two sites, answered a rival's counter-offer to her strongest seconded engineer with a real role and recognition, a pay match with no change to her scope, or letting her go and relying on the other three, and set, as a percentage, how firmly a role would wait for staff returning from Stuttgart.

The fourth stage was the pitch. Demir committed the COO to quarterly targets on defect rate and productivity tied to each mechanism, a single year-end target reported only once the year was over, or no hard targets at all, asking HQ to trust the local team; presented the logic behind it as the full chain from mechanism to absorbed skill to operating metric to profit, the profit number alone, or a list of what had been funded; stated whether the plan was staffed without slowing current output, assumed output would hold regardless, or accepted a production dip to move faster; and set, as a percentage, how firmly she would defend the view that an aligned transfer effort, not sheer volume of activity, would close the gap.

## The Company and its Setting

Marmara Beyaz Eşya A.Ş. made dishwashers and small kitchen appliances at a single plant of 1,400 employees in the Gebze industrial corridor outside Istanbul. Eighteen months earlier, NordHaus Group, a German appliance maker, had bought 80% of the company's equity, drawn by Marmara's low manufacturing costs and its access to Middle East and North Africa export markets. The deal assumed Marmara would absorb NordHaus's production, quality and product-development methods and close the performance gap within twenty-four months. Twelve of those months were already gone, and the integration effort so far had consisted of manuals and a shared intranet.

## The Situation

The scorecard the COO presented was blunt (Exhibit 1). Marmara's plant ran at 14,000 defective parts per million against NordHaus's group standard of 3,500, a gap carrying an estimated ₺290 million a year in warranty costs. Manufacturing productivity ran 35% below the group benchmark, and bringing a new appliance model to market took 22 months at Marmara against 14 months at group plants. Marmara's revenue of ₺11.8 billion a year, roughly €330 million, converted to an EBITDA margin of 9%, four points below NordHaus's group standard of 13%. Three tracking scores stood at their starting readings: a transfer-effectiveness score, capturing how much of NordHaus's know-how had moved into local hands, read 12 out of 100; a strategic-alignment score, capturing how well HQ's intent and the plant's own priorities pulled in the same direction, read 20 out of 100; and a subsidiary-performance score, tracking overall convergence toward group benchmarks, read 15 out of 100. Four NordHaus engineers seconded to Gebze made up the entire people-based channel for moving know-how into the plant, and a fourth score simply counted how many of the four remained in place. If nothing changed from the pattern of the previous eighteen months, the defect gap, the productivity shortfall and the slow product cycle would persist into the next scorecard, and NordHaus would treat that as confirmation that local management could not deliver the convergence the deal required.

## What Demir Controlled

Across the year, Demir controlled how the €2.4 million transfer budget and the 600-day engineer pool were split among the four mechanisms (Exhibit 2), how many staff moved to Stuttgart, where the standardise-or-adapt line was drawn, how the at-risk engineer's situation was handled, how firmly a returning role was guaranteed, and what she ultimately told the COO about targets and staffing. Her team would be judged on whether the transfer-effectiveness, alignment, subsidiary-performance and engineer-retention scores moved together over the year, and on whether the plan she presented could be funded within €2.4 million and staffed without stopping the line.

## Closing

Demir closed her notebook as the COO's return flight to Stuttgart was announced over the plant's paging system. She had four working sessions, twelve months, and a leadership team whose jobs sat in the same paragraph as the ultimatum. The first session — deciding why eighteen months of manuals had changed almost nothing — began the following Monday.
