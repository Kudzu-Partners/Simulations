# Levent Learning Partners: From Brief to Blueprint

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1841 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1841 — *From Brief to Blueprint* · v1.0 · September 2026

---

On 3 March 2026, Sibel Aydemir, Head of Custom Programs at Levent Learning Partners (LLP) in Istanbul, opened a request for proposals from Anadolu Express, a fast-growing Turkish e-commerce and logistics group. The brief asked for a leadership-development programme for 300 newly promoted team leads, fixed at EUR 900,000 and due back as a full proposal in nine working days. Anadolu Express had already diagnosed its own problem: new managers, the RFP said, lacked delegation and time-management skills.

LLP's own diagnostic pack told a different story. Engagement-survey extracts, exit-interview themes, a 360-degree snapshot, and turnover and manager-span figures, gathered as part of LLP's pre-bid diagnostic work, pointed toward feedback avoidance, unclear role authority, and a punitive error culture inside Anadolu Express's frontline layer, not a shortage of calendar discipline. Two of the three firms bidding for the contract were known, on past form, to build proposals straight off the written brief; that approach alone carried something like a seventy percent chance of winning a price-sensitive contract like this one. Aydemir's team had nine working days to decide whether to join them or make a different case.

## The Protagonist

Aydemir, 41, had joined LLP as a needs-analysis consultant in 2013 and had led its custom-programs unit since 2021. Her reputation rested on one habit: reading a client's stated problem against the underlying data before designing anything, and being willing to tell a paying client its self-diagnosis was wrong. That habit had won LLP loyal, long-running clients; it had also, at least once, cost the firm a contract to a rival that simply built what the client asked for. The Anadolu Express RFP was the largest her team had chased that year, and it would test the same instinct at a bigger scale than before.

## The Decision

The choice facing Aydemir's four-person deal team, a needs-analysis lead, a learning architect, a pricing and commercial lead, and a client partner, was not primarily technical. It was whether to agree with a paying client or correct it. Reading the pack, the team could conclude the real gap was mostly the time-management story the client had already written, with a little of the deeper culture material folded in; that the real gaps were feedback avoidance, unclear role authority and a punitive culture around error; that the gap really was delegation and time management, exactly as the RFP had stated it; or that the newly promoted leads were not yet ready for the role. Whichever reading it settled on, the team then had to decide how to carry it into the client relationship: confirm the ask while also flagging the deeper findings alongside it, reframe the diagnosis and put it at the centre of the proposal, or confirm the brief outright and build the proposal directly from it. Underneath either choice sat a third: how much time to spend triangulating that reading across the surveys, the 360 data, the exit interviews and the turnover figures before committing it to paper.

## The Company and its Setting

LLP was the custom-programs arm of a large Istanbul executive-education institution, built on diagnosing a client's real training need rather than selling off-the-shelf courseware. The unit ran EUR 11 million a year in revenue at a 20% operating margin, about EUR 2.2 million, carried by fourteen designers and consultants and a network of 55 outside faculty and facilitators. Anadolu Express was a different scale of company: EUR 2.3 billion in annual revenue and 11,000 employees, growing fast enough that its frontline manager population was turning over ahead of the firm's ability to develop it.

## The Situation

The commercial terms were fixed and unforgiving (see Exhibit 1). The RFP was worth EUR 900,000 for 300 participants, due in nine working days, with three firms, including LLP, competing for it. A full-fidelity design, addressing every gap the pack surfaced, in depth, for all 300 leads, would run close to EUR 1.05 million once coaching and application projects were priced in, roughly seventeen percent over budget before a single session was delivered. LLP's own economics meant the eventual price also had to clear about EUR 720,000 to protect the unit's standard 20% margin, leaving a working band of roughly EUR 720,000 to EUR 900,000.

If Aydemir's team did nothing to distinguish its bid, and simply built to the brief as written, it would be doing exactly what two of its three competitors were already expected to do, in a field where that approach alone carried something like a seventy percent chance of winning outright. The nine-day clock did not pause for a deeper diagnostic pass.

## What Aydemir Controlled

The first round belonged to the diagnosis itself: which of the four readings of the pack the team adopted, what stance it took with the client as a result, and how hard it worked to triangulate that reading across the data before writing it down.

A second round turned the reading into a costed blueprint inside the EUR 900,000 ceiling. The team had to choose how much of the gap map to build now: touching every gap lightly with a single half-day workshop each, prioritizing the two highest-impact gaps and pushing the rest to a later phase, or building deep coaching and application work against every gap for all 300 leads. It had to choose a dominant method for the harder, behavioural gaps: a blend of hands-on practice sessions, short lecture segments and micro-learning modules; structured skills practice with observation and manager coaching plus on-the-job application projects; or a programme of expert-led lectures combined with structured, self-paced e-learning modules. It had to commit a specific euro figure, from a wide range, to that coaching and application depth. And it had to decide whether each module shipped with a defined behaviour-change measurement plan, an end-of-module satisfaction survey, or no measurement plan at all.

If the proposal advanced, a third round asked the team to position the finished pitch: confirming the ask up front and setting the deeper findings a long way into the document, leading with the client's business outcome and the evidence behind the diagnosis, or opening the pitch by telling the client its brief had the wrong diagnosis. It had to set a price inside the working band, and decide how forcefully, in writing, to argue that a corrected diagnosis would raise rather than lower its own odds of winning. And because a contract this size almost always carried more than one internal voice with a view on the account, voices that did not always want the same thing, the team had to decide how to handle whichever stakeholders disagreed once the proposal landed: back the side favouring the deeper diagnosis and work around the other; try to satisfy both, folding a visible piece of the literal ask into the reframed design; or give the more literal-minded stakeholder exactly the programme it wanted and step back from the reframe.

And if LLP won, a further round awaited once a first cohort was already running: how to respond if a stakeholder later asked for the literal programme after all, holding the line that the diagnosis was settled, visibly folding in a piece of the original ask while re-anchoring on the outcome the budget was justified against, or simply swapping in the literal programme to keep that stakeholder satisfied; how to bring along any line manager who did not see the deeper work as part of their own job, mandating attendance, reframing the work as the manager's own retention and performance problem with practice built into the day job, or making the harder modules optional; what evidence to commit to showing the client months later, a mix of reaction scores and a completion rate, harder measures of behaviour change, retention and promotion-readiness, or a straightforward satisfaction score; and, on a sliding scale, how much of the original design to protect from being diluted to keep a stakeholder comfortable.

## Closing

At her desk on the morning of 3 March, Aydemir had the RFP, the diagnostic pack, and nine working days on the clock. Two of her three competitors would read the brief exactly as written. Her own dashboard already carried a needs-fit score, a win-probability estimate, an account-health measure and a budget line, all waiting to move the moment her team committed its first reading of the data (see Exhibit 2), and no decision made yet.
