# Pennine Connect: The Owner's Case

**Case Code:** OPS-2026-1914 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1914 — *Owning the Megaproject* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 15 June 2026, Rhian Powell read the funding-review letter twice and then turned to the single sheet her finance director had clipped behind it. The letter, jointly signed by HM Treasury and the scheme's sponsor, suspended the second-stage funding decision for Pennine Connect — the 38-kilometre rail link and two new stations that Powell's organisation had been created to deliver between Manchester and Leeds. The clipped sheet carried one number. The scheme's benefit-cost ratio, the appraised value of its benefits divided by its whole-life cost, stood at 1.30. The sponsor required 1.80 to release full funding.

The letter also set a date. On 13 October 2026, 120 days later, Powell would take a re-shaped business case, a delivery model and an investment plan to the sponsor board. If what she brought was not credible, funding would be paused and the scheme cut back.

## The Protagonist

Rhian Powell, 51, had been Senior Responsible Owner of Pennine Connect Ltd since the company was incorporated in 2024. She had spent fourteen years at a national rail infrastructure manager, four of them letting renewals contracts, then three years as client director on a motorway widening, then two in a combined authority's transport directorate. She had never built anything herself. Her working life had been spent buying work from people who did, and answering for it afterwards. The single-contract procurement strategy now under attack had been drafted by the pre-formation development team; Powell had endorsed it at her first board in March 2024.

## The Decision

Powell had to decide what Pennine Connect would put in front of the sponsor board on 13 October: what the railway was for, how the work would be bought, how the £4.8 billion (pounds sterling) would be released, and who would hold the right to change any of it once construction began.

## The Company and its Setting

Pennine Connect Ltd was formed in 2024, headquartered in Manchester, and publicly sponsored by the Department for Transport and two combined authorities. It employed 140 people and had a sanctioned envelope of £4.8 billion and a target opening in 2034. It did not build. Its staff let contracts, governed them, and reported to the sponsor.

The comparator set was not encouraging. UK rail schemes of this size had run 40% to 60% over their sanction estimates. The previous regional scheme in the sponsor's own portfolio had overrun by 60% and opened four years late; its owner had let a single lump-sum contract at sanction and held the builder to it.

## The Situation

The sponsor tracked Pennine Connect on four measures (see **Exhibit 1**). The benefit-cost ratio stood at 1.30 against the 1.80 required; below 1.50 the sponsor's rules removed the second station, a £620 million item whose catchment carried roughly 40% of the scheme's forecast benefits. Worst-case cost exposure — the overrun the public owner would carry if the scheme ran 50% over — stood at £2,400 million. Schedule float to the 2034 opening was 18 months, with a city-centre tunnel and a live-rail junction on the critical path. Stakeholder value, an index of sponsor and supply-chain confidence maintained by the sponsor's assurance team, stood at 20 out of 100. Of the £4,800 million envelope, none had yet been authorised.

Powell's own team did not agree on what had gone wrong. Her cost lead read it as an estimating problem: the rate build-up was conservative and could be re-cut. Her commercial lead read it as bidder optimism and wanted a harder-nosed contractor on the bidder list. Her stakeholder director read it as political: two of the four appraisal assumptions had been fixed in a spending review, and she wanted the 120 days spent in Whitehall. Her strategy lead read it as a drafting failure — the case described 38 kilometres of railway, and the reviewer had asked what the railway was for.

The same disagreement ran through a second question. Some of the board took Powell's job to be securing the money and releasing it on programme. Others took it to be buying a finished railway under one contract and holding the supplier to the price. A third view was that the client team should manage the works directly rather than through intermediaries. A fourth held that the scheme was an investment Pennine Connect was making on the public's behalf, to be shaped, structured and released in stages.

If none of this changed, the arithmetic was not in dispute. The review stayed frozen; the sponsor board paused funding on 13 October; at 1.30 the case sat below the 1.50 line, so the second station came out with the catchment behind roughly 40% of the benefits; and the £2,400 million of overrun exposure stayed where it was.

## What Powell Controlled

Within the 120 days, Powell set the terms of the re-submission. She chose how the scheme's purpose was written — as the engineering scope, 38 kilometres of track and two stations, set out in fuller detail; as that same scope re-costed, 4% trimmed from the estimate and nothing taken out of the railway; or as a set of regional outcomes, meaning jobs, labour-market access, freight off the M62 and development around the stations. She chose whether the second station was kept and integrated with station-area development, deferred to a later phase, or removed now; how much of the envelope (£0–120 million) funded benefits realisation and a named owner for it after handover; and how far the rebuilt benefit case rested on survey and evidence rather than assertion.

She chose how the work was packaged (see **Exhibit 2**): one lump-sum design-and-build covering the whole £4.8 billion; separate packages let to different contractors; an integrated alliance carrying one shared outcome across owner and suppliers; or a target-cost contract sharing overrun and underrun against an agreed number. She chose where the tunnel and junction risk sat — transferred whole to a single contractor, retained by Pennine Connect on a reimbursable basis, or shared with the specialist contractor in a surveyed and managed pool — how tightly supplier reward was tied to her cost and schedule targets, and how much risk premium, from £0 to £1,100 million, she was prepared to pay for risk she moved off her own balance sheet.

She chose how the £4,800 million was released: in full at sanction, in two blocks, or in tranches against gates. She set the amount authorised at this gate, anywhere from £200 million to the full envelope. She set what unlocked the next tranche — a fixed date in the delivery programme, on the argument that a scheme running to 2034 lost more to delay than to surprises in the ground; no written test, on the argument that a scheme of this political weight would be released when ministers were ready; or named evidence, meaning tunnel ground investigation results and firm prices, on the argument that the money should wait on the boreholes, with a right to descope retained. And she set how firmly that right to stop or reprice was written down.

Finally, she chose where decision rights sat once delivery started: with a monthly review committee giving advice; with whoever was in the room when something went wrong; or with a sponsor board holding written stop, reprice and descope rights at each gate. The risk register's central adverse scenario had the city-centre tunnel landing £300 million above the current allowance, and Powell had to say in the submission how such a movement would be handled — by reopening the contract and negotiating, by funding it from the public balance sheet, or by repricing it at a gate under the contract's risk-sharing terms. She also had to decide what the board pack led with: the cost variance line alone, which was what the sponsor's reporting template asked for; the strongest coherent version of the case, with the exposure detail held for questions; or all four measures, including the ones that showed the scheme at its weakest.

## Closing

At 6.10 p.m. on 15 June, Powell wrote the four measures on the whiteboard in the order the sponsor reported them, and under them the date: 13 October. She had 120 days, 140 people, and a business case that a reviewer in London had already read once.

|---|---|---|---|---|
