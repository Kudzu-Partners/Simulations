# Aldermoor Executive Education: The Pennine Flagship

**Case Code:** MKT-2026-1941 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1941 — *Impact by Design* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

On the morning of Monday, 20 April 2026, Naomi Fairhurst read the Pennine Energy Networks request for proposal twice and marked the same sentence both times. Pennine would judge the winning bid on two numbers taken eighteen months after the first cohort began: the elapsed time its transformation projects took from approval to delivery, and how fast decisions crossed the boundary between its engineering, commercial and regulatory functions. The document mentioned participant feedback once, to say that it would not be used.

Fairhurst had six weeks. Aldermoor Business School Executive Education had to submit a priced proposal by June 2026 for a custom programme covering 240 of Pennine's senior managers in two cohorts, inside a fixed budget of GBP 1.6 million — about GBP 6,667 a head. Award was expected in July and the first cohort in September. Two other business schools and one global consultancy were bidding the same brief.

## The Protagonist

Naomi Fairhurst, 49, had been Director of Executive Education at Aldermoor for four years, after eleven years designing custom programmes for a corporate academy in the utilities sector. She had been appointed on a mandate to move the division's custom work from well-reviewed to demonstrably useful, and she had told the school's dean, in writing, that renewals and ranking position would follow evidence rather than enthusiasm. Nothing in her four years had yet tested that claim at the scale of the Pennine contract. Her division's budget for the following year, and the case for the two design posts she had asked for, both sat on the dean's desk unanswered.

## The Decision

Fairhurst had to settle four things before the pitch, in sequence, each one narrowing the next: what the programme was actually for, how it would be built and how its effect would be measured, how it would be delivered and staffed, and what Aldermoor would charge and commit to. Each stage was to be recorded, so that the design team could see, stage by stage, what its choices had done to the strength of the design, to the team's own estimate of its odds, and to the margin.

## The Company and its Setting

Aldermoor Exec Ed was the open- and custom-programme unit of a research-intensive business school in the English Midlands. It turned over GBP 18 million a year — 55% from bespoke client programmes, 45% from open-enrolment courses — with a core team of 34 staff and a faculty pool drawn from the school and from outside practitioners. At the time of the RFP it was running 45 client programmes. The division's target contribution margin was 34%; that margin funded the faculty pool and cross-subsidised the open portfolio, which was why the finance office treated it as a floor rather than an aspiration (see **Exhibit 1**).

Pennine Energy Networks plc was a GBP 4 billion regulated utility. The regulator had ordered a change to its operating model, to be delivered over eighteen months, and the 240 managers in the RFP were the people who would have to deliver it. Pennine's sponsor and its procurement lead had both said, in the bidders' briefing, that they would sit in the same room at the pitch.

## The Situation

The folder on Fairhurst's desk already contained a draft. It stated the aim as stronger transformation-leadership capability, proposed a set of well-tested leadership modules in a sensible order, delivered residentially by Aldermoor faculty, with a baseline and a follow-up survey at eighteen months costed at GBP 70,000, absorbed by the existing team, priced at GBP 1.45 million and defended on the school's brand and rankings. It could be submitted as it stood.

The division's bid tracker for the Pennine bid opened as follows. The strength of the design, on an index the division used to score how far a proposal was tied to a client's own measures, stood at 12. The team's estimate of its odds against three rivals stood at 20%. Contribution margin stood at the 34% target, and committed delivery cost stood at GBP 0 of the GBP 1,600,000 envelope, because nothing had been committed yet. Every one of those four numbers would move as the design was fixed, and the tracker would show the movement after each stage (see **Exhibit 1**).

Three arguments were running inside the team, and none of them had been settled. Idris Bello, the programme design lead, wanted every module tied to a named Pennine measure and a comparison group built into the evaluation from the start, and accepted that this cost money the design budget would otherwise spend on content. Catherine Vane, the faculty and delivery director, pointed out that a 34-person team already running 45 programmes could not deliver a high-contact model without either recruiting or dropping work it had already sold. Simon Ackroyd, the commercial manager, kept returning to the arithmetic: whatever was chosen had to be bought, staffed and measured inside GBP 1.6 million while leaving the margin intact, and the consultancy across the table carried a different cost structure and knew it.

## What Fairhurst Controlled

At the first stage she had to fix the purpose. Four statements were on the whiteboard: that the programme existed to shorten project cycle time and speed cross-functional decisions during the transformation; that it existed to build stronger transformation-leadership capability; that it existed to give 240 managers an inspiring and highly rated programme; or that it existed to sell Aldermoor's existing leadership curriculum. With it she had to fix what would count as proof at month 18 — a measured change in Pennine's two business numbers against a baseline, an observed change in how managers ran the work, module assessment scores, or participant ratings — and how firmly the team would hold to whichever standard it had just set, recorded as a percentage.

At the second stage she chose how the programme was built. It could be designed backward from the outcome with every module mapped to a Pennine measure, assembled from best-practice modules and well sequenced, staged as a signature residential event with marquee speakers, or adapted from the standard open-enrolment curriculum. She also chose what evaluation was built in: a baseline with a comparison group and eighteen-month follow-through at GBP 130,000; a baseline with a follow-up survey at GBP 70,000; an end-of-programme survey and testimonial film at GBP 15,000; or nothing formal. How tightly each module was wired to a specific Pennine measure was hers to set, as a percentage.

At the third stage she chose the delivery model from the four in **Exhibit 2**, and how to staff it. She could recruit two programme managers and contract external practitioner-faculty while trimming low-margin open dates, at GBP 110,000; free internal faculty by deprioritising two lower-value custom clients, at GBP 45,000; absorb the work into the existing team at no cost; or settle staffing after the design was finished. She set the share of external practitioner and coaching capacity as a percentage of the teaching effort.

At the last stage she set the fee, anywhere between GBP 800,000 and the GBP 1.6 million ceiling. She set the scorecard Aldermoor would sign: cycle time and decision speed with a baseline and an agreed attribution method; the same two measures with neither; stated improvements in leadership capability and collaboration; or satisfaction and recommendation targets. And she chose how to answer procurement in the room — by showing the margin arithmetic and the evaluation design, by leaning on brand and rankings, by cutting to GBP 1.25 million to undercut the consultancy, or by refusing to move. How hard she pressed that argument was hers to set as well, again as a percentage.

## Closing

At 8:40 a.m. Fairhurst wrote the two Pennine measures at the top of a clean sheet and ruled four boxes beneath them. The pitch was six weeks out, the award in July, the first cohort in September, and the draft in the folder was already complete.

**Exhibit 2. The four delivery models on the table, as Aldermoor's design team described them**

| Model | How the teaching would be delivered | Argument made for it | Objection raised against it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faculty-led residential | Cohorts off site, taught by Aldermoor faculty in blocks | Highest prestige; strongest day-rate margin; the format Pennine's board would recognise | Managers return on Monday to jobs that have not changed |
| Blended with coaching | Digital core, applied projects between modules, one-to-one coaching | Puts the work into the participants' own diaries; lighter cost base than residential | Depends on coaches Aldermoor does not employ; less visible to the client |
| Embedded action-learning | Cohorts work on live Pennine transformation projects, faculty attached to each | Learning happens inside the change Pennine is being judged on; most differentiated bid | Heaviest faculty load of the four; hardest to schedule against 45 running programmes |
| Hybrid | Blended core with an action-learning capstone on one live Pennine project | Keeps the blended cost base and still ends inside real work | Two delivery machines to run at once; the capstone still needs faculty who are already committed |

*Source: Aldermoor Exec Ed design-team working notes, April 2026 (disguised). Arguments and objections are as recorded in the notes and were not resolved before the pitch.*
