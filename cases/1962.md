# Lowlands Logistics Group: The First Hundred Days

**Case Code:** ORG-2026-1962 · **Companion simulation:** Express 1962 — *Merging Identities* · **v1.0 · August 2026**

---

At 7:40 on Wednesday, April 1, 2026 — the day the merger closed — Marieke Vos opened the day-one pulse survey at her desk in Rotterdam. Of the 3,000 people who now worked for Lowlands Logistics Group, 34% described themselves as Lowlands people. Eighty-one percent still described themselves as Stadhaven people or Nimbus people. On the survey's ten-point fairness scale, the combined workforce rated the merger 5.2.

An email from the chief executive had arrived twenty minutes earlier. He wanted the founding message — the account the group would give of what it now was — by the end of week two. Vos had that fortnight to decide, and one hundred days before the board expected a reckoning of what the integration had bought and what it had cost.

## The Protagonist

Marieke Vos, 44, joined Stadhaven B.V. in 2011 as a network operations manager and had run its Rotterdam programme office since 2019, delivering the 2023 depot automation rollout four months late and inside budget. The combined board appointed her Director of the Integration Office on March 12, three weeks before close, on a mandate that expired on day 100, when it would decide whether the office continued. Her appointment read differently on each side of the building. Stadhaven managers saw one of their own at the centre of the merger; Nimbus managers saw a fifteen-year incumbent given control of the process that would decide whose practices survived.

## The Decision

Three versions of the founding message sat on Vos's desk, each with an advocate on the executive team. Joost Rietveld, the group chief executive and formerly Nimbus's, wanted a single new company announced at once: one name, one mark, one set of working practices in force by the end of week two, with both legacy identities formally retired. Hendrik Bloem, Stadhaven's managing director and now the group's chief operating officer, wanted both legacy identities left intact — two brands, two organisation charts, two toolchains — with any blending deferred until the businesses had settled. The group communications director wanted a third: a shared Lowlands identity built from the start, with named elements of each legacy carried forward into it and stated in advance as commitments rather than concessions.

Whichever account she chose, Vos also had to set the speed of the blend, on a scale running from changing nothing this phase to changing everything at once.

## The Company and its Setting

Lowlands Logistics Group was a EUR 750 million Dutch logistics technology company created on April 1, 2026 from two firms of comparable size and opposite temperament (see **Exhibit 1**). Stadhaven B.V., founded in Rotterdam in 1998, employed 1,800 people on EUR 410 million of 2025 revenue and described itself as engineering-led; its phrase was that it never missed a shipment. Nimbus N.V., founded in Amsterdam in 2016, employed 1,200 people on EUR 340 million of revenue and described itself as the cloud-native firm that had bought a legacy player. Nimbus was the acquirer.

The combined engineering organisation of 1,150 people was to build a single freight platform from the two existing ones, and the EUR 22 million of synergies underwriting the price came almost entirely from it.

## The Situation

The day-one data pack (see **Exhibit 2**) gave Vos six readings. Identification with Lowlands stood at 34% and with a legacy firm at 81%; the two overlapped, because people could hold both. Perceived fairness — ratings of what was being decided, how, and how it was explained — stood at 5.2 out of 10. Integration progress against the platform roadmap stood at zero, and none of the engineers flagged as being at risk of leaving had yet been secured. The office also kept an identity-threat index, a composite of survey and interview signals on which lower was safer. It read High.

People analytics had flagged roughly 250 of the 1,150 engineers — 22% — as at elevated risk of leaving, concentrated in Stadhaven's oldest platform teams and Nimbus's cloud group. Losing the 50 most critical would, on the office's estimate, slip the integrated platform by six to nine months and put the EUR 22 million at risk.

Three decisions were queued and could not be held past week ten. The first was which engineering standards and toolchain the merged organisation would run on. The second was how to harmonise titles and the pay gap between the two leveling systems, about 8% in Nimbus's favour at senior grades. The third was who would lead the merged platform organisation, where the candidates were a Nimbus engineering director and a Stadhaven architect of fifteen years' standing. Alongside them sat three smaller questions Vos's staff kept raising and her executive colleagues kept dismissing: the sign on the Rotterdam headquarters, the two product brand names, and a planned office consolidation.

The arithmetic of doing nothing was not dramatic. Left alone, 81% of the workforce would go on identifying with a company that no longer existed. The fairness rating would stay near 5.2, none of the 250 would be secured, and the roadmap would stay at zero while the two engineering organisations kept building the systems they already had.

## What Vos Controlled

Four readings of the strain circulated in her own office, and she had to commit to one. It sat with Stadhaven alone, as the firm being bought; with Nimbus, whose people feared being slowed by a 28-year-old incumbent; with both, in different forms; or it was overstated in a merger both boards had agreed to. In the same week she had to place the weight of the coming decisions: in the operational ones, in the symbolic and people ones, or in neither more than the other. She had to set the office's opening posture — declare Lowlands the operating standard, hold every identity question until the businesses settled, or signal that both legacies would carry forward and consult before any contested call. And she had to fix how much consultation the hundred days would carry, as a percentage of decisions taken with structured employee input.

At week three she set the founding message and the pace. The sign, the brand names and the office consolidation could be handled three ways: retire the old marks at once; leave them alone and spend the time on operations; or take each separately and decide it on purpose.

At week six the queued decisions came due, each with three answers. The toolchain: adopt the Nimbus stack, adopt the Stadhaven stack, or select component by component with both engineering groups in the room. Titles and pay: leave the 8% gap, bring the Nimbus senior bands down to Stadhaven's, or move both onto common bands phased across the year and funded from the integration budget. The platform lead: the Nimbus director, the Stadhaven architect, or whichever candidate a joint panel selected against published criteria. She also had to choose how all three were made and told — outcomes published on the intranet, outcomes announced without the reasoning, or each decision consulted with those affected and explained face to face by the leader who made it. And she had to set a retention budget for the flagged engineers at EUR 0, EUR 3 million for the top names only, or EUR 6 million across the at-risk population.

On day 100 she would face the combined board and a town hall of employees. She had to choose what to lead with: the roadmap delivered, the calm kept, or the four measures together. She had to state the principle she had run the integration on. And she had to answer the board's challenge by defending her strongest number, by attributing any shortfall to the legacy cultures, or by naming the trade-off she had made and what it bought and cost. The board would also ask her to put a number on how far she backed fair process and recognisable continuity, rather than speed, as the thing that protected the deal's value.

## Closing

At 8:05 Vos closed the survey and wrote the two numbers on the whiteboard, 34 and 81, with the fairness rating under them. The founding message was due to Rietveld on the Friday of week two. The queued decisions were due by week ten. On day 100 the board wanted four numbers and one paragraph explaining them.
